<p>This guide is the ordered sequence a UK doctor follows to move private practice work into a limited company, from the decision to the first corporation tax payment. One point governs everything below and it is the point most generalist guides get wrong for doctors: <strong>you incorporate your private work, never your NHS work</strong>. The NHS side of your income stays exactly where it is.</p>

<p>This guide assumes you have decided. If you have not, <a href="/blog/gp-limited-company-tax-benefits-drawbacks">GP limited company tax benefits and drawbacks</a> sets the two structures side by side, and the <a href="/calculators/private-practice-incorporation">private practice incorporation calculator</a> runs your own figures. Be honest with yourself about the size of the prize first: at 2026/27 rates the pure tax saving on private work is modest at typical profit levels, and the dividend-rate rise that took effect on 6 April 2026 has already narrowed it. The durable reasons to incorporate are structural. The arithmetic on its own will not carry the decision, and that comparison page sets both sides out. This guide is general information, not personal advice.</p>

<h2>What can and cannot move into the company</h2>

<p>An NHS GMS contract can be held by a company, but only by a company limited by shares where at least one share is legally and beneficially owned by a medical practitioner and every other shareholder is a qualifying person (NHS Act 2006 s.86(1)(c) and s.86(3)). <strong>An ordinary personal service company does not meet those conditions</strong>, and neither does any company with an outside shareholder. PMS agreements are made under a separate provision (s.92) with the detail in regulations, so the safe way to hold the point is that the contract sits with GPs, their partnerships, or a tightly restricted company whose shareholders all qualify.</p>

<p>The pension limb is the one that decides the question in practice, and it does not depend on any of that: <strong>income routed through a company is not NHS-pensionable</strong>. So what you can move is your private and non-NHS work, which for most doctors means private consultations and self-pay clinics, insurance medicals, medico-legal and expert-witness work, occupational health, cosmetic and aesthetic clinics, and private locum work falling outside IR35. Our overview of <a href="/blog/private-practice-tax-nhs-and-private-income">private practice tax and NHS and private income</a> explains how the two sides sit alongside each other.</p>

<p>Your regulatory position is personal to you and the company does not change it. Your <strong>GMC</strong> registration and licence to practise are held by you under the Medical Act 1983 and the company cannot hold them. A GP's entry on the NHS England national <strong>Performers List</strong> (National Health Service (Performers Lists) (England) Regulations 2013, SI 2013/335) is a prerequisite for performing NHS primary medical services, which the company is not doing. Neither transfers, and neither needs to.</p>

<p>A word on the vocabulary, because search results confuse it. An <strong>incorporated company</strong> and a limited company are the same thing in ordinary use: incorporation is the act of bringing the company into existence and "limited" describes the liability of its members.</p>

<h3>The NHS pension accrual you give up, and how to price it</h3>

<p>This is the single most important number in the decision, and it is the one that most often never makes it into the model. Private or locum income taken from the company as dividends is <strong>not NHS-pensionable</strong>, so any of that income which would otherwise have been pensioned stops earning NHS accrual entirely. Whether that costs you anything turns on your role, because the certification route differs.</p>

<table>
<thead>
<tr><th>Your role</th><th>How the income is pensioned today</th><th>The deadline that binds it</th></tr>
</thead>
<tbody>
<tr><td>GP partner or non-GP partner (Type 1)</td><td>Annual Certificate of Pensionable Profits, via PCSE</td><td>28 February, a year in arrears</td></tr>
<tr><td>Salaried or solo GP (Type 2)</td><td>Type 2 self-assessment, via PCSE</td><td>28 February, a year in arrears</td></tr>
<tr><td>Freelance GP locum</td><td>Locum forms A and B, via PCSE</td><td>10 weeks from the end of the work, after which it cannot be pensioned at all</td></tr>
<tr><td>Hospital consultant</td><td>Only the NHS employment, through NHS payroll</td><td>No end-of-year form, because private work was never pensionable</td></tr>
</tbody>
</table>

<p>The routes themselves are set out in full elsewhere: <a href="/blog/nhs-pension-for-locums-form-a-form-b">NHS pension for locums, forms A and B</a> covers the locum clock, and <a href="/blog/gp-partner-vs-salaried-gp-tax-comparison">partner versus salaried GP tax</a> sets the two GP positions side by side.</p>

<p><strong>None of these routes can take company income.</strong> The table splits doctors into two very different positions. If you are a consultant, your private work has never been pensionable and incorporating it gives up no accrual, so the pension line in your model is zero. If you are a GP with solo, locum or private income currently running through one of the first three routes, incorporating that income has a price, and it is calculable.</p>

<p>Accrual in the 2015 section is <strong>1/54th</strong> of each year's pensionable earnings, revalued while you remain an active member at <strong>CPI plus 1.5%</strong>. So every £1,000 of otherwise-pensionable income you route through the company instead gives up £1,000 divided by 54, which is <strong>£18.52 a year of pension</strong>, payable for life and index-linked. Scale that to a realistic figure: <strong>£30,000</strong> of solo income taken as dividends instead of being pensioned gives up £30,000 divided by 54, or <strong>£555.56 a year</strong>, every year of retirement, for each year you do it.</p>

<p>For some doctors giving that accrual up is the whole objective of the exercise. A high earner already at or over the <strong>£60,000</strong> annual allowance (2026/27, unchanged from 2025/26), or caught by the taper, may want private income held outside pensionable pay deliberately. The taper thresholds, the pension input amount and carry forward are set out on the <a href="/calculators/nhs-pension-annual-allowance">NHS pension annual allowance calculator</a>. <strong>Never look at the tax saving in isolation; always pair it with the pension-accrual loss.</strong></p>

<h2>The incorporation process, step by step</h2>

<p>The mechanics of a change from sole trader to a limited company are the same for a doctor as for anyone else. What differs is the order, because three of the steps below are medical and have to be settled before the company sees a patient. Allow several weeks end to end, with most of that time going on the transfer and the registrations, and very little of it on the company formation itself.</p>

<ol>
<li><strong>Plan the structure and the dates.</strong> Decide who the directors and shareholders are, the share classes, and whether a spouse or family member will hold shares (which needs genuine commercial substance behind it). Choose the transfer date and the company year-end together, in one sitting, because the transfer date fixes the capital gains position and the year-end fixes every filing date that follows.</li>
<li><strong>Form the company at Companies House.</strong> Identity verification is a legal requirement for every director and every person with significant control, and it produces a Companies House personal code you will need in order to file, so start it first. Then choose a compliant name, and register online for <strong>£100</strong> with the registered office, the director details, the persons of significant control, the share capital and the articles. Online applications are usually registered <strong>within 24 hours</strong>; the paper route (form IN01) costs £124 and takes 8 to 10 days. Note the medical trap in the name rules: <strong>"NHS" is a sensitive expression</strong>, and using it needs a letter of non-objection from the Department of Health and Social Care, as do "health centre" and "health service". Keep the certificate of incorporation safe, because you will need it repeatedly.</li>
<li><strong>Transfer the private business to the company.</strong> The private-practice trade, its equipment and any transferable private goodwill move to the company as a going concern under a written agreement. Where there is chargeable private goodwill, <strong>s.162 incorporation relief</strong> (TCGA 1992 s.162, amended by FA 2026 s.39) can defer the gain, but for a transfer of a business on or after <strong>6 April 2026</strong> the relief is no longer automatic and <strong>the relief must be claimed</strong>. The conditions, the claim deadline and the treatment of any non-share consideration are set out in our guide to <a href="/blog/incorporation-relief-private-medical-practice-s162">section 162 incorporation relief for a private medical practice</a>. Remember that a GP cannot transfer NHS goodwill at all (see the pitfalls below).</li>
<li><strong>Settle indemnity, regulation and the contracts.</strong> Ask your defence organisation, in writing, whether your existing <strong>professional indemnity</strong> arrangement covers work carried out by and billed through the company, and put corporate cover in place if it does not. Review the contracts that actually matter to a consultant: private hospital <strong>practising privileges</strong>, room-hire agreements, premises leases, equipment finance and service contracts. Some can be assigned; others have to be formally novated. Practising privileges are the clearest example, because they are granted to you as a named practitioner, so ask each private hospital whether it will <strong>novate</strong> the agreement to the company or would prefer to keep contracting with you personally. Leases usually need landlord consent. If the company will be the provider of a CQC-regulated activity, ask the CQC what that means for your registration while there is still time to act on the answer.</li>
<li><strong>Open a company bank account.</strong> Open it in the company name using the certificate of incorporation, the articles and director identification. Keep company money strictly separate from personal money from the first day the company trades; mixing the two creates the director's loan problem described below.</li>
<li><strong>Tell HMRC the company is active.</strong> You are usually set up for corporation tax at the same time as you register at Companies House, but the obligation to tell HMRC the company is active <strong>within three months</strong> of the start of its tax accounting period is still yours, so check that it actually happened. That notification sets the company's filing and payment obligations, and the rates and marginal relief that apply to its profits are set out on <a href="/blog/gp-corporation-tax">GP corporation tax</a>.</li>
<li><strong>Register for VAT only if standard-rated work makes it necessary.</strong> Registration turns on standard-rated turnover only, which for a doctor means cosmetic, medico-legal or some occupational-health work and not genuine medical care, and our guide to <a href="/blog/gp-vat-registration">GP VAT registration</a> gives the thresholds and the test.</li>
<li><strong>Set up payroll and decide how you take the money out.</strong> If you take a salary, register as an employer and operate PAYE with Real Time Information reporting. How to split what you take between salary and dividends is a decision in its own right and is worked through on <a href="/blog/salary-vs-dividend-medical-limited-company-2026">salary and dividends in a medical limited company</a>. Then run the company accounting: bookkeeping, annual accounts and the corporation tax return.</li>
</ol>

<h2>A worked timeline, with the dates that fall out of one transfer date</h2>

<p>Take a hospital consultant who decides in September 2026, incorporates in October and starts trading through the company on <strong>1 November 2026</strong>, choosing a <strong>31 October</strong> year-end so the first accounting period is a full twelve months. Every other date below is derived from those two choices.</p>

<tr><td>November 2026</td><td>First RTI submission if a salary is taken; s.162 claim diarised with the accountant</td><td>Payroll runs from the first payment; the claim is a 2026/27 matter and the deadline mechanics sit on the section 162 page</td></tr>

<p>Look at the last two rows together, because that ordering is what small company directors get wrong. <strong>You pay the corporation tax before you file the return that works it out.</strong> On these dates the money leaves on 1 August 2028 and the return is not due until 31 October 2028, so the accounts have to be far enough advanced by the summer to produce a reliable number, which in practice means the bookkeeping cannot wait for the filing deadline.</p>

<h2>Common pitfalls to avoid</h2>

<h3>1. Pricing the tax and ignoring the pension</h3>

<p>The most expensive mistake is treating incorporation as a pure tax play and only later realising the income that used to be pensioned now earns no NHS accrual. The arithmetic above turns that worry into a number, so put the number in the model at the start. Decide it deliberately, with the accrual priced, before you incorporate.</p>

<h3>2. Assuming NHS goodwill can be sold</h3>

<p>The sale of NHS GP goodwill has been prohibited since <strong>1 April 2004</strong> (current instrument SI 2019/251, which also blocks selling company shares whose value includes that goodwill). So incorporating a GP's NHS work cannot monetise NHS goodwill, and the dentist-style "sell the goodwill and claim relief" playbook does not translate. Only transferable <strong>private-practice goodwill</strong> can change hands. If what you are contemplating is an outright sale of the private practice, and not a transfer into your own company, the capital gains position and Business Asset Disposal Relief are covered on <a href="/blog/selling-private-medical-practice-cgt-badr">selling a private medical practice</a>.</p>

<h3>3. Taking money the company has not got</h3>

<p>A medical company is a <strong>close company</strong>. If you draw more than the company can properly pay you as salary or dividend, you run an overdrawn director's loan and a tax charge follows on the balance left outstanding after the year-end, which is set out with its rate and timing on <a href="/blog/consultant-directors-loan-account-s455-medical-company">the consultant's director's loan account guide</a>. Plan your drawings so the loan does not run overdrawn at the year-end and the question never arises.</p>

<h3>4. Mixing money and missing the basics</h3>

<p>Keep company and personal money separate, document every dividend with a board minute and a dividend voucher, and only declare dividends out of distributable profits. If you provide services through the company to NHS trusts or large private hospitals, check the off-payroll position, because the hirer rather than you determines IR35 status for medium and large clients, and a caught engagement removes the extraction efficiency that made the company attractive. Our note on <a href="/blog/locum-doctor-ir35-what-you-need-to-know">locum doctor IR35</a> explains who decides.</p>

<h2>Ongoing obligations once incorporated</h2>

<p>An incorporated practice files annual accounts and a confirmation statement with Companies House (the confirmation statement carries a £50 fee with the first statement in each twelve-month payment period), a company tax return with HMRC, and, if you run payroll, RTI submissions. You will need corporate professional indemnity kept in force, proper bookkeeping records, and discipline about dividend paperwork. Each director also keeps their Companies House personal code, because it is needed when the confirmation statement is filed. These compliance costs are real and recurring, which is part of why incorporation only earns its keep above a certain level of sustained private profit.</p>

<h2>Getting it right for your circumstances</h2>

<p>The sequence above is the same for most doctors, but the numbers are personal. The right answer turns on your contract type, your annual-allowance position, how much you want to retain in the company, and your family and succession plans. If you are a GP partner carving private work out of a partnership, the interaction with the partnership's own accounts and capital arrangements needs looking at while the transfer date is still movable.</p>
