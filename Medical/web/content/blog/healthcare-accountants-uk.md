<p>Healthcare accountants work only with doctors, medical practices and other healthcare professionals. That means they already know how NHS contract income, the NHS Pension Scheme and mixed NHS and private earnings behave inside a set of accounts.</p>

<p>The specialism is real rather than a label, because several of the rules below apply to almost no other client a high street firm ever sees. What follows is the job itself, the questions that test whether a firm can do it, and where the detail sits for your own role.</p>

<h2>What does a healthcare accountant actually do?</h2>

<p>The core of the work is ordinary: annual accounts, self-assessment, payroll and bookkeeping. What sits on top of it is not. A medical practice earns under a national contract with its own payment machinery, its partners are taxed on figures they never see in a bank statement, and the pension runs on paperwork that no other profession files.</p>

<p>So the job divides into three parts. Preparing the accounts and returns for the practice or the individual. Getting the NHS pension paperwork right and on time, including the certificates that set how much you are recorded as having earned. Then modelling decisions before they are made, such as taking on private work, buying into a partnership, or putting income through a company.</p>

<p>Some members also have a remediable pension savings statement to reconcile after the <a href="/blog/mccloud-remedy-nhs-pension-doctors-explained">McCloud remedy</a>.</p>

<p>The <a href="/services">full service list</a> sets out the compliance side, and the guide to <a href="/blog/gp-accountant">what a specialist medical accountant does for a GP</a> covers the practice-level version in depth.</p>

<h2>Why the tax bill can be bigger than the money you took</h2>

<p>The last of those four is the one that causes the most trouble in practice, and it is worth seeing with figures on it. For 2026/27 the higher rate of income tax is 40% on income up to £125,140, and Class 4 National Insurance is 2% on profits above £50,270.</p>

<p>Take Dr A, a GP partner whose allocated profit share for 2026/27 is £96,000. Drawings over the same year came to £78,000, because the practice held cash back for a premises payment. Tax is charged on the £96,000, not on the £78,000.</p>

<p>The £18,000 difference sits above the higher-rate threshold, so it attracts income tax of £18,000 at 40%, which is £7,200, and Class 4 National Insurance of £18,000 at 2%, which is £360. That is roughly £7,560 of tax and National Insurance attaching to money that never reached the personal account.</p>

<p>What changes the answer is the size of the undrawn slice: if drawings are trued up to the profit share before the year end, the gap closes and the reserve needed falls with it. The <a href="/calculators/gp-partner-drawings-planner">partner drawings planner</a> shows the shape for your own figures.</p>

<h2>Which kind of doctor are you, and where should you read next?</h2>

<p>Most of what applies to you depends on your role rather than your specialty, and the routes below cover the majority of readers.</p>

<h3>GP partners and practices</h3>

<p>You are self-employed, taxed on your profit share, and the practice files a partnership return with your share flowing to your own. Partnership accounts, capital accounts and pension certification are the recurring work. Start with the guidance for <a href="/for-gps">GP practices and partners</a>.</p>

<h3>Salaried GPs</h3>

<p>You are an employee taxed at source, with a pension certificate to complete each year and, for many, a second strand of income on top. The interesting question is usually whether a move to partnership makes sense. The <a href="/calculators/salaried-gp-vs-partner">salaried GP versus partner comparison</a> models the difference.</p>

<h3>Locum doctors</h3>

<p>You are usually a sole trader, occasionally working through a company, and the pension paperwork runs on a much shorter clock than everyone else's. Off-payroll status is decided by the hirer for NHS work. The <a href="/for-locum-doctors">locum guidance</a> and the <a href="/blog/locum-tax">locum tax hub</a> cover both.</p>

<h3>Junior doctors and registrars</h3>

<p>Rotations, student loan thresholds and occasional bank or locum shifts make an otherwise simple PAYE position untidy. The first return is usually triggered by the extra shifts rather than by the training post. See <a href="/for-junior-doctors">guidance for doctors in training</a>.</p>

<h2>What about hospital consultants, nurses, vets and other healthcare professionals?</h2>

<p>A hospital consultant with private work has two income streams under two regimes, and only the NHS post is pensionable. That combination drives most of the planning, from how private income is structured to how it is invoiced. The <a href="/for-consultants">consultant guidance</a> and the <a href="/blog/private-practice">private practice hub</a> both start there.</p>

<p>Nurses, midwives and other clinical staff sit in the same pension scheme as doctors, and the questions that come up are mostly about employment status and professional subscriptions rather than practice accounts. Those have their own page on <a href="/blog/nurse-tax-relief-professional-subscriptions">tax relief and subscriptions for nurses</a>.</p>

<p>Veterinary practices are private businesses with no NHS contract at all, so almost none of the machinery above applies to them, and the accounting conversation is about practice ownership instead. That is covered separately for <a href="/blog/accountants-for-vets-veterinary-practice-tax">veterinary practices</a>. Opticians and similar independent healthcare businesses share some of the same ownership ground.</p>

<h2>Does searching for healthcare accountants near me still help?</h2>

<p>Less than it used to, and the reason is mechanical. Pension certification runs through an online portal, returns are filed digitally, practice ledgers already live in the cloud, and Making Tax Digital has pushed the last of the paper out. The documents that decide your position are not in a filing cabinet down the road.</p>

<p>What proximity genuinely buys is a face to sit across from at a partnership meeting, which is worth having if your practice wants it. What it does not buy is competence on the rules above. A medical accountant UK-wide who has read a hundred pension certificates is a better bet than a nearby generalist who has read none.</p>

<p>The test that actually matters is whether someone picks up the phone in January and February, not where their office is. Reading is also <a href="/locations">organised by location</a> if that is how you prefer to browse.</p>

<h2>Accountants for medical professionals, and when you actually need one</h2>

<p>Plenty of doctors need nothing at all, and saying otherwise would be selling. A hospital doctor or salaried GP with one NHS payslip, no private work and no property income generally has no return to file and no decision to model.</p>

<p>The position changes when a second thing appears. Two locum sessions a month, a first private invoice, an expert-witness instruction, a rental property, or an offer of partnership all create either a filing obligation or a choice worth modelling first. Accountants for medical professionals earn their place at that point rather than before it, and the useful ones will tell you when you have reached it.</p>

<p>One label to treat carefully: NHS accountants sometimes means finance staff inside an NHS organisation, which is a different profession from advising people the NHS pays. If you are reading this as a doctor, the second one is what you want.</p>

<h2>What has changed for 2026/27?</h2>

<p>Making Tax Digital for Income Tax now applies. Sole traders and landlords with qualifying income above <strong>£50,000</strong> came into it on <strong>6 April 2026</strong>, which catches most full-time locums and most unincorporated private practices. General partnerships are deferred with no confirmed date, and limited companies are outside it entirely, so a GP partnership is not yet mandated at partnership level even where its individual partners are.</p>

<p>Mileage also moved: the approved rate for the first 10,000 business miles rose to <strong>55p</strong> from 6 April 2026, with 25p after that, and any guidance still printing 45p is a year out of date. The <a href="/blog/gp-tax-deductions-complete-list-2026">deductions list</a> carries the detail.</p>
