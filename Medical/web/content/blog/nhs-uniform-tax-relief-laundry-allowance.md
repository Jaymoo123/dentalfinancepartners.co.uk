<p>NHS staff who launder their own uniform at their own expense can claim a flat rate deduction set by occupation: £125 a year for nurses, healthcare assistants and radiographers, and £185 for ambulance staff on active service. Where the employer reimburses the cost, or offers free laundering that the member of staff chooses not to use, there is no claim.</p>

<p>The deduction is not cash. A £125 flat rate reduces taxable income by £125, so it is worth £25 at basic rate and £50 at higher rate.</p>

<h2>Who can claim a uniform tax refund: UK health and care staff by occupation</h2>

<p>There is no single uniform rebate that every member of NHS staff gets, which is why the figures published for it disagree so widely. HMRC agrees a <strong>flat rate expense</strong>, an annual amount deducted without keeping receipts, with each occupational group, and the rate follows the job rather than the employer. The rates below apply to staff of the NHS, private hospitals, local authorities and independent care providers alike, so look for your own group.</p>

<p>Two further amounts sit on top of the £125, and the condition attached to them is regularly misreported. HMRC allows <strong>£12 a year for shoes</strong> where wearing a prescribed style is obligatory at the hospital or other workplace, and <strong>£6 a year for stockings, tights or socks</strong> where a prescribed style or colour is similarly obligatory. The test is the obligation, not who paid for the items. Those are additions rather than alternatives, so the maximum is £143.</p>

<p>They are also not restricted to registered nurses and midwives. EIM67200 says references to a nurse "include nurses and midwives of all grades and includes auxiliaries, students, dental nurses, nursing assistants and healthcare assistants or workers", so a healthcare assistant told to wear a set style of shoe can claim the £12 in the same way a staff nurse can.</p>

<p>One thing to know before comparing figures elsewhere: these amounts are not uprated each April. They have stood since 6 April 2014, so nothing is gained by waiting for a new tax year.</p>

<h2>What counts as a uniform, and does buying it qualify?</h2>

<p>The deduction sits in ITEPA 2003 s.367, which covers maintaining special clothing an employee is required to wear. Tax relief on work uniform cleaning covers laundering, repairing and replacing. It never covers the initial cost of buying the clothing, and it never covers everyday clothing worn for work, even where an employer specifies a colour or a style.</p>

<p>The relief for washing a uniform is fixed by occupation rather than by what you actually spend. That cuts both ways. You do not have to prove your costs, and you do not get more by proving them.</p>

<h2>How much is the NHS uniform tax rebate actually worth?</h2>

<p>Multiply the flat rate by your <strong>marginal rate</strong>, the percentage of tax paid on your next pound of income. At the 2026/27 basic rate of 20%, the £125 group saves £25 a year, the £185 ambulance rate saves £37 and the £80 group saves £16. At the higher rate of 40%, those become £50, £74 and £32.</p>

<p>Take S, an illustrative healthcare assistant on £27,000 in 2026/27 who washes her own tunics because her ward has no laundry service. Her flat rate is £125 and she has never claimed, so she claims for 2026/27 and the four earlier tax years. Each year the £125 deduction saves 20% of £125, which is £25.</p>

<p>The four closed years are repaid together: 4 × £25 = £100. The current year comes through her tax code and is worth another £25 across the remaining pay periods. Her first claim therefore produces roughly £125 in total, then about £25 a year while the code carries it. Had S been a higher rate taxpayer, the same deduction would save 40%, or £50 a year, making those four closed years worth £200.</p>

<h2>When can you not claim the laundry allowance?</h2>

<p>Three rules stop a claim, and between them they rule out a large share of NHS staff. Reading them first is quicker than filling in a form that will be refused.</p>

<h3>Your employer pays or reimburses the cost</h3>

<p>HMRC is blunt about this: if your employer pays all of your expenses, you cannot claim any tax relief. Where the employer meets part of the cost, only the balance is claimable.</p>

<h3>Tax rebate, washing uniform at home, and the trust laundry you do not use</h3>

<p>Laundering is not claimable where the employer provides a free laundering service and the member of staff chooses not to use it. HMRC's anti-fraud campaign names this as a headline error, describing uniform washing costs claimed "when your employer has a laundry facility that you choose not to use, such as by washing your uniform at home instead". Preferring a home machine is a choice, not an obligation.</p>

<h3>The clothing itself, and anything you would wear anyway</h3>

<p>Buying scrubs, tunics or a fleece is outside the relief entirely, however clearly they are work clothing. So is anything that functions as ordinary clothing, which is why plain black trousers bought to meet a dress code are not claimable.</p>

<h2>How to claim uniform tax relief yourself</h2>

<p>There are three routes and all of them are free. Which one applies depends on whether you already file a tax return.</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>The gov.uk online service</strong>, the fastest route for a PAYE employee, which needs a Government Gateway account.</li>
<li><strong>Form P87</strong>, HMRC's postal claim for employment expenses, which suits several past years at once or an employment that has ended.</li>
<li><strong>Your Self Assessment return</strong>, at box 18 of the SA102 employment pages. Anyone who completes a return must claim this way rather than separately.</li>
</ul>

<p>You need your National Insurance number, your employer's PAYE reference from a payslip or P60, and the tax years being claimed. Nothing else. A first claim is a job of a few minutes.</p>

<h2>How many years can you backdate a claim?</h2>

<p>In HMRC's words, you can claim for the current tax year and the 4 previous tax years. A claim made during 2026/27 reaches back to 2022/23, and that year closes for good on 5 April 2027. For someone in the same role throughout, that is five years of relief in one claim, which is why a first claim is worth more than every year after it.</p>

<h2>Your tax code, uniform relief, and why no cheque arrives</h2>

<p>Most people expect a payment and get a coding notice instead. For the current year HMRC normally makes a uniform tax code adjustment, raising the amount you can earn before tax so the relief reaches you a little at a time through payroll. Only the closed years produce an actual repayment.</p>

<p>That carries one consequence worth checking first. Where the flat rate already sits in your code, from an earlier claim or an employer notification, claiming it again does not double the relief. It creates an underpayment HMRC will collect back. Your current code is on your P2 coding notice or in your personal tax account, and a flat rate expense already allowed is itemised there.</p>

<h2>Should you use a tax refund company?</h2>

<p>Companies advertise heavily on this vocabulary and offer to make the claim in return for a share of the repayment, often a large share of a small amount. They cannot obtain anything a taxpayer cannot obtain directly.</p>

<p>HMRC's position is that anyone claiming through the free service on gov.uk "will receive 100% of the money owed". Its campaign puts the other half plainly: "if it turns out you aren't eligible to claim, you're still responsible for any claims made on your behalf". The agent takes a share of the upside and none of the downside.</p>

<p>The mechanism behind that trade has changed. Repayment agents used to take a <strong>deed of assignment</strong>, a binding transfer that sent the repayment to the agent and could not be undone by the taxpayer. HMRC legislated to render assignments of income tax repayments void, so they are no longer recognised for income tax purposes. Agents now rely on a <strong>nomination</strong> instead, which directs the payment the same way but can be withdrawn at any time.</p>

<h2>Professional subscriptions are a separate claim on the same form</h2>

<p>Your registration fee and any professional subscription paid personally are deductible in their own right under ITEPA 2003 s.343 and s.344, where the body appears on HMRC's List 3 of approved organisations. They go on the same P87 or online claim under the same four year limit, so both are worth doing at once.</p>
