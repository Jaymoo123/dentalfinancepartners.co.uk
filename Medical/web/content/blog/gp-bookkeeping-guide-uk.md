<p>GP practice bookkeeping is a different job from standard business bookkeeping. A general practice records NHS contract income that arrives in several distinct streams, separates partner drawings from the profit each partner is actually taxed on, allocates shared practice costs, and now has to keep records in a digital, Making Tax Digital ready form. This guide walks through how to record each piece correctly so the partnership return and every partner's personal return stand up.</p>

<p>The detail that trips practices up is income. A GP partnership does not bill in dental UDA bands or charge a simple fee per job. Core funding comes through the <strong>Global Sum</strong>, weighted by the Carr-Hill formula, on top of which sit <strong>QOF</strong>, enhanced services and Primary Care Network funding. Recording those streams accurately, and matching them to the period they relate to, is most of what GP bookkeeping is about.</p>

<h2>Recording GP NHS income streams correctly</h2>

<p>NHS primary medical care in England is delivered under GMS (General Medical Services), PMS (Personal Medical Services) or APMS (Alternative Provider Medical Services) contracts. Whichever your practice holds, the bookkeeping should give each income stream its own category rather than dropping everything into one "NHS income" line. The streams to track separately are:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong><a href="/blog/how-gms-funding-works-global-sum-carr-hill-explained">Global Sum</a>.</strong> The core per-patient payment, weighted by the Carr-Hill formula. The national price is set per <strong>weighted patient</strong>, and a weighted list differs from a headcount, so the amount that lands is specific to your practice: record what your NHS statements show.</li>
<li><strong><a href="/blog/qof-income-gp-practice-accounting-explained">QOF (Quality and Outcomes Framework)</a>.</strong> A voluntary, points-based quality scheme. The QOF point value is uprated each year, and payment usually follows the achievement period, so this is a classic accruals item.</li>
<li><strong><a href="/blog/enhanced-services-gp-practice-income-tax">Enhanced services</a>.</strong> Extended hours, minor surgery, contraception and other locally or nationally commissioned services. Keep each contract identifiable for both the books and renewal discussions.</li>
<li><strong><a href="/blog/pcn-funding-network-contract-des-explained">PCN and Network Contract DES funding</a>.</strong> Primary Care Network income, including the <a href="/blog/arrs-reimbursement-employing-pcn-staff-tax">Additional Roles Reimbursement Scheme (ARRS)</a>, needs proper allocation between participating practices.</li>
<li><strong><a href="/blog/dispensing-practice-income-accounts-tax">Dispensing income</a></strong> where the practice is a dispensing practice, and any <strong>private or non-NHS fees</strong> (insurance medicals, occupational health, medico-legal), which can carry different VAT and tax treatment.</li>
</ul>

<p>Note what GP income is <strong>not</strong>: it is not measured in UDAs or dental activity bands. If a source quotes "the Global Sum is £X per patient", treat that as indicative only and book the real numbers from your statements.</p>

<h2>Accruals and cut-off: matching NHS income to the right year</h2>

<p>Because QOF and many enhanced-services receipts land after the work is done, GP practices should use accruals accounting and apply clean cut-off at the accounting date. Income earned in the year but received afterwards is accrued in; payments received in advance are deferred. The same applies to large costs such as annual indemnity or insurance that span periods. Getting cut-off right is what makes the profit figure accurate, and the profit figure is what each partner is taxed on, so loose cut-off feeds straight through to personal tax.</p>

<h2>Drawings versus profit share: the distinction that matters</h2>

<p>The single most important bookkeeping concept in a GP partnership is the gap between drawings and profit share. Drawings are advance cash payments a partner takes during the year, posted to that partner's <strong>current account</strong> as they occur. Profit share is the actual allocation of the year's profit under the partnership agreement, calculated after year-end.</p>

<p>Drawings are never an expense in the practice accounts, and a partner is <strong>not taxed on what they drew</strong>. A GP partner is taxed on their profit share. The partnership files an SA800; each partner's share then flows to the partnership pages of their personal return and is taxed as trading income, with Class 4 National Insurance (6% on profits between £12,570 and £50,270, then 2% above, for 2026/27). Class 2 is no longer a required payment from 6 April 2024 where profits are at or above the small profits threshold.</p>

<p>Practically, the books need a <strong>current account per partner</strong> recording drawings taken, any practice costs a partner paid personally, and the year-end profit allocation. The difference leaves a balance owed to or by each partner. Because partners often take unequal drawings to reflect different profit-share ratios or sessions, the system has to track each partner individually. For the mechanics of how shares are set and the tax planning around them, see our guide to <a href="/blog/gp-partnership-profit-sharing-tax-planning">GP partnership profit sharing and tax planning</a>.</p>

<h2>Capital accounts, partner changes and premises</h2>

<p>Alongside current accounts, each partner has a <strong>capital account</strong> recording their longer-term investment in the practice (a share of net assets, working capital, and any owned premises). When a partner joins, a buy-in pays for their share of net assets; when a partner leaves, a buy-out returns their capital. These movements must be posted carefully, especially where they happen mid-year, because they change the profit-sharing split and the capital balances at the same time. For how the current account and the capital account actually read in a set of accounts, see our guide to <a href="/blog/reading-gp-partnership-accounts-current-capital-accounts">reading GP partnership accounts</a>.</p>

<p>One point GP bookkeeping must get right: NHS goodwill is not part of the picture. The sale of NHS GP goodwill has been prohibited since 1 April 2004 (currently under SI 2019/251), so a partner's entry or exit is about <strong>tangible assets, premises and capital accounts</strong>, never a sale of NHS goodwill. Premises are often held in a separate property partnership outside the medical partnership, with income support via <a href="/blog/gp-surgery-notional-rent-vs-cost-rent-explained">notional rent or legacy cost rent</a>, and they carry their own bookkeeping and the well-known "last man standing" risk. We cover that in our note on <a href="/blog/gp-partnership-last-man-standing-premises-risk">the last man standing premises risk</a>.</p>

<h2>Allocating practice expenses</h2>

<p>GP practice costs need consistent categories so the partnership return is accurate and nothing deductible is missed. The main groups are:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Staff costs.</strong> Usually the largest line: employed staff salaries, employer National Insurance, employer pension contributions and any agency cover. Remember partner drawings and profit share sit outside this entirely, they are distributions of profit, not staff costs.</li>
<li><strong>Premises costs.</strong> Rent (shown separately where it is paid to partner landlords), rates, utilities, repairs, and the treatment of any owned building.</li>
<li><strong>Professional and clinical costs.</strong> GMC retention fees, indemnity subscriptions (MDU, MPS or MDDUS), BMA and Royal College membership where on HMRC's approved List 3, and CPD relevant to current practice. A medical-specific point: NHS GP clinical negligence has been state-indemnified through CNSGP since 1 April 2019, so partners' own paid indemnity is now mainly for private, non-clinical or regulatory cover.</li>
<li><strong>Equipment.</strong> Clinical and IT equipment is normally relieved through capital allowances (the Annual Investment Allowance gives 100% relief up to £1,000,000), not booked as a simple expense.</li>
</ul>

<p>For an itemised view of what a doctor can and cannot claim, see our <a href="/blog/gp-tax-deductions-complete-list-2026">complete list of GP tax deductions</a>.</p>

<h2>Keeping MTD-ready digital records</h2>

<p>Making Tax Digital for Income Tax (MTD for ITSA) mandates digital records and quarterly updates by <strong>qualifying income</strong>, phased in as £50,000 from 6 April 2026, £30,000 from 6 April 2027 and £20,000 from 6 April 2028. Two points matter for a GP practice.</p>

<p>First, <strong>general partnerships are deferred</strong> with no confirmed start date, so a GP partnership is not mandated at partnership level yet. Second, that does not let an individual partner off the hook: a partner with their own sole-trader income (private clinics, expert-witness work, locum sessions) is tested on that personal qualifying income, and most full-time locums and unincorporated private GPs exceed £50,000 and are in scope from 6 April 2026. Limited companies are outside MTD for Income Tax altogether, so a locum trading through a company is not in MTD for ITSA on that income.</p>

<p>The practical takeaway is to keep digital records now regardless of the partnership timeline, because the partnership's numbers feed personal returns that are increasingly being brought into MTD. Do not confuse this with the old £10,000 figure, which no longer applies.</p>

<h2>VAT for GP practices: usually exempt, but watch the watch-items</h2>

<p>NHS GMS and PMS income is outside the scope of VAT, and exempt medical care (where the principal purpose is the protection, maintenance or restoration of health) does not count towards the registration threshold. That keeps most practices below the line. Registration is required only when <strong>taxable (non-exempt) turnover</strong> passes <strong>£90,000</strong> in a rolling 12 months (the threshold rose from £85,000 on 1 April 2024), with a deregistration threshold of £88,000.</p>

<p>The bookkeeping job is to track non-exempt income separately, because some receipts are standard-rated even though a doctor provides them: many medico-legal and expert-witness reports, purely cosmetic or aesthetic work, and some occupational-health and administrative services. A practice or partner generating enough of those can cross £90,000 on taxable turnover alone. Our <a href="/blog/gp-vat-registration">guide to GP VAT registration</a> sets out what counts and when partial exemption applies.</p>

<h2>Software: get the setup right, not a particular brand</h2>

<p>You do not need a single specified product. Mainstream cloud bookkeeping software handles GP partnership accounting perfectly well once it is configured with the right income categories, a current account per partner, and bank-feed coding rules that keep the NHS streams separate. The choice that matters is configuration and discipline, not logo. Where a practice uses dedicated medical software or integrates with the clinical system, that can streamline income recording, but the same principles apply. Our overview of <a href="/blog/gp-accounting-software">GP accounting software</a> compares the options.</p>

<p>Whatever the tool, the records have to be digital end to end to satisfy Making Tax Digital, and they have to reconcile to the bank, <a href="/blog/gp-practice-income-pcse-statement-reconciliation">the NHS statements</a> and each partner's current account.</p>

<h2>Common GP bookkeeping mistakes to avoid</h2>

<ul>
<li><strong>Treating drawings as profit (or as an expense).</strong> Drawings go to current accounts; profit share is the taxable figure. Mixing them distorts both the accounts and every partner's tax.</li>
<li><strong>Lumping all NHS income together.</strong> Without separate Global Sum, QOF, enhanced-services and PCN lines you cannot check funding, accrue correctly or answer queries.</li>
<li><strong>Weak cut-off.</strong> Booking QOF or enhanced-services income in the month the cash lands rather than the year it was earned misstates profit and tax.</li>
<li><strong>Mixing personal and practice money.</strong> Paying practice costs from a personal account, or vice versa, without posting it to the right current account.</li>
<li><strong>Mishandling partner changes mid-year.</strong> Buy-ins, buy-outs and changed profit-share ratios need careful current and capital account adjustments.</li>
<li><strong>Assuming the partnership is in MTD when it is deferred, or assuming a partner is out when their own income is in scope.</strong></li>
</ul>

<h2>Reading a set of GP practice accounts</h2>

<p>Bookkeeping exists to produce a document you eventually have to read: the annual <strong>GP practice accounts</strong>, usually tabled at a partners' meeting and rarely explained line by line. Clinical training covers none of this, so the paperwork can feel opaque even when you have been a partner for years.</p>

<p>Once you know what each statement is for, that meeting becomes a decision you take instead of a briefing you sit through. A set of <strong>medical practice accounts</strong> earns its keep in several ways:</p>

<ul>
<li>Showing you how the practice performed, stream by stream, instead of how busy the year felt.</li>
<li>Giving you the profit share that drives your personal tax and pension figures.</li>
<li>Evidencing practice earnings to your bank, a lender or a landlord.</li>
<li>Supporting your forecasts, staffing decisions and investment in premises or equipment.</li>
<li>Valuing the position of a partner joining you or retiring.</li>
</ul>

<p>Your profit share is also what separates your tax position from a salaried GP's, and the two are set side by side in the guide to <a href="/blog/gp-partner-vs-salaried-gp-tax-comparison">partner versus salaried GP tax</a>.</p>

<h3>Treating the year end as a decision</h3>

<p>Your practice most likely runs its accounting <strong>year end</strong> to 31 March, in line with the NHS financial year, which keeps the practice accounts and the NHS statements on the same clock. Treat that date as a choice you own instead of one you inherited: moving an established year end changes which profits fall into which tax year for you and for every one of your partners, so take advice before you move it.</p>

<h3>Benchmarking practice performance</h3>

<p><strong>Benchmarking</strong> sets your cost and income lines against practices of a similar size and locality, normally as a percentage of income instead of in pounds. Treat what comes back as a prompt for your next question and not as a verdict, because a high locum line may reflect a vacancy you already understand.</p>

<p>Any comparison you run depends on consistent coding underneath it. That is the practical argument for building the structure set out below and then leaving it alone.</p>

<h3>How pensions appear in the accounts</h3>

<p>The <strong>superannuation</strong> line will confuse you more than any other line in your accounts. Employer contributions for employed staff are a practice cost and sit in staff costs. Your own contributions are personal, and they are usually deducted at source from the practice's NHS receipts, so post them to your current account and keep them out of practice expenses.</p>

<p>Your pensionable profit is then certified separately, which is why the accounts and the pension paperwork never match line for line. For the relief position see <a href="/blog/gp-pension-contributions-tax-relief">GP pension contributions and tax relief</a>, and for scheme membership see the <a href="/nhs-pension">NHS pension</a> overview.</p>

<h2>A chart of accounts for a GP practice</h2>

<p>Bookkeeping software will not invent the right structure for general practice, so you have to build it. Set up a <strong>chart of accounts</strong> that mirrors how the money actually arrives, so every receipt you post has one obvious home and nothing drifts into a catch-all. The <strong>income categories</strong> below are your working minimum as a GP partnership.</p>

<table>
<thead>
<tr><th>Income category</th><th>What belongs in it</th><th>Coding note</th></tr>
</thead>
<tbody>
<tr><td><strong>Core contract income</strong></td><td>Global Sum and the other core GMS, PMS or APMS payments</td><td>One code, with the payment period recorded so quarterly revisions can be traced</td></tr>
<tr><td><strong>QOF</strong></td><td>Achievement and aspiration payments</td><td>Separate code, accrued to the achievement year</td></tr>
<tr><td><strong>Enhanced services</strong></td><td>Nationally and locally commissioned services</td><td>A sub-code per contract, so each one can be reviewed at renewal</td></tr>
<tr><td><strong>PCN income</strong></td><td>Network Contract DES payments, including ARRS reimbursement</td><td>Separate code, with the allocation between member practices documented</td></tr>
<tr><td><strong>Dispensing income</strong></td><td>Dispensing receipts where the practice dispenses</td><td>Its own code, kept clear of core contract income</td></tr>
<tr><td><strong>Premises income</strong></td><td>Notional or cost rent reimbursement, and any rent received</td><td>Separate code, and separate again from the property partnership if one exists</td></tr>
<tr><td><strong>Private and non-NHS fees</strong></td><td>Insurance medicals, occupational health, medico-legal and report fees</td><td>Track separately, because the VAT treatment differs from exempt medical care</td></tr>
<tr><td><strong>Sundry income</strong></td><td>Room hire, recharges, refunds and small one-off receipts</td><td>Review regularly and promote anything recurring to its own code</td></tr>
</tbody>
</table>

<p>The <strong>sundry income</strong> line is where a practice quietly loses track of itself. Anything you cannot name is a category you have not created yet: room hire, shared staff recharges, refunds and one-off receipts each deserve a code of their own. Keep sundry small enough that you can explain every entry in it.</p>

<h3>Coding the expense side</h3>

<p>Mirror the same discipline on your costs: staff, premises, professional and clinical, administration, and equipment. Equipment is the line that will catch you out, because you relieve it through capital allowances instead of expensing it straight to the profit and loss account.</p>

<p>The Annual Investment Allowance gives you 100% relief on up to £1,000,000 of qualifying expenditure. Above that, main-rate plant attracts a writing-down allowance of 14% from 6 April 2026 for income tax and 1 April 2026 for corporation tax, the special rate pool runs at 6%, and a 40% first-year allowance applies to new main-rate plant you buy from 1 January 2026.</p>

<h2>How specialist medical bookkeeping helps</h2>

<p>GP income, the drawings-versus-profit distinction, premises held in a separate partnership, and the NHS-pension and annual-allowance angles all sit outside standard small-business bookkeeping. A specialist medical accountant configures the income categories correctly, keeps the records MTD-ready, and makes sure the partnership numbers feed each partner's return accurately. That accuracy also supports related decisions covered elsewhere on this site, from the <a href="/calculators/nhs-pension-annual-allowance">NHS pension annual allowance</a> for higher-earning partners to whether private work should ever be <a href="/blog/medical-practice-incorporation-step-by-step">incorporated</a> (a private-work-only question, since an ordinary personal service company cannot hold an NHS contract and company income is not NHS-pensionable).</p>
