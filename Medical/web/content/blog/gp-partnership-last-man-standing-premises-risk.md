<p>When partners leave a GP partnership faster than new ones join, the liability attached to the surgery does not shrink to match. A leasehold practice can leave the remaining partners, in the worst case a single partner, holding the whole lease. An owner-occupier practice can leave departing partners unable to find anyone to buy their share of the building. This is the <strong>last man standing</strong> problem, and it has become the single biggest reason incoming partners now hesitate to buy in to premises.</p>

<p>This guide explains how the risk arises, why it has grown, the protections that have been built in (the contract-linked break right in the NHS standard lease, and the negative-equity protection in the Premises Costs Directions), and how the partnership deed and the ownership structure are used to manage it. It is written for partners weighing a buy-in and for practices trying to make their premises liability survivable. It is risk awareness rather than legal drafting advice, and premises remain a specialist area where practice-specific advice matters.</p>

<h2>What last man standing actually means</h2>

<p>The plain version is this: when partners leave and are not replaced, the liabilities tied to the surgery do not disappear. A lease still runs for its term. An owned building still carries its loan. Those obligations concentrate on whoever remains, and if numbers fall far enough they can land on one partner, potentially exposing that individual to the whole liability.</p>

<p>There are two flavours. The <strong>leasehold version</strong> leaves one partner on the hook for the full lease and the end-of-lease dilapidations. The <strong>owner-occupier version</strong> leaves a departing partner unable to sell their share of the building, or leaves the practice holding surplus or over-valued premises on its books. Both stem from the same root cause: premises liability is fixed by contract and does not flex with partner numbers. This is why premises risk now sits near the top of any buy-in conversation, and why it deserves to be understood before, not after, a partner commits.</p>

<h2>Why the risk has grown</h2>

<p>Several pressures have pushed this risk up the agenda. Partnership numbers have been under strain, with partners leaving for salaried, locum or portfolio roles and fewer clinicians willing to take on premises liability. Many surgeries sit on long leases, and some were developed at a scale that made sense for a larger partnership than the one now occupying them. Recruiting an incoming partner willing to buy in to the building has become harder, which removes the natural buyer for an outgoing partner's share.</p>

<p>The result is a structural mismatch: the premises were taken on by a partnership that expected to stay roughly the same size, while the partnership itself has become smaller and more fluid. The national picture varies from area to area, so the dynamic matters more to any one practice than a headline statistic does. For the broader financial picture of becoming a partner, see our guide to the <a href="/blog/becoming-gp-partner-financial-implications">financial implications of becoming a GP partner</a>.</p>

<h2>How the risk concentrates: an illustrative walk-through</h2>

<p>It helps to follow the dynamic through an anonymised, clearly illustrative example instead of describing it in the abstract. The figures and timings below are made up to show the mechanism, not to describe any real practice. Picture a partnership that, some years ago, took a long lease on a purpose-built health centre sized for six full-time partners. At that point the lease liability, the rent and the eventual dilapidations were spread six ways, and each partner's slice of the obligation felt manageable against their profit share.</p>

<p>Over the following years the partnership thins. One partner retires and is replaced by a salaried GP and not by a new partner, because the practice cannot find an incoming partner willing to take on the lease. A second partner moves to a portfolio career and, again, is backfilled with sessional and locum cover. A third reduces to a part-time clinical role outside the partnership. The clinical work still gets done, but the number of partners actually signed up to the lease has fallen, while the lease itself has not changed at all. Its term still runs, the rent is still due, and the repairing and dilapidations obligations are unchanged.</p>

<p>The arithmetic is unforgiving. The same fixed liability that once sat across six partners now sits across two or three, and because the liability on the lease is usually joint and several (covered below), each of those remaining partners is individually exposed to the whole of it, not merely a larger fraction. If the thinning continues to a single remaining partner, that one GP can in principle stand behind the entire rent and the full end-of-lease repairing cost on a building scaled for a partnership several times larger. Nothing dramatic has to happen for this to arise: no insolvency, no dispute, just ordinary retirements and career changes that are not matched by incoming partners willing to take on the premises. That is precisely why the risk is structural rather than exceptional, and why it is worth planning for before, not after, the partnership starts to thin.</p>

<p>The owner-occupier version of the same walk-through runs in parallel. Replace the lease with an owned building carrying a loan, and the departing partners now want their capital (their share of the building) returned on exit. If no incoming partner will buy that share, the remaining partners must either fund the buy-out from their own resources or leave the share unsold, and the loan and the upkeep continue to fall on a shrinking group. The liability has moved from a lease to a building, but the concentration mechanism is identical.</p>

<h2>The leasehold version of the risk</h2>

<p>In a leasehold practice the central feature is <strong>joint and several liability</strong>. Partners are usually jointly and severally liable on the lease, which means the landlord can pursue any one partner for the whole of the rent, and for dilapidations at the end of the term, regardless of that partner's share of the practice. The Partnership Act 1890 sits behind general-partnership liability, and it is unforgiving in this respect.</p>

<h3>The last man standing exposure</h3>

<p>If partners leave without the lease being reassigned, and no new partners take on the lease obligations, the remaining partner can face the full ongoing rent and the repairing obligations on a building far larger than one GP can use or fund. The lease does not care that the partnership has shrunk. This is the leasehold version of the problem in its starkest form, and it is the scenario the protections below are designed to soften.</p>

<h3>The NHS standard lease and the break right</h3>

<p>The NHS Property Services and BMA standard lease for health-centre occupiers typically includes a tenant <strong>break right tied to the core NHS contract</strong>: the tenant can break the lease if the core NHS contract is terminated or not renewed. That is more favourable than a typical commercial lease, where the tenant is generally locked in for the term with at most occasional break dates. It is a genuine protection, because it links the property obligation to the income that supports it. It is not a complete fix, though: it is contract-linked, not an at-will exit, and the exact wording varies, so the lease itself has to be read carefully and never assumed.</p>

<h3>Surplus premises and dilapidations</h3>

<p>Two further leasehold exposures deserve a flag. The first is <strong>dilapidations</strong>: at the end of the lease the tenant is usually liable to put the premises back into the contractual state of repair, which can be a significant cost that lands on whoever holds the lease at that point. The second is being tied to <strong>surplus space</strong> the practice no longer needs but cannot easily give up. Both are reasons to understand the full lease terms before taking them on, and to plan for the end of the term and not only the start.</p>

<h2>The owner-occupier version of the risk</h2>

<p>Where the partnership owns its surgery, the risk takes a different shape. Departing partners naturally want their capital out, which means their share of the building. But if incoming partners increasingly will not buy in, there is no ready buyer for the outgoing share. The remaining partners may have to fund the buy-out themselves, or the share simply sits unsold.</p>

<h3>Negative equity and surplus premises</h3>

<p>Where a building is worth less than the loan secured on it, or is over-specified for current needs, the difficulty deepens: departing partners are reluctant to release on poor terms, and incoming partners are reluctant to take on a liability that exceeds the asset value. There is a specific protection here, and it is narrower and stronger than it is usually reported. Direction 13(5) to (6) of the Premises Costs Directions applies where a grant-funded owner-occupier has terminated its GMS contract before the guaranteed period of use expires, has sold the practice premises, and there is no lease of those premises back to it and no possibility of one. Where all of that holds, the repayable amount is capped at the actual sale price of the premises, or if higher the best sale price reasonably obtainable on the open market as determined by the district valuer or the appointed valuer. It is the higher of the two figures, not whichever the commissioner prefers. That is designed to stop a grant clawback from creating negative equity. It is genuine mitigation, but it does not conjure a buyer or release trapped capital, so it limits the downside without removing the problem. The mechanics of notional rent and grant abatement that sit behind this are covered in our guide to <a href="/blog/gp-surgery-notional-rent-vs-cost-rent-explained">notional rent vs cost rent</a>.</p>

<h3>Why incoming partners resist buying in</h3>

<p>The core dynamic is simple. Incoming partners look at the liability, the illiquidity of a surgery as an asset, and the recruitment trend that makes a future exit uncertain, and many decide a salaried role or a non-property-owning partner route suits them better. That decision is rational at the individual level, but in aggregate it removes the buyer that the outgoing-partner model relies on. Understanding this is the key to managing the risk: the structures and deed provisions below exist largely to make buying in less daunting, or to make the building survivable without a steady stream of buyers.</p>

<h2>What an incoming partner should check before buying in</h2>

<p>A practical due-diligence list, to work through with professional advice and without relying on any assumed figures:</p>

<ul>
<li>The lease term and any break rights, including the contract-linked NHS break right and its exact wording.</li>
<li>The dilapidations exposure and who would bear it at the end of the term.</li>
<li>The premises valuation, and how the buy-in and any future buy-out are priced.</li>
<li>Whether the premises sit in a separate property partnership or in the medical partnership.</li>
<li>The deed's exit and retirement-limit provisions, notice periods and continuity terms.</li>
<li>Where the premises are owned, the loan position and the notional rent supporting it, covered in our <a href="/blog/gp-surgery-notional-rent-vs-cost-rent-explained">notional rent guide</a>.</li>
</ul>

<h2>The two-tier partnership: property-owning and non-property-owning partners</h2>

<p>A common response is to hold the premises in a <strong>separate property partnership or LLP</strong>, outside the medical partnership. Only some partners (the property owners) carry the building, while clinical partners can join the medical partnership without buying in to the premises. This decouples the clinical career decision from the property investment decision. The tax detail, including SDLT, capital allowances and capital gains tax, sits in the <a href="/blog/gp-surgery-premises-own-vs-rent-tax-guide">own vs rent tax guide</a>.</p>

<h3>The advantages</h3>

<p>New partners can join without a premises buy-in, which directly addresses the recruitment barrier. Property risk is ring-fenced from the clinical partnership, so a problem with the building is less likely to destabilise the practice. And the rent stream is cleanly separated in its own vehicle.</p>

<h3>The cautions</h3>

<p>The property partners still carry the liability; separating the vehicle moves the risk, it does not remove it. The structure has to be documented carefully, with the property-partnership agreement, the lease and the medical-partnership deed all consistent with each other. And it does not by itself solve the last man standing problem if every property partner wants out at the same time. So this is a structure to weigh with advice, not a guaranteed escape from the underlying dynamic.</p>

<h2>How the partnership deed manages the risk</h2>

<p>The deed is the primary control. The levers below are matters to settle with your solicitor and accountant when the deed is reviewed.</p>

<h3>Limiting simultaneous exits</h3>

<p>A well-drafted deed can cap how many partners may retire in a single accounting period. That prevents a domino collapse of partner numbers, where several exits in quick succession leave the rest exposed before any recruitment can catch up. Staggering exits buys the time needed to recruit, restructure or assign.</p>

<h3>Notice periods and buy-out terms</h3>

<p>Long notice periods give the practice room to plan. Clear buy-out terms, including how a premises share is valued and the timing of payment, remove the uncertainty that otherwise has to be negotiated under pressure at the point of exit. The deed should spell out what happens to an outgoing partner's premises share, so nobody is relying on goodwill at a difficult moment.</p>

<h3>Continuity and avoiding accidental dissolution</h3>

<p>Provisions that keep the partnership going, instead of dissolving it on a partner's departure, matter because an unintended dissolution can crystallise liabilities. A <strong>partnership at will</strong> (no deed, or a deed that has lapsed) is especially dangerous here, because the default rules can allow dissolution on notice in a way that nobody planned for. A current, continuity-minded deed is the antidote.</p>

<h3>How a premises share is valued and bought out</h3>

<p>One clause does more practical work than almost any other: how an outgoing partner's premises share is valued, and when it is paid. Without a clear mechanism, every exit becomes a negotiation conducted at the worst possible moment, when one side wants out and the other does not want to fund a buy-out. A workable deed (or, where the building sits in a separate vehicle, the property-partnership agreement) usually sets out who values the share (commonly an independent surveyor), the basis of that valuation, and a timetable for payment that does not place an unmanageable demand on the remaining partners all at once. Staged payment terms, for example, can let the continuing partners absorb a buy-out without a cash crisis. The point is not the precise mechanism, which is a matter for your solicitor, but that one exists and is agreed in advance and not improvised on exit. No standard valuation figure or formula applies here, because a premises valuation is property-specific and a matter for a surveyor.</p>

<h3>Aligning the deed, the lease and the property structure</h3>

<p>The deed, the lease and any property-partnership agreement must be consistent. If the deed assumes the premises sit in a property partnership but the lease is held by the medical partnership, or if the buy-out mechanics in the deed conflict with the property-partnership agreement, the protections can fall apart at the moment they are needed. A common failure mode is documents drafted at different times by different advisers that quietly contradict each other: the deed promises a buy-out the property-partnership agreement does not fund, or the lease names tenants who are no longer the current partners. Reviewing all three together, and refreshing them when partners join or leave, is the safest approach. This is also where the accounting and the legal documents have to line up, because the way the premises and the buy-out obligation are recorded in the partners' capital accounts has to reflect what the deed actually says.</p>

<h2>Who is named on the lease, and who owns what share?</h2>

<p>Two documents decide who is actually carrying the premises on the day a partner leaves: the lease, which names the tenants, and, where the surgery is owned, the declaration of trust that records the shares. Both go quietly out of date as partners join and retire, and keeping them current is the cheapest work on this whole topic. Where the surgery is owned the loan adds its own pressure, because mortgage redemption penalties can make an early exit expensive, and the tax treatment of that borrowing is covered in the <a href="/blog/gp-surgery-premises-own-vs-rent-tax-guide">own vs rent tax guide</a>.</p>

<h3>Joining new partners to the lease, and taking retired partners off</h3>

<p>When a practice takes on a new partner, that partner should be joined to the lease as a tenant, and a retiring partner can usually be removed at the same time. Done at every partner change, this keeps the people carrying the liability the same as the people running the practice. It also avoids the position a surprising number of practices reach, where they have to trace partners who left years ago because those partners are still named on the lease and the landlord's consent turns on them.</p>

<p>An incoming partner should take advice at the same point on whether they are picking up obligations that should have sat with earlier partners, for example whether the building has been kept in repair through the years before they arrived. These are steps to take with your solicitor, and what your lease permits depends on its own wording.</p>

<h3>Where the surgery is owned, the declaration of trust</h3>

<p>Where premises are held jointly, the instrument that records who owns what share is usually a separate declaration of trust, sitting alongside the partnership deed and any property-partnership agreement. If the deed refers to premises shares and no declaration of trust exists, that is worth raising with your solicitor well ahead of any exit.</p>

<h2>What a last man standing partner can actually do</h2>

<p>If you are the remaining partner, there are options, though none is guaranteed and all should be taken with professional advice. Engage the ICB early: they have a view of the local landscape and may know of practices looking to expand or take over. Explore <strong>assignment</strong> of the lease to another practice. Consider <strong>surrender</strong>, if the landlord will agree. For grant-funded premises, the Premises Costs Directions provide their own routes, set out below. The common thread is to start the conversation early, because the workable options narrow as time runs down.</p>

<h3>Assignment, surrender and the NHS routes in more detail</h3>

<p>It is worth understanding what each of those routes actually involves, because they are not interchangeable. <strong>Assignment</strong> moves the lease to a new tenant, typically another practice or an NHS body, and is usually the cleanest exit where a willing assignee exists, but it normally needs the landlord's consent and the assignee has to be acceptable to both the landlord and the commissioner. <strong>Surrender</strong> hands the lease back to the landlord and ends it early, which only happens if the landlord agrees and often only on terms (a payment, or settling outstanding dilapidations), because the landlord is giving up a paying tenant. Neither is automatic, and both take time to arrange.</p>

<p>For premises that received an improvement grant or other capital support, the NHS (General Medical Services) Premises Costs Directions 2024, in force 10 May 2024 and replacing the 2013 Directions, add a further set of routes aimed precisely at a contractor who cannot find a successor. Direction 13(4) removes the repayment obligation where the owner-occupier leases the premises to another contractor or to a provider of primary medical services commissioned by NHS England, and that person agrees to take on the grant conditions for the remainder of the guaranteed period of use. Direction 13(7) does the same where leasehold premises are involved and the lease is assigned to such a person on the same terms. Direction 13(9) to (12) is the fallback: where the premises are leasehold and the contractor has used all reasonable endeavours to find an assignee or to agree a surrender with the landlord and has failed, and NHS England has been unable or has chosen not to procure a replacement provider, NHS England may waive the repayment at its discretion, having regard to the circumstances of the termination, the remaining guaranteed period, the commitments given when the grant was awarded and any succession planning undertaken. The negative-equity cap described earlier sits in the same direction. Taken together these are meaningful backstops, but two of the three are conditional on somebody else agreeing to take the premises on, and the third is a discretion, so the outcome depends on the commissioner and the specific premises. The practical message is unchanged: open the conversation with the ICB and your advisers well before the situation becomes urgent, because every one of these routes works better with time in hand.</p>

<h3>If no partner and no practice will take the lease on</h3>

<p>If no practice will take yours on and the commissioner still wants services provided from the building, an interim provider may be found to take the lease. Where the premises are no longer needed for NHS services at all, the lease's alternative-use provisions become the live question: it may be possible to assign or sublet to a private health provider, or to another use entirely, depending on the restrictions the lease imposes.</p>

<p>One term worth knowing before you read your own lease is the <strong>Armageddon clause</strong>, a termination right separate from an ordinary break right that can normally be operated only in very limited circumstances. It is market shorthand and not a statutory term, so what it permits depends entirely on the drafting. Where a lease carries one, or carries a break right, take advice well before the relevant date, because both are drafted narrowly and both can be lost by missing a deadline.</p>

<h3>Surplus and over-specified premises</h3>

<p>A related problem is space the practice no longer needs. A building scaled for a larger partnership, or for services that have since moved elsewhere, can leave the practice paying for and repairing rooms it cannot fill. Sub-letting part of the premises to another health provider, where the lease permits it and the landlord and commissioner agree, can sometimes share the cost, though it brings its own management and lease-compliance obligations. Surplus premises rarely have a tidy fix, which is one more reason the size and flexibility of the premises commitment deserve thought at the point it is taken on, not only when it has become a burden.</p>

<h2>What the lease does to the practice accounts as partners leave</h2>

<p>There is no such thing as a last man standing GP practice lease. The lease is an ordinary one, its wording does not change as partners leave, and what changes is the accounts. The exposure described above is a legal liability, and it is also a number that eventually has to appear in the practice accounts. Dilapidations provision accounting turns on three tests, and a partnership losing partners can move from failing those tests to meeting them without a word of the lease changing. GP practice accounts are normally prepared under UK GAAP, where FRS 102 Section 21 (Provisions and Contingencies) governs when a liability of uncertain timing or amount has to be recognised.</p>

<h3>When a provision for dilapidations goes on the balance sheet</h3>

<p>A provision is the recognition of a liability whose timing or amount is uncertain. HMRC sets out the basis at BIM46515: a provision must satisfy the definition of a liability, which is a present obligation arising from past events whose settlement is expected to result in an outflow of resources. Anticipation of future spending, however probable and however carefully estimated, is not enough on its own without an obligation at the reporting date.</p>

<p>So a provision for dilapidations is recognised once the lease imposes the obligation, the wear or alteration that creates it has already happened, and the cost can be estimated with sufficient accuracy. Where an outflow is possible but not probable, or where no reliable estimate can be made, FRS 102 Section 21 requires the obligation to be disclosed as a contingent liability in a note to the accounts, with nothing recognised on the balance sheet itself (BIM46530). A shrinking partnership is very often sitting in that second position, which is why the exposure can be entirely real and still invisible on the face of the accounts.</p>

<p>Going concern is what usually moves a practice from the second position to the first. While the partners expect to keep trading, and to renew or reassign in due course, the end of the lease is distant and the estimate is soft. Once partner numbers fall far enough that the partners and their accountant have to form a view on whether the practice will still be trading, the end of the lease moves inside the period they have to consider, the estimate firms up and the provision has to be made. The accounts then worsen at the moment the partnership is least able to absorb it. That is the accounting half of the last man standing dynamic, and it is why the timing of the assessment matters as much as the size of the liability.</p>

<h3>Are dilapidations costs deductible for a GP partnership?</h3>

<p>Partly, and the split follows the capital and revenue divide. HMRC allows a provision only where it relates to allowable revenue expenditure, accords with generally accepted accounting practice, conflicts with no statutory rule about the timing of relief, and is estimated with sufficient accuracy (BIM46510). Dilapidations in the nature of deferred repairs are revenue, and are deductible to the extent the cost would have been allowable had the repairs been carried out during the lease (BIM43260).</p>

<p>Capital dilapidations costs are not deductible at all, and BIM43255 names three: the cost of rebuilding the leased premises, the cost of reinstating any part of them the tenant has demolished, and the cost of demolishing a structure the tenant added. A purpose-built surgery altered repeatedly over a long lease can carry all three, which is one reason the end-of-lease figure a surveyor produces is rarely deductible in full.</p>

<p>Where the partners agree a composition payment with the landlord, a lump sum paid to be released from the obligation to make good the dilapidations, the same divide applies: HMRC allows it to the extent it reflects deferred repairs that would have been allowable if spent during the lease, disallows the capital element, apportions where necessary, and does not make the deduction conditional on the work actually being done (BIM43265).</p>
