# BMV acquisition — discussion paper

Date: 2026-08-08. Status: DISCUSSION, nothing decided, nothing built.
Owner's framing: if BMV access lets me buy at X% off, I could build a rental portfolio well below
market value long term. Explicitly *not* a lead-gen play.

Regulatory and market facts verified separately — see `BMV_FACTS.md`.

## The one genuine edge

Every BMV investor's binding cost is **deal origination**. They buy motivated-seller leads at
£50-500 each, pay agencies £2-5k/month, or run direct mail at negative ROI for months. It is the
single biggest reason small investors fail to scale: they can underwrite and they can borrow, but
they cannot find enough discounted stock.

We can rank for `we buy any house` (5,400/mo, **KD 3**) and `sell my house fast`
(1,300/mo @ £52.41, KD 26) at near-zero marginal cost, using engines that already exist.

That is a real, rare and defensible edge, and it is the *only* part of this where our existing
skills transfer. Worth being blunt about the corollary: SEO skill does not help us underwrite, price
refurbishment, manage builders, or let property. Those are the parts that actually lose money.

## What changes versus the lead-gen version

| | Lead-gen | Principal |
|---|---|---|
| Constraint | traffic | **capital + operations** |
| Revenue per enquiry | £30-100 once | equity + yield, compounding |
| Scales with | content | cash |
| Speed | weeks | one deal at a time |
| Ethics objection | routing a distressed seller into someone else's margin | we provide the speed and certainty ourselves |

The ethics point is real, not cosmetic. As principal, the discount is consideration for something we
actually deliver: a guaranteed price, a completion date, no chain, no fall-through. That is a
legitimate trade a seller may rationally want. As a lead seller, we would capture a fee for
introducing someone to a discount we do not provide and cannot stand behind.

It only holds if the conduct matches: tell the seller the open-market figure and our figure side by
side, name the discount in cash terms, present the alternatives (open market, auction, part
exchange), and let them walk. If we would not be comfortable with the seller reading the whole file
afterwards, it is the wrong deal.

## The structural problem: we can't buy most of what we originate

Conversion is the hard number here. Most `sell my house fast` enquiries are not distressed enough to
accept a real discount — they are curious, or they want a valuation, or they will list with an agent
next week. A funnel that produces enough deals to build a portfolio produces far more enquiries than
we can ever buy, and every one we cannot buy is a cost we have already paid for.

This is what makes the pure-principal version inefficient at the start, and it points at the answer.

## Recommended structure: originate once, monetise twice

Run the origination funnel. Apply a defined buy box. Buy the small fraction that fits, and sell the
rest — properly and transparently — to cash-buying companies, auction houses and local investors.

- The lead sales fund the acquisitions, so the funnel pays for itself from month one instead of
  waiting on capital.
- We get **first refusal on our own deal flow**, which is exactly the position every other investor
  is trying to buy.
- It reconciles this with the lead-gen research rather than discarding it.
- Capital requirement becomes incremental: buy one when one fits, rather than needing a war chest
  before starting.

Disclosure obligation that comes with it: if we take first pick and sell on the rest, the seller must
be told plainly what we are — a buyer *and* an introducer — before they give us anything. Being
straight about that is also the thing that makes the model survive scrutiny.

## Lower-risk routes to the same discount

Distressed sellers are not the only source of BMV, and some sources carry no ethical weight at all
because the discount comes from a *problem*, not from a person's misfortune:

1. **Short-lease flats — downgraded after fact-checking.** These do trade at a steep discount
   (60-70 yrs: 20-35%; 40-60 yrs: 35-60%) and the uplift is earned by doing the extension rather
   than extracted from a distressed vendor. But the premise most people are trading on is false:
   **marriage value abolition under LAFRA 2024 is NOT in force and remains payable in August 2026**,
   with realistic commencement 2027-28 at the earliest. Extend today and you pay it in full; wait
   and you carry the asset for years while the lease shortens and the new prescribed rates move the
   premium in an unknown direction. Still workable as a pure arbitrage where the discount exceeds
   the full extension cost *including* marriage value — not workable as a reform bet.
   The genuinely valuable part is that most published 2026 content gets this wrong, so an
   authoritative leasehold resource that gets it right earns citations on the Tier 1 leasehold
   cluster (KD 0-1) regardless of whether we ever buy a flat.
2. **Auction.** Probate, repossession, mixed-use, non-standard construction. Public, priced,
   no seller-facing conduct risk.
3. **Refurbishment / BRRR.** The discount is created by the works, which is the cleanest form there is.
4. **Planning gain.** Buy with permitted-development or planning upside.
5. **Developer part-exchange stock.** `part exchange house` 2,900/mo, KD 13 — developers take these
   in and want them gone.

Value traps to underwrite against, not chase: cladding/EWS1, subsidence, Japanese knotweed within
7m, non-standard construction, structural movement, contaminated title, and anything unmortgageable
for a reason that cannot be cured.

## Open decisions before anything is built

1. **Capital.** How much is actually available, and is it ours or borrowed? This determines whether
   the answer is "buy one this year" or "originate and sell while we save".
2. **Buy or broker.** Principal only, or principal plus introducer? Different compliance profile.
3. **Entity.** Under Ashfield Trading, or a separate SPV? Almost certainly separate — mixing a
   trading company with property holding creates tax and lender problems.
4. **Geography and buy box.** Price ceiling, yield floor, area, property type. Without this, deal
   flow is noise.
5. **Ops.** Who surveys, who refurbishes, who lets. This is the gap our skills do not cover.
6. **Compliance posture.** See `BMV_FACTS.md` — the requirements differ sharply between buying as
   principal and sourcing for others.

## What the fact-check changed

Three things materially altered the picture (all sourced in `BMV_FACTS.md`):

1. **Hold vs flip is the compliance fork, and the stated plan is on the clean side.** Buying as
   principal to hold as a rental sits outside the Estate Agents Act, redress schemes and the FCA.
   Buying to *flip* pulls you into HMRC AML supervision as an estate-agency business, even though
   you are principal-side. A long-term rental portfolio is the cheaper, cleaner structure.
2. **The real risk is consumer protection, not licensing.** The CPUTR were replaced by DMCCA 2024
   Part 4 from 6 April 2025, and the CMA can now fine up to **10% of global turnover directly**,
   without going to court. It has named aggressive tactics toward vulnerable consumers as an early
   priority — which is precisely this channel. The OFT's 2013 undertakings (state the discount
   clearly and prominently, value in good faith, no last-minute price-chipping) are the working
   standard. Note that 76% of sellers report a last-minute reduction attempt in this sector, so the
   conduct bar is where competitors are weakest and where enforcement will land.
3. **Numbers are less romantic than the pitch.** Genuine cash buyers pay 75-82% of market value, not
   the 50-60% the guru content implies. The widely-quoted "auction is 45% below market" figure is
   guide price versus national average house price — marketing-grade, not an achievable discount.
   Realistic capital is **£40-80k per deal, £45-55k central**, on a £100k Northern property.
   And nobody publishes enquiry-to-completion conversion, so that has to be measured, not assumed.

## My view

The edge is real and unusually well matched to what this estate already does. The origination
advantage is genuine: every competitor's biggest cost is the thing we can do for free.

But note what the hybrid structure actually implies. Selling the deals we don't buy *is* sourcing —
it triggers redress registration, HMRC AML, ICO and s.21 disclosure, and it puts us back on the side
of the ethics objection that started this. So the hybrid is not a free lunch; it is a deliberate
choice to take on the sourcing compliance stack in exchange for funding the acquisitions.

Two coherent positions, and they should not be blurred:

- **Clean and slow.** Principal only, buy to hold, no onward selling of leads or deals. Outside the
  EAA, redress and FCA entirely. Only DMCCA conduct rules and ICO apply. Constrained to roughly one
  deal per £45-55k of capital, and we absorb the cost of every enquiry we cannot buy.
- **Funded and heavier.** Originate, take first refusal, sell the rest. Pays for itself from month
  one, but requires the full sourcing stack and demands the conduct standard be genuinely good,
  because the CMA can now fine 10% of turnover directly.

I lean to **clean and slow first**, for one reason: we cannot yet size the funnel. Nobody publishes
enquiry-to-completion rates, so the economics of the hybrid rest on a number we do not have. Build
the origination page, measure real enquiry volume and quality for a quarter, then decide — by then
the conversion question is answered with our own data instead of inference, and the compliance
decision can be made knowing what it buys.

Of the acquisition routes, probate, no-chain and repossession stock is the best risk-adjusted source:
no defect at all, just a speed discount. Refurb/BRRR is next cleanest because the discount is created
by the works. Short-lease is interesting but not the slam dunk I called it earlier.

Nothing should be built until the capital and buy-box questions are answered, because origination
without the ability to transact just produces enquiries we disappoint — which is both a wasted cost
and, in this sector, a conduct risk.
