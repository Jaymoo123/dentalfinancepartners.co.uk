<p>When a marriage ends in England and Wales, two separate legal processes run side by side. The divorce itself dissolves the marriage. The <strong>financial settlement</strong> divides everything the two of you own and owe: the house, savings, pensions, businesses and debts. Plenty of people finish the first and never formalise the second, and it is one of the most expensive mistakes in family law, because financial claims between ex-spouses stay open until a court order closes them.</p>

<p>This guide covers the whole process: what counts as a matrimonial asset, how courts decide what is fair, the routes to agreement, what disclosure involves, and how to make a settlement legally binding. If you want a sense of the numbers first, our <a href="/calculators/settlement-range-estimator">settlement range estimator</a> gives an indicative range based on the factors courts actually use, and the <a href="/calculators/divorce-cost-calculator">divorce cost calculator</a> shows what the process itself is likely to cost by route.</p>

<h2>Divorce and money are separate processes</h2>

<p>The divorce application, conditional order and final order deal only with the legal status of the marriage. Under the no-fault regime introduced by the Divorce, Dissolution and Separation Act 2020 (in force since April 2022), the divorce itself is close to administrative: no blame, a minimum 20-week wait from application to conditional order, then six weeks and a day to the final order (<a href="https://www.gov.uk/divorce">gov.uk: get a divorce</a>).</p>

<p>None of that touches your finances. The court does not review your money as part of the divorce, does not divide anything automatically, and does not need to see a financial agreement before granting the final order. Financial claims are dealt with under the <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1973/18">Matrimonial Causes Act 1973</a>, and they need their own order. Until one exists:</p>

<ul>
<li>Either of you can apply to court for a share of the other's assets and income, including assets acquired <em>after</em> the divorce.</li>
<li>Inheritances, business growth, lottery wins and property gains remain potentially claimable.</li>
<li>Neither of you has certainty, however amicable things feel today.</li>
</ul>

<p>Courts have permitted claims brought long after separation where no order was ever made, which is why family lawyers treat "we sorted it between ourselves" as unfinished business. The fix is a <a href="/blog/consent-orders-and-clean-break-orders">consent order, usually with clean break terms</a>, covered later in this guide.</p>

<h2>What counts: matrimonial and non-matrimonial assets</h2>

<p>The settlement conversation starts with a full picture of what exists. Assets broadly fall into two categories.</p>

<h3>Matrimonial assets</h3>

<p>Everything built up during the marriage through the couple's joint endeavour, whichever name it is held in:</p>

<ul>
<li>The family home, even if owned before the marriage in one name, because it is treated as having a central place in the marriage</li>
<li>Savings, ISAs and investments accumulated while married</li>
<li>Pensions accrued during the marriage, often the second largest asset after the house</li>
<li>Business interests built up during the marriage</li>
<li>Vehicles, contents and other property of value</li>
</ul>

<h3>Non-matrimonial assets</h3>

<p>Assets that came from outside the marriage partnership: property owned before the marriage, inheritances, and gifts from third parties. Two caveats matter enormously in practice:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Needs trump source.</strong> If matrimonial assets are not enough to meet both parties' needs, especially housing for children, non-matrimonial assets can be invaded to bridge the gap.</li>
<li><strong>Mixing changes character.</strong> An inheritance paid into the joint account, or used to renovate the family home, tends to become matrimonial over time. Family lawyers call this matrimonialisation.</li>
</ul>

<p>Debts are part of the same exercise. Mortgages, loans, credit cards and tax liabilities are set against the asset pool, whoever's name they are in, and the court looks at what the borrowing was for.</p>

<h2>How courts decide what is fair: the section 25 factors</h2>

<p>There is no formula. Section 25 of the <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1973/18/section/25">Matrimonial Causes Act 1973</a> lists the factors a court must weigh, and the same checklist shapes every negotiated settlement because both sides' advisers negotiate in its shadow:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>The welfare of any child of the family under 18</strong>: the court's first consideration, before either adult's position</li>
<li>Income, earning capacity, property and other financial resources each party has or is likely to have</li>
<li>Financial needs, obligations and responsibilities, now and in the foreseeable future</li>
<li>The standard of living enjoyed during the marriage</li>
<li>The age of each party and the duration of the marriage</li>
<li>Any physical or mental disability</li>
<li>Contributions each has made or will make, including looking after the home and caring for children, which weigh equally with financial contributions</li>
<li>Conduct, but only where it would be inequitable to disregard it, a deliberately high bar</li>
<li>The value of any benefit (such as a pension) a party loses the chance to acquire</li>
</ul>

<p>Case law layers three principles on top: <strong>needs</strong>, <strong>sharing</strong> and <strong>compensation</strong>. In most divorces there is not enough money for anything beyond needs, so the practical question is how to house and support two households, with children's needs first. Where assets exceed needs, the sharing principle points to equal division of matrimonial property. Compensation for relationship-generated disadvantage (classically, a career given up to raise children) is rare as a separate head but feeds into needs and maintenance.</p>

<h3>So what does a fair split actually look like?</h3>

<p>Some honest generalisations, which any individual case can depart from:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Long marriage, children, one main earner:</strong> the parent housing the children often receives more than half of the capital, sometimes with ongoing spousal maintenance, because their needs and lower earning capacity demand it.</li>
<li><strong>Long marriage, no dependent children, similar earnings:</strong> equal division of matrimonial assets is the likely destination.</li>
<li><strong>Short marriage, no children:</strong> outcomes lean towards returning each party to their pre-marriage position, with pre-marital assets carrying real weight.</li>
<li><strong>Pensions:</strong> increasingly shared rather than ignored; see our <a href="/blog/pensions-and-divorce">complete guide to pensions and divorce</a>.</li>
</ul>

<h2>Financial disclosure: the foundation of every settlement</h2>

<p>Every route to a settlement rests on both parties knowing what there is to divide. In court proceedings, disclosure is compulsory via <strong>Form E</strong>, a long sworn statement of assets, income, pensions and needs (<a href="https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/form-e-financial-statement-for-a-financial-order-matrimonial-causes-act-1973-for-financial-relief-after-an-overseas-divorce-etc-part-3-of-the-mat">Form E on gov.uk</a>). In mediation and solicitor negotiation, the same information is exchanged voluntarily, often using Form E as the template, and the summary of both parties' finances goes to the court with the consent order on Form D81.</p>

<p>Disclosure must be full and frank. Hiding assets is a false economy: a consent order obtained on misleading disclosure can be set aside later, court proceedings can reopen, and courts can draw adverse inferences, award costs against the non-discloser, and in serious cases treat concealment as contempt. If you suspect assets are being hidden, raise it early; there are formal mechanisms (questionnaires, third-party disclosure orders, in extreme cases freezing orders) to deal with it.</p>

<h2>The routes to a settlement</h2>

<p>There are four broad routes, in rising order of cost and conflict. Our <a href="/calculators/mediation-vs-solicitor-comparison">mediation vs solicitor comparison tool</a> puts realistic numbers on the first three.</p>

<h3>1. Direct agreement between yourselves</h3>

<p>Cheapest and quickest where the asset picture is simple and trust is intact. The essential step people miss: the agreement still needs to be turned into a consent order and approved by the court to be binding. A signed letter, a spreadsheet or a handshake binds nobody.</p>

<h3>2. Mediation</h3>

<p>A trained, neutral family mediator helps you reach agreement over a handful of sessions. It is confidential, far cheaper than parallel solicitors, and for most people who later apply to court a <strong>MIAM</strong> (mediation information and assessment meeting) is a required first step anyway. The government's <a href="https://www.gov.uk/guidance/family-mediation-voucher-scheme">Family Mediation Voucher Scheme</a> contributes up to £500 towards mediation where children are involved, while the scheme remains open. The mediator produces a memorandum of understanding, which a solicitor then converts into a consent order.</p>

<h3>3. Solicitor negotiation</h3>

<p>Each party instructs a solicitor and terms are agreed through correspondence and round-table meetings. Appropriate where there is an imbalance of power or financial knowledge, complex assets (businesses, trusts, significant pensions), or mediation has failed. Costs scale with conflict; see <a href="/blog/cost-of-divorce-uk">our full breakdown of divorce costs</a>.</p>

<h3>4. Court: financial remedy proceedings</h3>

<p>The route of last resort, started by filing Form A. The process runs through a First Appointment, a Financial Dispute Resolution (FDR) hearing where a judge gives an indication of a likely outcome, and, for the minority of cases that do not settle at or before FDR, a final hearing where a judge imposes an order. It is the slowest and most expensive route by a wide margin, and even here most cases settle before the end.</p>

<h2>Making it binding: consent orders and the clean break</h2>

<p>Whichever route you take, the settlement becomes legally enforceable only when a judge approves it as a <strong>financial consent order</strong>. The judge checks the order is broadly fair against the disclosed finances; approval is usually on paper, without a hearing. Where possible, the order should include <strong>clean break</strong> terms dismissing future claims in both directions, including claims against each other's estates on death. That is what actually ends the financial relationship.</p>

<h2>Timing traps to know about</h2>

<ul>
<li><strong>Do not apply for the final order of divorce before finances are resolved</strong> without understanding the consequences. Ending the marriage first can affect pension rights (for example widow's benefits) and, historically, positions on death before an order is made. Many solicitors advise delaying the final order until the financial order is approved.</li>
<li><strong>The remarriage trap.</strong> If you remarry without having applied for financial provision from the earlier marriage, you generally lose the right to apply for most orders against your former spouse. File first, remarry after.</li>
<li><strong>Tax windows.</strong> Since April 2023, separating spouses have an extended window for transferring assets between each other on a no gain, no loss basis for capital gains tax: broadly three tax years after the tax year of separation for ordinary transfers, and without time limit where the transfer is part of a formal divorce agreement or court order. Timing transfers badly can create avoidable tax; timing them well is free money. See gov.uk guidance on <a href="https://www.gov.uk/capital-gains-tax/married-couples-civil-partnership">capital gains tax for married couples</a>.</li>
<li><strong>Interim support exists.</strong> If you cannot meet outgoings while the settlement is negotiated, the court can order maintenance pending suit. Do not let short-term cash pressure force a bad long-term deal.</li>
</ul>

<h2>Special situations</h2>

<h3>Businesses and limited companies</h3>

<p>A business built during the marriage is a matrimonial asset, but courts prefer not to kill the goose. Typical outcomes leave the business with the spouse who runs it, offset by a larger share of other assets, a lump sum (sometimes staged), or maintenance. Valuation is usually the battleground: accounts, dividend capacity and liquidity all matter, and a single joint expert valuation is the norm in proceedings.</p>

<h3>Inheritances</h3>

<p>Received inheritances are non-matrimonial in principle but reachable where needs require. Future inheritances are almost never counted; they are uncertain until received.</p>

<h3>High asset and complex cases</h3>

<p>Trusts, share options, deferred bonuses and overseas assets all have established treatment in the case law, and capitalising maintenance uses actuarial tables (the Duxbury approach). These cases justify specialist advice early; the cost of getting the structure wrong dwarfs the fees.</p>

<h3>Scotland and Northern Ireland</h3>

<p>Everything above describes England and Wales. Scotland runs a materially different system under the Family Law (Scotland) Act 1985, with a defined "matrimonial property" window and a stronger presumption of equal sharing. Northern Ireland has its own legislation. Do not apply English guidance across borders.</p>
