  - question: "Is financial abuse a crime in the UK?"
    answer: "Economic abuse is legally recognised as a form of domestic abuse under section 1 of the Domestic Abuse Act 2021, which defines it as any behaviour that has a substantial adverse effect on your ability to acquire, use or maintain money or other property, or to obtain goods or services. Recognition in that Act is what opens up the civil and family law protections described in this guide. Separately, controlling or coercive behaviour in an intimate or family relationship is a criminal offence under section 76 of the Serious Crime Act 2015, and specific acts such as taking out credit in your name without consent can be fraud or theft. Whether to involve the police is a decision only you can make, and a specialist domestic abuse service can talk it through with you confidentially before you do anything. If you are in immediate danger, call 999, and if you cannot speak, press 55 when prompted from a mobile so the call is transferred to the police (gov.uk)."
  - question: "Do I have to sit in mediation with someone who financially controlled me?"
    answer: "No. Before most family court applications about money or children you are expected to attend a MIAM, a mediation information and assessment meeting, but gov.uk confirms you do not have to attend a MIAM in some circumstances, for example where there has been domestic abuse. A mediator will also assess suitability separately and should not proceed where one party cannot negotiate freely. Even where mediation is possible, shuttle mediation (each party in a separate room, with the mediator moving between them) and remote sessions are normal adjustments. Mediation can be genuinely useful for some survivors because it avoids court, but it only works where both people can say no safely. Our divorce mediation guide sets out how the process runs and what the exemptions look like in practice."
  - question: "Can I get legal aid if I have experienced financial abuse?"
    answer: "Possibly. Legal aid for family cases was largely withdrawn in 2013 but remains available where you have evidence that you or your children have experienced domestic abuse, and you must also pass the means test on income and capital. Gov.uk lists the bodies that can supply that evidence, including the courts, the police, a multi-agency risk assessment conference, social services, a health professional, a refuge manager, a domestic abuse support service, your bank, your employer, or the provider of any benefits you have received. Because economic abuse is domestic abuse under the 2021 Act, evidence about financial control counts. If you are refused on means because assets are held jointly with the other party, say so: the assessment can take account of assets you genuinely cannot access."
  - question: "My ex took out credit cards and a loan in my name during the marriage and I only found out when I separated. What can I do?"
    answer: "This is coerced debt and there is a route for it. Do not simply stop paying, because that damages your credit file without resolving anything. Get an appointment with a free debt advice service and explain the background. Trained money and debt advisers can complete the Economic Abuse Evidence Form, a single mechanism developed by Surviving Economic Abuse and Money Advice Plus that tells a creditor you have experienced economic abuse without you having to repeat your story to each firm. Surviving Economic Abuse reports that 70% of requests supported by the form received a decision within 6 weeks, and that the average decision time was 13 days with the form against 63 days without it. Creditors that have signed the UK Finance Financial Abuse Code have committed to handling these cases properly. Outcomes vary and can include writing off or suspending the debt, separating joint liabilities, or stopping contact through the other party. In parallel, the debt still has to be dealt with in the divorce settlement itself, because the court looks at the net asset position."
  - question: "Will the court give me a bigger share of the settlement because of the abuse?"
    answer: "Not automatically, and it is important to go in with realistic expectations. Section 25(2)(g) of the Matrimonial Causes Act 1973 requires the court to take conduct into account only where it would be inequitable to disregard it, which is a high threshold. In Tsvetkov v Khayrova [2023] EWFC 130 the court confirmed a two-stage approach: first the conduct must be established, then the court considers how it and its financial consequences affect the outcome, and conduct must be pleaded early rather than raised at the final hearing. Where abuse has identifiable financial consequences, for example money deliberately dissipated, earning capacity destroyed, or debts run up in your name, those consequences can be reflected, sometimes by adding back dissipated sums. Where the harm is real but not financially quantifiable, the court's response is more often through needs and through costs orders than through a larger percentage share."
  - question: "What if my ex refuses to disclose their finances and controlled all the money during the marriage?"
    answer: "That is a disclosure problem rather than a conduct problem, and the machinery for it is strong. Both parties owe the court a duty of full and frank disclosure, normally on Form E, and a court application on Form A costs £321. Where answers are evasive, questionnaires, third-party disclosure orders and adverse inferences follow, and a settlement obtained by material non-disclosure can be set aside years later. If you have never seen the accounts, that is a common starting point and not a fatal one, because the obligation to produce documents sits with the person who holds them. Our guide to hidden assets in divorce covers the enforcement tools in detail. What you should not do is log into their accounts or take their documents to build your own case, because unlawfully obtained material usually has to be returned and can damage your position."
  - question: "Can I be protected in court from the person who abused me?"
    answer: "Yes, and you should ask early. Family courts can make participation directions under Part 3A of the Family Procedure Rules, which include separate entrances and waiting areas, staggered arrival times, screens in the courtroom, and attendance by video link. The Domestic Abuse Act 2021 also made victims of domestic abuse automatically eligible for special measures in family proceedings. Since 21 July 2022, section 65 of that Act has prohibited a party from cross-examining an alleged victim in person, and where cross-examination is necessary the court can appoint a qualified legal representative to carry it out instead. Tell your solicitor or the court staff what you need as soon as proceedings start, ideally in writing, because arrangements such as screens and separate waiting areas need to be booked in advance."
  - question: "I cannot afford the court fees because my ex controls the accounts. Is there help?"
    answer: "Yes. The divorce or dissolution application fee is £628 (from 13 July 2026), the consent order fee is £62 and a contested financial order application on Form A costs £321. Help with Fees (form EX160) is assessed on the applicant's own income and disposable capital, which matters if the money you can actually reach is far less than the household's apparent wealth. The income thresholds are £1,420 for a single applicant and £2,130 for a couple, with a per-child uplift of £425 for a child aged 0 to 13 or £710 for a child aged 14 or over, and help tapers by 50p for each £1 of monthly income above the threshold, capped at the fee. Disposable-capital limits are £4,250 for a fee up to £1,420, or £16,000 if the applicant or their partner is aged 66 or over. If you have already paid, there is a 3-month refund window."

<p>Economic abuse has been a legally recognised form of domestic abuse since the <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2021/17/section/1/enacted">Domestic Abuse Act 2021</a>, which defines it in section 1 as any behaviour that has "a substantial adverse effect on B's ability to acquire, use or maintain money or other property, or obtain goods or services". That covers a partner who controls all the accounts, runs up credit in your name, sabotages your job, or withholds money for food and clothing.</p>

<p><strong>If you are in immediate danger, call 999. If you cannot speak safely, press 55 when prompted from a mobile and the call will be transferred to the police (gov.uk).</strong></p>

<p>This guide covers what that legal recognition actually changes in a divorce or separation in England and Wales: the mediation exemption, the legal aid evidence gateway, court protections, what conduct arguments can and cannot achieve in a financial settlement, and the coerced-debt route with creditors. Court fees are a common first obstacle when someone else controls the money, so run your own figures through the <a href="/calculators/help-with-fees-checker">Help with Fees checker</a> before assuming the <strong>£628</strong> divorce application fee is out of reach: it is assessed on the applicant's own income and disposable capital, not the household's apparent wealth.</p>

<h2>What economic abuse looks like in a divorce</h2>

<p>Economic abuse is broader than money going missing. It restricts, exploits or sabotages your access to economic resources, and it often continues after separation, which is why it surfaces during the financial settlement.</p>

<ul>
  <li><strong>Restricting</strong>: no access to the joint account, an allowance paid in cash, receipts demanded, bank cards withheld, no name on the deeds or the tenancy.</li>
  <li><strong>Exploiting</strong>: credit cards, loans or car finance taken out in your name, benefits paid into their account, your wages moved into an account you cannot use.</li>
  <li><strong>Sabotaging</strong>: interference with your job or study, damaging property you need, deliberately building arrears so a home is at risk, running down assets once separation starts.</li>

</ul>

<p>Anyone can experience it, of any gender and at any income level: a high household income does not mean the person on the receiving end has any money of their own. Marek, an anonymised example, earned well but had his salary paid into an account he did not control, and found two credit cards in his name only when he tried to rent a flat.</p>

<h2>Safety first: practical money steps before a divorce</h2>

<p>Nothing below is worth doing if it puts you at risk. Contact a specialist service before you change any financial arrangement: the period around separation is a recognised point of heightened danger, and a support worker can help you plan the order of events.</p>

<ul>
  <li>Use a device and an email address the other person cannot access, and check whether calls or messages appear on a shared bill or a linked family account.</li>
  <li>Keep copies of documents you are entitled to hold: your own payslips, bank statements for accounts in your name or joint accounts, your P60, benefit letters and tenancy or mortgage paperwork.</li>
  <li>Check your credit file with each of the three UK agencies, the fastest way to find credit taken out in your name and any financial associations with the other person.</li>
  <li>Open a bank account in your sole name at a bank where neither of you has an existing relationship, and ask about its domestic abuse support team.</li>
  <li>Do not confront the other person about what you have found, and do not announce that you are gathering paperwork. Take advice first.</li>
  <li>Do not log into their accounts or take their private documents. Unlawfully obtained material usually has to be returned and can damage your own position.</li>
</ul>

<p>The divorce itself is unaffected: since the Divorce, Dissolution and Separation Act 2020 there is no fault to allege, and the minimum timeline is 26 weeks (a 20-week reflection period, then a 6-week-and-1-day wait to the final order), which abuse does not shorten. <h2>Conduct arguments and what they achieve in a financial settlement</h2>

<p>This is where expectations most often part company with the law. Section 25(2)(g) of the Matrimonial Causes Act 1973 requires the court to take conduct into account only where it would be <strong>inequitable to disregard it</strong>, a deliberately high threshold. In <a href="https://www.bailii.org/ew/cases/EWFC/HCJ/2023/130.html">Tsvetkov v Khayrova [2023] EWFC 130</a> the court set out a two-stage approach: the conduct must first be established, and only then does the court consider how it and its financial consequences affect the outcome. It must be pleaded at the earliest opportunity, and a party can be barred from relying on it if the threshold plainly will not be met.</p>

<p>The practical distinction is between abuse with a measurable financial footprint and abuse without one. Money deliberately dissipated can be added back to the spender's side of the ledger, and debts run up in your name, an earning capacity destroyed by years out of work, or assets moved to defeat your claim all feed into the numbers directly, the last of these through section 37 of the same Act. Harm that is genuine but not quantifiable in pounds is usually reflected instead through needs (a larger share because your position is weaker) and through costs orders where the other side's behaviour has driven up the bill. Ongoing income needs are dealt with through maintenance rather than conduct: see our <a href="/blog/maintenance-and-support/spousal-maintenance-guide">spousal maintenance guide</a>.</p>

<h2>Coerced debt and the divorce financial settlement</h2>

<p>Debt taken out in your name under pressure is not simply yours to absorb. Trained money and debt advisers can complete the <a href="https://survivingeconomicabuse.org/economic-abuse-evidence-form/">Economic Abuse Evidence Form</a>, developed by Surviving Economic Abuse and Money Advice Plus, which tells a creditor you have experienced economic abuse without you having to repeat the account to each firm. Surviving Economic Abuse reports that 70% of requests supported by the form received a decision within 6 weeks, and an average decision time of 13 days against 63 days without it. Creditors signed up to the UK Finance Financial Abuse Code, 33 firms covering 49 brands as at 2026, have committed to trained staff and consistent handling.</p>

<p>Possible outcomes include a debt being written off or suspended, joint liabilities separated, interest stopped, or contact routed away from the other person. None is guaranteed, and the debts still have to be accounted for in the settlement, because the court works from the net asset position. Where you suspect money has been moved out of sight, disclosure is the route, not self-help: our guide to <a href="/blog/financial-settlements/hidden-assets-divorce">hidden assets in divorce</a> sets out questionnaires, third-party orders and adverse inferences. A consent order costs <strong>£62</strong> and a contested financial order application on Form A costs <strong>£321</strong>, and a clean break order matters more than usual here, because it closes off future financial claims and with them a route back into your life.</p>

<h2>Getting support during a divorce involving economic abuse</h2>

<p>Free, confidential help exists and using it early changes outcomes. Refuge runs the <strong>National Domestic Abuse Helpline on 0808 2000 247</strong>, free and open 24 hours a day. The Men's Advice Line is <strong>0808 801 0327</strong> and Galop supports LGBT+ victims on <strong>0800 999 5428</strong>. Live Fear Free in Wales is <strong>0808 80 10 100</strong>, Scotland's Domestic Abuse and Forced Marriage Helpline is <strong>0800 027 1234</strong>, and Northern Ireland's Domestic and Sexual Abuse Helpline is <strong>0808 802 1414</strong> (all per gov.uk). Surviving Economic Abuse is the UK charity dedicated to economic abuse and publishes practical guidance on accounts, debts and benefits.</p>

<p>One jurisdiction note: this guide describes England and Wales. Scotland has a different divorce process, its own protective orders, and a matrimonial-property regime valuing assets at the date of separation, so take Scottish advice if you live there. Northern Ireland differs again.</p>

<p>Economic abuse is domestic abuse in law, and that recognition gives you real procedural rights: exemption from mediation, an evidence route to legal aid, court protections that keep you away from the other party, and a structured way to raise coerced debt with creditors. It rarely changes the percentage split, because conduct only moves the numbers where it has left a financial mark.
