faqs:
  - question: "Is divorce law in Scotland really different, or is it just the paperwork?"
    answer: "It is genuinely different law, not a different set of forms. England and Wales operate under the Matrimonial Causes Act 1973, where section 25 gives the court wide discretion to meet needs from whatever resources exist, whenever they were acquired. Scotland operates under the Family Law (Scotland) Act 1985, which sets out five statutory principles, defines a fixed category of matrimonial property, freezes its value at the relevant date and starts from equal sharing. The practical consequences are large: a pension built up before the marriage is largely protected in Scotland but fully in scope in England and Wales, an inheritance is excluded in Scotland but can be drawn in to meet needs south of the border, and indefinite spousal maintenance is normal in some England and Wales cases but has no real equivalent in Scotland. The divorce process itself also differs, with no-fault divorce under the Divorce, Dissolution and Separation Act 2020 applying only to England and Wales."
  - question: "What exactly is the relevant date, and why does it matter so much?"
    answer: "Section 10(3) of the Family Law (Scotland) Act 1985 defines the relevant date as the earlier of the date the parties ceased to cohabit and the date the divorce summons was served. Every asset and debt in the sharing exercise is identified and valued as at that single day. It matters because everything after it usually falls outside the pot: a pay rise, a bonus, further pension accrual, investment growth, and equally a debt run up after separation. Two couples with identical finances today can end up with very different settlements simply because one separated in 2022 and the other in 2025. Disputes about the exact date are common where a couple continued living under one roof after the marriage ended, and the date is a question of fact about whether cohabitation as spouses truly ceased, not about who moved out."
  - question: "My wife inherited £150,000 from her mother during our marriage and it is sitting in a savings account. Do I get half?"
    answer: "Almost certainly not. Section 10(4) excludes property acquired by gift or succession from a third party, so an inheritance is not matrimonial property in Scotland however long the marriage lasted, which is a sharper rule than the England and Wales approach where inherited money can be drawn into the pot to meet needs. Two qualifications matter in practice. First, if the inheritance was converted into something else during the marriage, for example used to buy a jointly titled house or mixed into a joint account and spent on family assets, the resulting asset can lose its protected character and arguments about tracing become important. Second, the excluded money still exists as a resource, so it can influence a claim under principle (b) about economic advantage and disadvantage, or a hardship claim under principle (e). The safe assumption is that a ring-fenced, untouched inheritance stays with the person who inherited it."
  - question: "How is a pension shared in a Scottish divorce?"
    answer: "The mechanism is the same as the rest of the United Kingdom, a pension sharing order under the Welfare Reform and Pensions Act 1999, with the 4-month implementation period once the order takes effect. What differs is the valuation. In Scotland the scheme value is taken at the relevant date, not at the date of the hearing, and then apportioned under regulation 4 of the Divorce etc. (Pensions) (Scotland) Regulations 2000 using the formula A multiplied by B divided by C, where A is the value at the relevant date, B is the period of scheme membership that fell within the marriage and C is total scheme membership before the relevant date. Only the resulting matrimonial slice is shared. Someone who joined a scheme at 22 and married at 35 therefore keeps the pre-marriage portion outright, which can be a very large protection compared with the England and Wales position where the whole fund is potentially in scope."
  - question: "Can I get long-term spousal maintenance in Scotland?"
    answer: "Not in the way that is possible in England and Wales. Scots law prefers a clean break achieved through capital, and section 13(2) allows a periodical allowance only where an order for a capital sum or transfer of property would be inappropriate or insufficient. Where an allowance is made under principle (d), for someone who was substantially dependent on the other spouse's financial support, section 9(1)(d) caps it at not more than three years from the date of the decree of divorce, on the basis that the award is there to fund adjustment rather than to replace income indefinitely. A longer award is only available under principle (e), where a spouse would otherwise suffer serious financial hardship, and those awards are unusual and closely evidenced. Aliment, which is support payable while the marriage still subsists, is a separate matter and stops on divorce."
  - question: "What if we agree everything ourselves? Do we still need a court order in Scotland?"
    answer: "No, and this is one of the most useful practical differences. Most Scottish financial settlements are recorded in a minute of agreement drawn up by solicitors and registered in the Books of Council and Session, which makes it enforceable without going near a judge. There is no Scottish equivalent of the England and Wales requirement to have a consent order approved by the court for the deal to bind, and no judicial scrutiny of fairness before registration, which cuts cost and delay but puts more weight on getting proper disclosure and independent advice first. If you also need a pension sharing order, note that the pension scheme will require either a qualifying agreement or a court order, so tell your solicitor early. Where agreement is not possible, financial claims are made within an ordinary divorce action in the sheriff court or the Court of Session."
  - question: "We separated three years ago and never sorted the money. Is it too late?"
    answer: "The timing rules are strict in a way that catches people out. Financial provision claims under the Family Law (Scotland) Act 1985 must be made in the divorce action itself, so once the decree of divorce is granted the door generally closes and there is no later application of the kind sometimes possible elsewhere. The relevant date, meanwhile, remains fixed at the point you separated three years ago, so the pot is valued as it stood then even though the divorce is happening now. That can cut either way: a house that has risen sharply since separation is shared at its lower relevant-date value, while a pension that has grown is shared on the smaller figure too. Get advice before the divorce action concludes rather than after, and be careful about a simplified application, which cannot be used at all where there are financial matters to resolve."
  - question: "Do the UK-wide tax rules still apply if I am divorcing in Scotland?"
    answer: "The tax rules that are set at United Kingdom level apply in Scotland in the same way. Capital gains tax is one of them, so transfers between separating spouses or civil partners are on a no gain, no loss basis for up to 3 tax years after the tax year of separation, or without limit where the transfer is made under a formal separation agreement or court order, following Finance Act 2023 and TCGA 1992 section 58. UK residential property gains must still be reported and paid within 60 days of completion, and the annual exempt amount for 2026/27 is £3,000. Child maintenance is also UK-wide, so the Child Maintenance Service formula and its Collect and Pay charges of 20% for the paying parent and 4% for the receiving parent work identically. The main devolved difference is property transaction tax: stamp duty land tax does not apply in Scotland, where land and buildings transaction tax administered by Revenue Scotland applies instead, with its own reliefs and thresholds."

<p>The rest of this site covers England and Wales. This guide covers Scotland, which runs a genuinely separate financial regime under the <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1985/37/contents">Family Law (Scotland) Act 1985</a>, and almost none of the England and Wales rules carry across the border. The single most important difference is the <strong>relevant date</strong>: Scots law freezes the asset pot on the day the couple stopped living together, or the day the divorce summons was served if that came first, and value built up after that date is usually not shared at all.</p>

<p>What follows covers how Scotland defines matrimonial property, why fair sharing normally means an equal split, how pensions are apportioned, why spousal maintenance is capped at three years, and what the process costs. Every figure here is Scottish: the England and Wales fees quoted elsewhere on this site, including the <strong>£628</strong> divorce application fee from 13 July 2026 and the <strong>£62</strong> consent order fee, do not apply in Scotland.</p>

<h2>How Scots law defines matrimonial property in a divorce</h2>

<p>England and Wales start from all the resources either spouse has and ask what each party needs. Scotland starts from a defined category. Under section 10(4) and (5), matrimonial property means property belonging to either spouse that was acquired between the date of the marriage and the relevant date, plus any house or furnishings bought before the marriage for use as a family home.</p>

<p>Two exclusions do most of the work:</p>

<ul>
  <li><strong>Gifts and inheritances from third parties are excluded</strong>, however long the marriage lasted, provided they have not been converted into a jointly held asset.</li>
  <li><strong>Pre-marriage acquisitions are excluded</strong>, with the family home exception above. A flat one spouse bought at 26 and kept as a rental stays out of the pot.</li>
</ul>

<p>Debts follow the same logic, so it is the <em>net</em> value of matrimonial property that is shared. A credit card balance run up after the relevant date is not deducted from the pot.</p>

<h2>The relevant date and why it drives a Scottish financial settlement</h2>

<p>Section 10(3) defines the relevant date as the earlier of the date the parties ceased to cohabit and the date of service of the divorce summons. Everything in the sharing exercise is identified and valued as at that one day, which is why Scottish solicitors ask about the separation date before they ask about anything else.</p>

<p>The consequences are blunt. A bonus paid three months after separation is not shared, nor are pension contributions made since. Equally, if the family home has risen by £70,000 since the relevant date, that growth normally belongs to whoever holds the title, a very different outcome from England and Wales, where assets are valued as at the date of the hearing. Disputes about the date are common where a couple carried on living in the same house after the marriage was over, and the test is whether cohabitation as spouses truly ceased, not who physically left.</p>

<h2>Fair sharing: how equal division works in a Scottish divorce</h2>

<p>Section 10(1) says the net value is shared fairly when it is shared <strong>equally</strong>, or in such other proportions as are justified by special circumstances. Equal is the default and the burden sits on the person arguing for something else. Special circumstances include the source of funds used to buy an asset, the terms of any agreement between the parties, destruction or dissipation of property, and the nature and use of assets such as a business one spouse runs.</p>

<p>Four other principles in section 9(1) sit alongside sharing: fair account of economic advantage gained and disadvantage suffered, fair sharing of the economic burden of caring for a child under 16 after divorce, a limited adjustment award for a dependent spouse, and relief from serious financial hardship.</p>

<h2>Pension sharing in Scotland: apportionment at the relevant date</h2>

<p>The order itself is the same UK-wide instrument, a pension sharing order under the Welfare Reform and Pensions Act 1999, available for divorces started on or after 1 December 2000 and carrying a 4-month implementation period once the order takes effect. Only the protected-payment element of the new state pension can be shared. What differs in Scotland is the number the order is applied to.</p>

<p>Regulation 4 of the Divorce etc. (Pensions) (Scotland) Regulations 2000 apportions the fund as <strong>A multiplied by B divided by C</strong>, where A is the value at the relevant date, B is the period of scheme membership falling within the marriage before the relevant date, and C is total scheme membership before the relevant date.</p>

<p>Take Morag, who joined her scheme at 24 and separated at 48. Her cash equivalent value at the relevant date was <strong>£180,000</strong> across 24 years of membership, of which <strong>15 years</strong> fell within the marriage. The matrimonial slice is £180,000 multiplied by 15 divided by 24, which is <strong>£112,500</strong>. An equal share of that slice is <strong>£56,250</strong>, delivered as a pension sharing order for <strong>31.25%</strong> of the total fund. In England and Wales the whole £180,000, plus any growth since separation, would have been the starting figure.</p>

<p>The mechanics of sharing, offsetting and attachment are identical either side of the border, so our <a href="/blog/pensions-and-divorce/pensions-and-divorce">pensions and divorce guide</a>, the <a href="/blog/pensions-and-divorce/pension-sharing-vs-offsetting">sharing versus offsetting</a> comparison and the <a href="/pension-sharing">pension sharing hub</a> all apply, with the Scottish valuation rules substituted.</p>

<h2>Spousal maintenance in Scotland: the three-year periodical allowance</h2>

<p>Scots law is built around a clean break funded by capital. Section 13(2) lets a court award a periodical allowance only where a capital sum or property transfer would be inappropriate or insufficient to meet the case. Where an award is made under principle (d), for a spouse who was dependent to a substantial degree on the other's financial support, section 9(1)(d) caps it at <strong>not more than three years</strong> from the date of the decree of divorce. The purpose is adjustment, not income replacement.</p>

<p>Longer awards exist only under principle (e), relief from serious financial hardship over a reasonable period, and they are rare and heavily evidenced. Aliment, the support one spouse owes the other while the marriage subsists, ends at divorce. Child maintenance is unaffected: the Child Maintenance Service operates UK-wide on basic rates of 12% for one child, 16% for two and 19% for three or more of gross weekly income, with Collect and Pay adding 20% for the paying parent and deducting 4% from the receiving parent.</p>

<h2>Process and cost: what a Scottish divorce actually involves</h2>

<p>There is no no-fault divorce in Scotland. The Divorce, Dissolution and Separation Act 2020, with its 20-week reflection period and 26-week minimum timeline, applies only to England and Wales. Scottish grounds are one year of separation with the other party's consent, two years without consent, or irretrievable breakdown established by behaviour or adultery.</p>

<p>The <strong>simplified procedure</strong> is a do-it-yourself route usable only where every condition is met: separation of one year with consent or two years without, no children of the marriage under 16, <strong>no financial matters to sort out</strong>, no other proceedings under way, and the residence requirements satisfied. That third condition rules it out for most readers of this guide. Per the Sheriff Court Fees Order 2026, in force 1 April 2026, the fee is <strong>£156</strong> for a simplified application and <strong>£191</strong> for an initial writ in an ordinary divorce action, both inclusive of the extract decree, with sheriff officer service charged on top.</p>

<p>Scotland's regime is narrower and more predictable than the discretion applied in England and Wales, and the relevant date decides more than any other single fact in the case. Predictability cuts both ways: a spouse who gave up a career to raise children has fewer routes to a long-term income than they would south of the border, and the answer usually has to be found in capital. Cross-border cases, where one spouse is in Scotland and the other in England, turn on which court is seised first.
