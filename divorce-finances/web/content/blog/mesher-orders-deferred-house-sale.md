faqs:
  - question: "What is a Mesher order in a divorce?"
    answer: "A Mesher order is a property adjustment order made by the family court in England and Wales that postpones the sale of the family home until a defined trigger event. The home is held in the joint names of both ex-spouses on a trust of land, one of them (usually the parent with day-to-day care of the children) has the right to live there, and the order records what percentage of the net proceeds each will receive when the house is eventually sold. It takes its name from the 1970s case of Mesher v Mesher. The point of the order is to stop children being uprooted by an immediate sale while still preserving the absent spouse's share of the equity for the future."
  - question: "What are the usual trigger events in a Mesher order?"
    answer: "The order itself lists the triggers, and the first one to occur ends the deferral and brings the house to sale. The most common trigger is the youngest child reaching 18 or finishing full-time secondary education, whichever is later. Orders also routinely include the occupying spouse remarrying, cohabiting with a new partner for a set period (six or twelve months is typical), voluntarily choosing to sell, or dying. Some orders add a fixed long-stop date. Because the wording of the triggers controls when the other spouse finally gets their money, it is one of the most heavily negotiated parts of the order, and vague drafting here is a common source of later disputes."
  - question: "Who pays the mortgage, insurance and repairs while a Mesher order runs?"
    answer: "Whatever the order says, so it must say something. Typically the occupying spouse pays the mortgage instalments, buildings insurance and day-to-day upkeep, since they have the benefit of living there. But both names usually stay on the mortgage, which means the lender can pursue either party if payments are missed, and the departing spouse's borrowing capacity for a new home is reduced because the existing loan counts against them. Well-drafted orders deal with who pays what, what happens if payments are missed, whether capital repayments change the split of proceeds, and who can insist on repairs before sale. Skimping on this detail is a false economy."
  - question: "I moved out under a Mesher order 8 years ago and the house sells next year. Will I pay capital gains tax?"
    answer: "Quite possibly, and this is the classic Mesher trap. Your share of the sale is a disposal for CGT. Private residence relief covers the years you actually lived there plus your final 9 months of ownership, but the remaining years since you moved out are potentially taxable. On a long deferral that unrelieved slice can be substantial, taxed at 18 or 24 percent after the £3,000 annual exempt amount, with the sale reportable to HMRC within 60 days if tax is due. You may be able to elect under TCGA 1992 s.225B to treat the house as your main residence for the absent years, which can wipe out the gain, but only if it remained your ex's main home throughout, and the same years then lose relief on any home you bought after leaving. Get the two outcomes calculated side by side before the sale, not after."
  - question: "My ex wants to keep the house until our youngest is 18, but I need a deposit for my own place now. What are my options?"
    answer: "A Mesher order is not the only route, and your need for housing counts in the settlement just as much as your ex's. Options include selling now and splitting the proceeds so you both rehouse; your ex buying out your share, perhaps with a larger mortgage or by offsetting against another asset such as pension rights; a smaller immediate lump sum to you combined with a reduced deferred share; or a deferred charge, where the house is transferred to your ex and you take a charge over it for a fixed percentage, payable on similar triggers. Courts weigh both parties' housing needs, so a deferral that leaves you unable to buy at all is a genuine argument against a Mesher order, not just a preference."
  - question: "What is the difference between a Mesher order and a Martin order?"
    answer: "Both defer the sale of the family home, but the trigger structure is different. A Mesher order is built around children: the deferral typically ends when the youngest turns 18 or finishes secondary education. A Martin order, named after Martin v Martin, gives the occupying spouse the right to stay for life or until they remarry, cohabit or choose to leave, with no child-related trigger at all. Martin orders are rarer and tend to appear where there are no dependent children, the occupying spouse cannot rehouse themselves, and the other spouse has no pressing need for the capital because they are already securely housed. The tax mechanics on eventual sale are similar, so the absent owner's CGT position needs checking under either order."
  - question: "Can a Mesher order be changed after the divorce?"
    answer: "Not easily. A Mesher order forms part of a final financial settlement, and the capital elements of such orders are intended to be final. The court will not simply rewrite the percentages or the triggers because one party's circumstances change. Orders usually contain some flexibility on timing, for example allowing sale by agreement or giving the occupier a right to buy out the other's share at market value, and an order for sale can be sought if the occupier obstructs a sale after a trigger has occurred. But you should negotiate on the basis that the deal is permanent, which is why the trigger list and the percentage split deserve so much attention before you agree them."
  - question: "Does a retained Mesher interest trigger the stamp duty surcharge when I buy my next home?"
    answer: "Usually not, thanks to a specific relief. Buying an additional dwelling normally attracts the 5 percent SDLT surcharge on the whole price if you already hold an interest in another property. However, Schedule 4ZA to the Finance Act 2003 disregards an interest you have retained in your former home under a property adjustment order where your ex-spouse continues to live there as their main residence. That means the retained Mesher interest should not, by itself, add the surcharge to your onward purchase. The conditions are precise, so flag the order to your conveyancer, and remember the relief solves the SDLT problem only: the CGT position on your retained share is a separate question entirely."

<p>A Mesher order postpones the sale of the family home after a divorce until a trigger event, most commonly the youngest child turning 18 or finishing full-time education. The house stays in both names on a trust of land, one ex-spouse lives in it, and the other waits for their share of the equity, sometimes for a decade or more. In England and Wales the court's power to make one comes from <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1973/18/section/24">section 24 of the Matrimonial Causes Act 1973</a>.</p>

<p>Deferring the sale solves a housing problem today and creates a tax and liquidity problem tomorrow. The spouse who moves out keeps a paper share of the house but loses access to the money, and their capital gains tax relief quietly stops building up from the day they leave. This guide covers the triggers, who wins and loses, the CGT mechanics with a worked example, and the alternatives.</p>

<p>Because a Mesher order is one piece of a wider deal, it only makes sense against the whole asset picture. Before you accept equity locked up for ten years, run the <a href="/calculators/settlement-range-estimator">settlement range estimator</a> to see what a court-typical division of all your assets looks like, so you know exactly what you would be trading for the deferral.</p>

<h2>What a Mesher order does to the family home</h2>

<p>Under a Mesher order the family home is held by both ex-spouses as trustees on a trust of land. The order names who may occupy it, lists the trigger events, and fixes the percentage of net sale proceeds each party will receive when the house is finally sold. The percentages need not be equal: a 60/40 or 70/30 split in favour of the occupying parent is common where their mortgage capacity is weaker.</p>

<p>The order should also allocate the running costs: who pays the mortgage, insurance and repairs, and whether capital the occupier pays off the loan adjusts the final split. Both names normally stay on the mortgage, so both stay liable to the lender throughout.</p>

<h2>Trigger events that end a deferred divorce house sale</h2>

<p>The trigger list is the heart of the order. The first event to occur ends the deferral and the house goes on the market (or the occupier buys the other out). Typical triggers are:</p>

<ul>
  <li><strong>The youngest child reaching 18</strong>, or finishing full-time secondary education, whichever is later.</li>
  <li><strong>The occupying spouse remarrying</strong>, or cohabiting with a new partner for a defined period, commonly 6 to 12 months.</li>
  <li><strong>The occupier choosing to sell</strong> or ceasing to live in the property as their main home.</li>
  <li><strong>The occupier's death.</strong></li>
  <li><strong>A fixed long-stop date</strong>, in some orders, so the deferral cannot run indefinitely.</li>
</ul>

<p>Drafting matters enormously here. A vague cohabitation clause is an invitation to litigate, and a missing long-stop can leave the absent spouse waiting on events they cannot influence. If you settle by agreement, the mechanics are those of a <a href="/blog/process-and-costs/consent-orders-and-clean-break-orders">consent order</a>, though a Mesher arrangement is by definition not a full clean break on capital.</p>

<h2>Mesher order pros and cons in a financial settlement</h2>

<p>For the occupying parent, the case is straightforward: the children stay in their home and schools, there is no forced sale, and time is bought to rebuild mortgage capacity. The costs sit mostly on the other side of the table. For the departing spouse, the equity is frozen: they cannot spend it or use it as a deposit, yet the joint mortgage still counts against their affordability for a new home. Both parties stay financially entangled for years, and the eventual sale price is a gamble. The quiet second trap is that the occupier faces a move anyway when the trigger fires, often in their 50s or 60s with limited mortgage options. How these trade-offs are weighed against the rest of the assets is covered in our <a href="/blog/financial-settlements/divorce-financial-settlement-guide">divorce financial settlement guide</a>.</p>

<h2>The CGT trap when one spouse leaves the family home</h2>

<p>Here is where deferral gets expensive, and where precision matters, because two different tax rules are in play and only one of them protects you.</p>

<p><strong>Rule one: transfers between the spouses are protected.</strong> Since the Finance (No. 2) Act 2023 reforms, transfers of assets between separating spouses are treated as no gain no loss for up to 3 tax years after the tax year of separation, and with <strong>no time limit at all</strong> where the transfer is made under a court order (<a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1992/12/section/58">TCGA 1992 s.58</a>). So restructuring ownership when the Mesher order is made, for example moving from joint tenants to defined trust shares, does not itself trigger tax.</p>

<p><strong>Rule two: the eventual sale is not protected.</strong> When the trigger fires and the house is sold to a buyer, each ex-spouse makes a normal disposal of their share. The occupier is usually fully covered by private residence relief (PRR) because they lived there throughout. The departing spouse is not: their PRR covers the years they actually occupied plus a final 9 months of ownership, and every further year of the deferral is potentially taxable, per HMRC's <a href="https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/husband-and-wife-civil-partners-divorce-dissolution-and-separation-hs281-self-assessment-helpsheet">helpsheet HS281</a>.</p>

<h3>Worked example: the cost of ten absent years</h3>

<p>Rob and Amy bought their home in 2016 for £200,000. They divorce in 2026, Rob moves out, and a Mesher order defers sale until their youngest turns 18 in 2036, when the house sells for £500,000. The total gain is £300,000; Rob's half share is £150,000. He occupied for 120 of his 240 months of ownership, plus the final 9 months exempt, so 111 of 240 months are unrelieved. That makes £69,375 of his gain taxable. After the £3,000 annual exempt amount, £66,375 is taxed at 24 percent as a higher-rate taxpayer: a bill of about <strong>£15,930</strong>, reportable and payable within <a href="https://www.gov.uk/report-and-pay-your-capital-gains-tax">60 days of completion</a>. Amy pays nothing.</p>

<p><strong>The relief, and its price.</strong> Rob may be able to elect under <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1992/12/section/225B">TCGA 1992 s.225B</a> to be treated as still occupying the house while it remained Amy's main residence, which would relieve the whole gain. Since April 2023 this election also covers a spouse who retains an interest and receives proceeds on a deferred sale, exactly the Mesher situation. The catch: those 111 months can only be relieved once. If Rob bought his own flat in 2027, electing for the old house strips PRR from the same period of the flat's ownership. For a departing spouse who rents throughout, the election is close to free money; for one who buys again quickly, it can move the tax bill rather than remove it. Run the numbers both ways before the trigger, not at sale.</p>

<p>One piece of good news on the buying side: a retained Mesher interest is disregarded for the 5 percent SDLT additional-dwelling surcharge on the departing spouse's next purchase, where the order leaves the ex living in the former home as their main residence (<a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2003/14/schedule/4ZA">FA 2003, Schedule 4ZA</a>).</p>

<h2>Martin orders: the other deferred sale order in a divorce</h2>

<p>A Martin order also defers sale, but with no child-driven trigger: the occupying spouse may stay for life, or until they remarry, cohabit or leave. It suits the narrower case where there are no dependent children, the occupier cannot rehouse from their share, and the other spouse is already securely housed.</p>

<h2>Alternatives to a Mesher order in a divorce settlement</h2>

<p>Because deferral carries real costs for both parties, it is worth testing the alternatives before agreeing one:</p>

<ul>
  <li><strong>Sale and division now.</strong> Both parties take their equity, rehouse, and achieve a genuine capital clean break.</li>
  <li><strong>Buyout and transfer of equity.</strong> One spouse keeps the home and pays the other out, by remortgage or asset offset; under a court order the transfer itself is no gain no loss with no time limit. See our guide to a <a href="/blog/the-family-home/house-buyout-transfer-of-equity-divorce">house buyout and transfer of equity</a>.</li>
  <li><strong>Offsetting against the pension.</strong> The occupier keeps more of the house; the other keeps more of the pension, as explained in <a href="/blog/pensions-and-divorce/pension-sharing-vs-offsetting">pension sharing versus offsetting</a>.</li>
  <li><strong>A deferred charge.</strong> The house is transferred outright to the occupier and the departing spouse takes a charge for a fixed percentage of value, payable on Mesher-style triggers. Ownership is cleaner, and since 2023 the deferred proceeds can carry the same PRR treatment that applied to the original transfer, often a better tax outcome than a retained trust share.</li>
</ul>

<h2>Deciding whether to defer the family home sale</h2>

<p>A Mesher order is a genuine solution to a genuine problem: children need a home now, and equity can wait. But it is a settlement with a long tail. The departing spouse should price the deferral properly, including the CGT exposure and the mortgage drag, before agreeing the percentages and triggers; the occupier should plan for the trigger from day one.
