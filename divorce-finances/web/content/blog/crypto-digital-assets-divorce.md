faqs:
  - question: "Do I have to disclose cryptocurrency on Form E?"
    answer: "Yes. Form E requires full and frank disclosure of everything you own, and cryptoassets are property like any other: exchange balances, coins in private wallets, NFTs, staking positions and interests held through platforms all count. Crypto normally goes in the section of Form E covering other assets, with recent statements or wallet records attached. The duty is ongoing, so if values change materially or you acquire or dispose of coins during proceedings, you must update your disclosure. Leaving crypto off Form E is not a grey area: the statement is signed with a statement of truth, and deliberate non-disclosure can amount to contempt of court, lead to adverse costs orders, and allow a settlement to be reopened later."
  - question: "What happens if my ex hides bitcoin in our divorce?"
    answer: "If concealment comes to light during proceedings, the court can order specific disclosure, ask questionnaires, and ultimately draw adverse inferences, meaning it assumes the hidden assets exist and adjusts the award against the non-discloser. Courts can also penalise dishonesty in costs. If the concealment only surfaces after a final order, the settlement can be set aside for fraudulent non-disclosure: the Supreme Court confirmed in Sharland and Gohil in 2015 that an agreement procured by fraud does not stand. Practically, the trail that exposes hidden bitcoin is usually mundane: bank statements showing transfers to exchanges, tax return entries, emails from platforms, or lifestyle spending that does not match the disclosed picture."
  - question: "My husband moved £30,000 into crypto just before we separated. Can the court do anything?"
    answer: "Yes, in two ways. First, the coins themselves remain matrimonial assets wherever the money went, so the £30,000 of value, at its current price, still belongs in the pot and must be disclosed. Second, if the transfer was an attempt to put money beyond your reach, or the coins were then dissipated through reckless trading, you can ask the court to add back the lost value, treating your husband as still having it when the split is calculated. Timing evidence matters here: bank statements showing the transfer date, the exchange it went to, and what happened next. If you can show a deliberate attempt to defeat your claim, the court also has power under section 37 of the Matrimonial Causes Act 1973 to set aside certain transactions."
  - question: "How do you value crypto in a divorce when the price keeps moving?"
    answer: "There is no fixed statutory valuation date. In practice the court works with values that are as close as possible to the date of the agreement or hearing, which is why volatile holdings need repeated dated snapshots rather than one number from months ago. Sensible approaches include valuing on an agreed date shortly before the consent order is drafted, expressing the split as a percentage of the holding rather than a fixed pound figure so both people share the movement, or selling the coins and dividing sale proceeds so the value is crystallised. What does not work is negotiating around a stale figure: a 20 percent move on a £40,000 holding is £8,000, which can be more than the entire cost of the divorce process."
  - question: "We separated in June 2024 but have not yet agreed a settlement. Can we still transfer coins between us without CGT?"
    answer: "Almost certainly yes, on two separate grounds. Separating in June 2024 puts your tax year of separation at 2024/25, and the no-gain-no-loss window introduced by the Finance Act 2023 runs for 3 tax years after that year, so transfers between you are automatically free of immediate CGT until 5 April 2028. Beyond that date, any transfer made under a formal court order, including an ordinary consent order, is no gain no loss with no time limit at all. Remember that no gain no loss defers tax rather than erasing it: whoever keeps the coins inherits the original purchase cost and will face the full historic gain when they eventually sell, so price that latent tax into the deal."
  - question: "Will HMRC data show crypto my spouse never told me about?"
    answer: "Not directly, because HMRC does not hand CARF data to divorcing spouses. Its effect is indirect but real. From 1 January 2026, exchanges serving UK customers must collect verified user details and transaction data and report them to HMRC, with the first reports covering the 2026 year. That makes it far riskier for a spouse to hold undeclared exchange balances, because the same data that reaches HMRC contradicts a false Form E, and tax records are disclosable in financial proceedings. In practice your route to the information is through the court: questionnaires, orders for specific disclosure of exchange statements and wallet records, and adverse inferences if answers do not add up."
  - question: "Is it better to sell the crypto or transfer it as part of the settlement?"
    answer: "It depends on who wants the exposure and where the tax falls. Selling crystallises the value: the seller pays CGT on gains above the £3,000 annual exempt amount at 18 or 24 percent, and what is divided is a clean cash figure. Transferring coins between spouses inside the no-gain-no-loss window triggers no immediate tax, but the recipient takes on the original base cost and therefore the whole latent gain. A spouse who receives £50,000 of coins bought for £10,000 is really receiving about £41,000 after latent higher-rate tax. If one of you wants to keep the holding and believes in it, a transfer with a price adjustment for latent CGT is often the fair route; if neither of you wants the volatility, selling and splitting proceeds is simpler."
  - question: "Are NFTs, staking rewards and play-to-earn balances included too?"
    answer: "Yes. The disclosure duty covers all property with realisable value, however novel the wrapper. NFTs should be listed with acquisition cost and a realistic current estimate, which may need specialist input because thin markets make headline floor prices unreliable. Staking positions should be disclosed including locked balances and accrued rewards, and staking rewards also have income tax consequences for the holder that a settlement should not ignore. In-game and platform balances count where they can be converted to money. The test is simple: if it can be sold or exchanged for value, it belongs on Form E, and pretending a category of digital asset is too obscure to mention is treated as non-disclosure, not as a technicality."

<p>Since <strong>1 January 2026</strong>, crypto exchanges serving UK customers have been required to collect verified user and transaction data under the <a href="https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/cryptoasset-reporting-framework/implementation-of-the-cryptoasset-reporting-framework-carf">Cryptoasset Reporting Framework (CARF)</a> and report it to HMRC, so a quietly held exchange account is far more visible than most people assume. In a divorce in England and Wales, cryptocurrency is part of the matrimonial pot and must be disclosed in full on Form E, exactly like a bank account, a house or a pension.</p>

<p>Crypto causes two specific problems in a divorce that ordinary assets do not: prices can move 20 percent between disclosure and a final order, and moving coins between spouses is a capital gains tax event unless it lands inside a specific window. Both are solvable with the right sequence of steps, set out below.</p>

<p>Because a volatile holding can shift the whole shape of a deal, it is worth running the <a href="/calculators/settlement-range-estimator">settlement range estimator</a> with your crypto valued at today's prices, then running it again if the market moves sharply before you agree. Seeing how a £10,000 swing changes your likely range is the fastest way to understand why valuation dates matter.</p>

<h2>Why crypto counts in your divorce financial settlement</h2>

<p>The court's job under <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1973/18/section/25">section 25 of the Matrimonial Causes Act 1973</a> is to divide all the resources of the marriage fairly, and "all" includes every digital asset with realisable value: coins on exchanges, coins in self-custody wallets, NFTs, staking positions and rewards, and balances held through investment platforms. None of it sits outside the pot because it is novel or hard to price. Where it fits alongside the home, pensions and savings is covered in our <a href="/blog/financial-settlements/divorce-financial-settlement-guide">divorce financial settlement guide</a>.</p>

<p>Treating crypto as a special category is the first mistake people make, in both directions. Holders sometimes assume it is invisible; the other spouse sometimes assumes it is untouchable. Neither is true.</p>

<h2>Disclosing crypto on Form E in your divorce</h2>

<p>Financial disclosure runs through <a href="https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/form-e-financial-statement-for-a-financial-order">Form E</a>, signed with a statement of truth, and the duty is full, frank and ongoing. Crypto belongs in the section covering other assets, with supporting evidence attached: exchange statements, wallet addresses and dated valuations. If holdings change materially during proceedings, the disclosure must be updated. Our <a href="/blog/process-and-costs/form-e-financial-disclosure-guide">Form E financial disclosure guide</a> walks through the form section by section.</p>

<p>The consequences of hiding coins are severe and well established:</p>

<ul>
  <li><strong>Adverse inferences:</strong> if the court believes assets exist but are concealed, it can assume their value and adjust the award against the non-discloser.</li>
  <li><strong>Costs orders:</strong> dishonest disclosure is routinely punished in costs.</li>
  <li><strong>Set aside:</strong> a settlement obtained by fraudulent non-disclosure can be reopened, as the Supreme Court confirmed in Sharland and Gohil in 2015.</li>
  <li><strong>Contempt:</strong> lying on a statement of truth is contempt of court.</li>
</ul>

<h2>Valuing volatile crypto for a financial settlement</h2>

<p>A pension valuation is good for months. A crypto valuation can be stale in a week. There is no single statutory valuation date, and the court works with figures as close as possible to the hearing or agreement, so a number lifted from a Form E completed in the spring can misstate the pot badly by autumn. <p>Three practical fixes deal with this. First, keep valuations live: agree that both sides will provide dated screenshots or exchange statements at set intervals, and revalue immediately before the consent order is drafted. Second, consider expressing the crypto split as a percentage of the holding rather than a fixed pound figure, so both of you share rises and falls between agreement and implementation. Third, where neither spouse wants the volatility, sell and divide the proceeds so the value is crystallised, accepting the CGT consequences below. Whichever route you take, the deal only binds once it is sealed in a court order, which is why the <a href="/blog/process-and-costs/consent-orders-and-clean-break-orders">consent order and clean break</a> step matters even more with volatile assets.</p>

<h2>Tracing hidden wallets in divorce proceedings</h2>

<p>Crypto's reputation for anonymity is overstated. Most holdings are bought with ordinary money through regulated exchanges, and that leaves a trail:</p>

<ul>
  <li><strong>Bank and card statements</strong> show transfers to exchanges, often years back. A £500 monthly payment to a trading platform that produces no disclosed balance is a question that demands an answer.</li>
  <li><strong>Tax returns</strong> can show crypto gains or losses already reported to HMRC, and tax records are disclosable in financial proceedings.</li>
  <li><strong>The blockchain itself is public.</strong> Once a wallet address is identified, its entire transaction history is visible, and forensic tracing firms can follow funds across wallets and exchanges where the sums justify the cost.</li>
  <li><strong>CARF reporting</strong> means that from January 2026 exchanges must hold verified identity and transaction data on UK users and report it to HMRC, with the first reports covering the 2026 year. An undeclared exchange balance now contradicts an official data trail, not just a spouse's suspicions.</li>
</ul>

<p>One warning on self-help: do not log into your spouse's accounts, copy their files or intercept their messages to prove a hidden wallet. Unlawfully obtained material can be inadmissible and can put you on the wrong side of the court. The lawful route, through questionnaires and orders for specific disclosure, is slower but it works, and adverse inferences fill the gaps where answers stay evasive. The general machinery for suspected non-disclosure, questionnaires, specific disclosure orders, section 37 applications and the penalties for hiding assets, is covered in our <a href="/blog/financial-settlements/hidden-assets-divorce">hidden assets in divorce guide</a>.</p>

<h2>CGT on crypto transfers in a divorce settlement</h2>

<p>Moving coins between spouses is a disposal for capital gains tax, and HMRC treats <a href="https://www.gov.uk/guidance/check-if-you-need-to-pay-tax-when-you-sell-cryptoassets">cryptoassets as chargeable assets</a> like shares. What decides whether tax is due immediately is timing:</p>

<table>
  <thead>
    <tr>
      <th>When the transfer happens</th>
      <th>CGT treatment (2026/27)</th>
    </tr>
  </thead>
  <tbody>
    <tr>
      <td>Inside the no gain no loss window: the tax year of separation plus 3 tax years, or any time under a court order (including a consent order)</td>
      <td>No gain no loss: no immediate CGT</td>
    </tr>
    <tr>
      <td>Later transfer with no court order</td>
      <td>Disposal at market value: gains above the £3,000 annual exempt amount taxed at 18 or 24 percent</td>
    </tr>
    <tr>
      <td>Selling coins to fund a settlement</td>
      <td>Normal disposal by the seller: CGT on gains above the £3,000 exempt amount</td>
    </tr>
  </tbody>
</table>

<p>The no-gain-no-loss rule sits in <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1992/12/section/58">section 58 of the Taxation of Chargeable Gains Act 1992</a>, extended for separating couples by the Finance Act 2023; the full timetable, with the deadlines worked through date by date, is in our <a href="/blog/tax-on-divorce/cgt-divorce-no-gain-no-loss-window">no gain no loss window guide</a>. But no gain no loss defers tax rather than deleting it, and that changes what a holding is worth in negotiation. The receiving spouse inherits the original purchase cost, so the latent gain travels with the coins.</p>

<p><strong>Worked example.</strong> A holding is worth £50,000 today and was bought for £10,000. Transferred inside the window, no tax is due now, but the recipient's base cost is £10,000. If they sell at £50,000 as a higher-rate taxpayer: gain £40,000, minus the £3,000 annual exempt amount, leaves £37,000 taxable at 24 percent, which is <strong>£8,880</strong>. The "£50,000" asset is really worth about £41,120 in the recipient's hands. If the settlement offsets those coins against £50,000 of cash or home equity, the spouse taking the crypto is short-changed by nearly £9,000 unless the latent tax is priced in. The full mechanics, including how the same logic applies to the family home and investments, are on our <a href="/capital-gains-tax-divorce">capital gains tax and divorce</a> page.</p>

<h2>Practical evidence steps before your divorce settlement</h2>

<ol>
  <li><strong>List every digital asset you hold</strong>: exchange accounts, wallets, NFTs, staking positions, with acquisition dates and costs. Your own clean disclosure sets the standard you can demand back.</li>
  <li><strong>Export dated records now</strong>: exchange statements and transaction CSVs, plus screenshots of wallet balances with visible dates. Do this early, before accounts can be emptied or closed.</li>
  <li><strong>Gather the money trail</strong>: bank statements showing transfers to or from crypto platforms, going back several years if holdings are disputed.</li>
  <li><strong>Ask the right questions in disclosure</strong>: has your spouse ever held accounts with named exchanges, and will they produce full transaction histories? Vague denials invite specific disclosure orders.</li>
  <li><strong>Agree a valuation mechanism</strong>: fixed dates, percentage splits or sale, so price swings cannot reopen the argument.</li>
  <li><strong>Seal the deal in a consent order</strong>: it makes the settlement binding, and it keeps later crypto transfers inside the no-gain-no-loss regime whenever they happen.</li>
</ol>

<h2>What a clean crypto settlement looks like</h2>

<p>Crypto changes the mechanics of a settlement, not the principles. It must be disclosed on Form E, it is traceable through bank records, tax data and the public blockchain, CARF reporting has been narrowing the space to hide since January 2026, and the CGT rules reward couples who transfer inside the no-gain-no-loss window or under a court order rather than drifting past it. Value it close to the deal, price in the latent tax, and put the result in a sealed order.</p>
