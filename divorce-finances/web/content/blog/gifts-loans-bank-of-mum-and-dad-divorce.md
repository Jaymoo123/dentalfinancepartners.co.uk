faqs:
  - question: "Is money from parents a gift or a loan in divorce?"
    answer: "The court starts from a presumption that money passed from a parent to their adult child is a gift, sometimes called the presumption of advancement. That presumption can be rebutted, but only with evidence that a genuine loan was intended at the time the money changed hands. If the evidence shows a real obligation to repay, the court treats the advance as a loan and considers deducting it from the assets as a debt. If the evidence is thin, or was created only after the marriage got into difficulty, the money is usually treated as a gift and stays in the pot to be divided. The label the family used in conversation matters far less than the paperwork and the repayment behaviour over the years."
  - question: "What is the difference between a soft loan and a hard loan in a financial settlement?"
    answer: "A hard loan is a genuine commercial-style obligation: there is usually a written agreement made at the time, agreed terms, a repayment schedule that has actually been followed, and a realistic prospect that the lender would enforce repayment. Courts deduct hard loans from the asset schedule like any other debt. A soft loan sits between a gift and a hard loan: the money may honestly have been described as a loan, but it came from family, nothing was documented, nothing has been repaid, and nobody seriously expects enforcement. Family courts frequently give soft loans little or no weight, on the basis that repayment is unlikely ever to be demanded, so the money stays available for division. The factors courts weigh were usefully collected in the family court decision P v Q in 2022, including whether terms exist, whether repayment has been made or demanded, and whether the lender would realistically sue."
  - question: "My parents gave us £80,000 towards our house deposit and we are now divorcing. Do they get it back?"
    answer: "Probably not, unless there is evidence from the time that it was a loan. A deposit contribution with no loan agreement, no charge on the property, no declaration of trust and no repayments over the years will almost always be treated as a gift into the marriage, and because it went into the family home it will usually be treated as matrimonial and shared. If your parents genuinely expected repayment, gather everything contemporaneous you can: emails or messages from the time of purchase, any written terms, bank records showing repayments, and anything the conveyancing solicitor recorded about the source of funds. If the mortgage lender was told the money was a gift, expect that gift declaration to be quoted back at you. Without time-of-transfer evidence, the realistic outcome is that the £80,000 stays in the pot."
  - question: "We signed a loan agreement with my in-laws but never repaid anything. Will the court count the debt?"
    answer: "A signed agreement helps, but it is not conclusive on its own. The court will look at how the loan actually behaved: were any repayments made, was interest charged or paid, did the lenders ever chase payment, and were there sensible terms about when repayment falls due? A document signed years ago that has sat in a drawer while no money moved looks like a soft loan, and a court may include the full amount in the pot anyway, or deduct only part of it, on the basis that repayment is unlikely ever to be pressed. The strongest position combines the agreement with a repayment history, even modest monthly amounts, and any evidence of the lender treating it as a real debt, such as reminders or a registered charge against the property."
  - question: "What evidence proves a family loan in divorce proceedings?"
    answer: "The evidence that persuades a judge is the evidence created when the money moved, not afterwards. In rough order of strength: a legal charge registered against the property at HM Land Registry; a written loan agreement signed at the time with repayment terms; a declaration of trust recording that the parents kept a beneficial share rather than lending at all; bank statements showing actual repayments over the years; and contemporaneous emails, messages or solicitor correspondence describing the money as repayable. What carries little weight is a statement written by a parent after the separation saying the money was always a loan, because courts see exactly that document in case after case. If the mortgage application described the funds as a gift, that declaration will undermine a later loan claim."
  - question: "How do I protect money I give my child before their marriage?"
    answer: "Decide honestly whether it is a gift or a loan, then document whichever it is before the money moves. If it is a loan, sign a proper loan agreement with terms, consider registering a charge against the property, and keep a repayment record even if payments are small. If you want to keep an ownership stake instead, use a declaration of trust recording your beneficial share at purchase. If it is a genuine gift, a prenuptial or postnuptial agreement can record the couple's intention that the gifted sum, or the asset it bought, is not shared equally on divorce; a gift also starts the seven-year clock for inheritance tax. The one thing that does not work is doing nothing and deciding what the money was only once the marriage is in trouble."
  - question: "Do parents have to join the divorce proceedings to get their loan repaid?"
    answer: "Sometimes. If the spouses agree the loan is real and build repayment into their settlement, the parents never need to be involved and the consent order simply reflects the debt. But where one spouse disputes the loan, or the parents claim they own a share of the family home under a trust, the parents may need to intervene in the financial remedy proceedings so the court can decide their claim. Intervening makes them a party: they file evidence, they can be cross-examined, and they carry a costs risk if they lose. Judges and practitioners generally see intervener disputes as expensive detours, which is exactly why contemporaneous paperwork matters: a documented loan or declared trust share rarely needs to be litigated at all."
  - question: "Is a loan from parents included in Form E?"
    answer: "Yes. Leaving it out and raising it later looks opportunistic and damages credibility. The other spouse can question the loan in a questionnaire, asking for the agreement, the repayment history and bank evidence of the original advance. If you are the spouse disputing the loan, those questions are exactly where the soft-versus-hard distinction gets tested. <p>Money from the bank of mum and dad lands in one of three boxes when a marriage ends: a <strong>gift</strong>, a <strong>soft loan</strong> or a <strong>hard loan</strong>, and the box decides whether the money is shared, discounted or repaid. In England and Wales the court's starting point is a presumption that money advanced from parent to child is a gift, so the burden of proving a real loan sits squarely on the person claiming one.</p>

<p>That classification is not a technicality. An £80,000 parental deposit treated as a hard loan comes off the top of the asset schedule before anything is divided; the same £80,000 treated as a gift stays in the pot and is split. Because a single judgment call can move tens of thousands of pounds, it is worth modelling your settlement both ways before you negotiate: the <a href="/calculators/settlement-range-estimator">settlement range estimator</a> lets you run the numbers with the family money in the pot and then out of it, so you can see exactly what is riding on the argument.</p>

<p>This guide explains how family courts draw the line, the evidence that actually persuades a judge, and how families can protect money before a wedding rather than fight about it after one.</p>

<h2>Gift, soft loan or hard loan: how divorce courts classify family money</h2>

<p>When a financial settlement is decided by a court, the judge works through the factors in <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1973/18/section/25">section 25 of the Matrimonial Causes Act 1973</a>, which include each party's resources and their liabilities. Family money enters that exercise in one of three ways:</p>

<ul>
  <li><strong>A gift.</strong> Money handed over with no expectation of repayment. Once given, it belongs to the recipient, and if it was poured into the family home or family spending it is normally treated as matrimonial and shared. The giver has no claim to it back.</li>
  <li><strong>A hard loan.</strong> A genuine obligation: written terms agreed when the money moved, a repayment schedule that has actually been followed, and a lender who would realistically enforce. The court deducts it from the assets like a bank debt.</li>
  <li><strong>A soft loan.</strong> The grey middle. The family called it a loan, but nothing was documented, nothing has been repaid, and nobody expects mum and dad to sue. Courts often leave soft loans in the pot entirely, or give them reduced weight, because repayment is unlikely ever to be demanded.</li>
</ul>

<p>The factors that separate soft from hard were helpfully collected by the family court in P v Q in 2022: whether there are written terms, whether repayments have been made or requested, whether the lender has pressed for payment, and whether, realistically, the obligation would ever be enforced. A loan that has behaved like a loan gets treated like one. A loan that has behaved like a gift usually gets treated like one too, whatever the family called it.</p>

<h2>How each type is treated in the financial settlement asset schedule</h2>

<p>The asset schedule is the single document at the heart of every <a href="/blog/financial-settlements/divorce-financial-settlement-guide">divorce financial settlement</a>: assets on one side, liabilities on the other, and the net pot divided from there. Here is where each classification lands:</p>

<p>Disclosure is where this battle usually starts. A spouse who owes family money must list it among their liabilities in <a href="/blog/process-and-costs/form-e-financial-disclosure-guide">Form E, the sworn financial disclosure statement</a>, and the other spouse can then demand the loan agreement, the repayment history and bank evidence of the original advance through a questionnaire. A family debt that first appears late in proceedings, with paperwork dated after the separation, is treated with scepticism. The official guidance on <a href="https://www.gov.uk/money-property-when-relationship-ends">dividing money and property when a relationship ends</a> is clear that financial claims are settled on full disclosure of assets and debts, and manufactured liabilities are one of the classic disclosure disputes.</p>

<h2>Evidence that persuades a court in divorce proceedings</h2>

<p>Judges see the same pattern constantly: money moved years ago with nothing in writing, and a letter from a parent appears after the separation saying it was always a loan. That letter is worth very little. What persuades a court is evidence created when the money moved, broadly in this order of strength:</p>

<ol>
  <li><strong>A registered legal charge.</strong> A charge secured against the property at HM Land Registry is the closest thing to conclusive: the parents took security like a real lender.</li>
  <li><strong>A declaration of trust.</strong> A deed recording that the parents kept a beneficial share of the property rather than lending at all. Trust claims over the family home are governed by the <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1996/47/contents">Trusts of Land and Appointment of Trustees Act 1996</a>, and a properly drafted deed at purchase makes the parents' stake visible from day one.</li>
  <li><strong>A written loan agreement signed at the time,</strong> with an amount, repayment terms and signatures from both spouses, not just the child of the lenders.</li>
  <li><strong>A repayment history.</strong> Bank statements showing regular payments, even small ones, are powerful because they show the loan behaving like a loan for years before anyone was divorcing.</li>
  <li><strong>Contemporaneous correspondence.</strong> Emails, messages or solicitor notes from the time describing the money as repayable.</li>
</ol>

<p>One trap deserves a highlight: mortgage gift declarations. Lenders routinely require parents contributing a deposit to confirm in writing that the money is a gift with no interest in the property. If that declaration exists, a later claim that the same money was secretly a loan starts a long way behind, because the family has already told a lender the opposite in a formal document.</p>

<h2>Worked example: how a disputed loan moves a financial settlement</h2>

<p>Take a couple with a family home worth £450,000 carrying a £150,000 mortgage, so £300,000 of equity, plus £40,000 of savings. The wife's parents advanced £80,000 towards the deposit ten years ago. Nothing was written down and nothing has been repaid.</p>

<ul>
  <li><strong>If the £80,000 is a hard loan:</strong> the pot is £340,000 minus £80,000, leaving £260,000. On an equal split each spouse takes £130,000 and the parents are repaid in full.</li>
  <li><strong>If the £80,000 is a gift or a soft loan given no weight:</strong> the pot stays at £340,000 and each spouse takes £170,000. The parents recover nothing through the proceedings.</li>
</ul>

<p>The difference is £40,000 per spouse on identical facts, which is why these arguments are fought hard, and why the spouse whose parents provided the money often "remembers" a loan while the other spouse "remembers" a gift. On the undocumented facts above, the presumption of gift plus ten years of silence means the realistic outcome is the second one. Where the home itself is being kept by one spouse, the classification also feeds directly into <a href="/blog/the-family-home/house-buyout-transfer-of-equity-divorce">the buyout price for the family home</a>, because the equity available to divide changes by the amount of the loan.</p>

<p>Parents who dispute the couple's version can apply to intervene in the financial remedy proceedings so the court decides their claim. Intervening makes them a party with evidence to file, cross-examination to face and a costs risk if they lose, so it is a last resort, not a plan.</p>

<h2>Protecting family money before marriage: the family home, trusts and nuptial agreements</h2>

<p>Every one of these disputes is cheaper to prevent than to fight. Families advancing money before or during a marriage have four clean options:</p>

<ul>
  <li><strong>Lend formally.</strong> A signed loan agreement with terms, ideally secured by a registered charge, plus actual repayments. This is what makes a loan hard rather than soft.</li>
  <li><strong>Keep a share.</strong> A declaration of trust at purchase recording the parents' beneficial percentage of the property, so the money was never the couple's to divide.</li>
  <li><strong>Record the intention in a nuptial agreement.</strong> A <a href="/blog/financial-settlements/prenuptial-postnuptial-agreements">prenuptial or postnuptial agreement</a> can state that gifted family money, or the asset it bought, is non-matrimonial and how it should be treated on divorce. Courts give real weight to agreements freely entered into with disclosure and advice.</li>
  <li><strong>Gift cleanly and accept it.</strong> A genuine gift is simpler, and it starts the seven-year clock for inheritance tax under the <a href="https://www.gov.uk/inheritance-tax/gifts">gov.uk rules on gifts</a>. What does not work is calling money a gift for the mortgage lender and a loan for the divorce court.</li>
</ul>

<p>Where a settlement is agreed and a family loan is accepted as real, build the repayment into the <a href="/blog/process-and-costs/consent-orders-and-clean-break-orders">consent order</a> so the debt is dealt with once, inside the binding order, rather than left hanging between the households.</p>

<h2>Getting family money treated fairly in your divorce</h2>

<p>The rules are blunt but predictable. Undocumented family money is presumed to be a gift and shared. A loan is deducted only if it looks and behaves like a real debt: written terms from the time, security or a trust deed where possible, and a repayment history. Evidence created after separation persuades nobody.
