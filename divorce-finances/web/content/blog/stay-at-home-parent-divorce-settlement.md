faqs:
  - question: "Am I entitled to half of everything if I gave up work to raise the children?"
    answer: "Equal sharing is the starting point for matrimonial assets built up during the marriage, and the fact that one parent earned the money and the other raised the children does not shift it. That is the core of White v White. What moves the final figure away from a clean half is needs. In a long marriage with a large asset pot, needs are met comfortably and the outcome often lands close to equal. In an ordinary case where the assets are a house with modest equity and two pensions, needs dominate, and a stay-at-home parent housing the children may receive well over half the capital, sometimes with a smaller share of the income side to balance it. The honest answer is that half is the starting point and the reference point, not a guaranteed outcome in either direction."
  - question: "I have not worked for 12 years and my youngest is 4. Will the court expect me to get a job?"
    answer: "Eventually, yes, but not immediately and not on unrealistic terms. The court assesses earning capacity as it actually is, including any increase it would be reasonable to expect you to take steps to acquire (section 25(2)(a)). With a 4-year-old, a judge is unlikely to expect full-time work straight away, and childcare costs are set against any earnings you could realistically get. The usual shape is a stepped one: maintenance at a level that reflects no or limited earnings while the child is young, reducing as the child reaches school age and then secondary school, with a term long enough to retrain or rebuild hours. What courts resist is an assumption that you will never work again where the children are older and the work is genuinely available. Evidence helps: job adverts in your field, the cost of the retraining you need, nursery and wraparound care quotes."
  - question: "What if my ex says the house was bought with his money before we married?"
    answer: "It is a real argument and it sometimes reduces the share, but it rarely defeats a needs-based claim. Property brought into the marriage by one spouse can be treated as non-matrimonial, which means it is not automatically shared equally. The limit on that argument is that non-matrimonial assets can still be used to meet needs where the matrimonial assets are not enough, and housing the children is the strongest need there is. The longer the marriage, and the more the house was lived in and improved as the family home, the more the original source fades. Where the pot is large enough to house both parents without touching the pre-marital element, the argument bites; where it is not, it usually does not."
  - question: "Do I get a share of my ex's pension if I never paid into one myself?"
    answer: "You can. A pension is a matrimonial asset like any other, and a pension sharing order (available under the Welfare Reform and Pensions Act 1999 for divorces started on or after 1 December 2000) moves a percentage of one spouse's pension into a pension in your own name. It is not a claim against their income later, it is a clean transfer of value, which is why it sits comfortably with a clean break. The share is expressed as a percentage of the cash equivalent value and takes 4 months to implement once the order takes effect. The state pension is different: only the protected-payment element of the new state pension can be shared, and the rest of your state pension is built from your own National Insurance record, which is where checking your National Insurance credits for years spent receiving Child Benefit matters."
  - question: "Does child maintenance count as my income when spousal maintenance is worked out?"
    answer: "Child maintenance is money for the children's costs and is assessed separately by the Child Maintenance Service using a formula based on the paying parent's gross weekly income. It is taken into account in the sense that the court looks at the whole picture of what comes into your household and what the children cost, but it is not treated as spousal income and it does not extinguish a spousal maintenance claim. In practice the two are worked out in sequence: the Child Maintenance Service figure is established first because it is largely mechanical, then the shortfall between your reasonable household needs and everything else coming in shapes the spousal figure. Child maintenance is also tax free and unaffected by the tax treatment of spousal maintenance."
  - question: "What if my ex stops paying spousal maintenance once I start working again?"
    answer: "They cannot lawfully stop unilaterally. A maintenance order remains binding until it is varied by the court or the term expires, so stopping payment without an order is a breach that can be enforced, including by attachment of earnings. What your ex can properly do is apply to vary the order downwards on the ground of a material change in circumstances, and your new earnings are exactly that kind of change. The realistic outcome is often a reduction rather than a cut to zero, particularly if your earnings are part time or lower than the standard of living during the marriage. If your income has risen substantially and the term is close to its end, a capitalised lump sum in place of the remaining payments is sometimes agreed instead, which gives both sides certainty."
  - question: "We were never married and I stayed at home with the children. Do I have the same claim?"
    answer: "No, and this is the single most damaging misconception in this area. There is no such thing as a common law spouse in England and Wales, and an unmarried parent has no claim for spousal maintenance, no pension sharing claim and no automatic share of a home held in the other parent's sole name. What does exist is narrower: child maintenance through the Child Maintenance Service, a possible claim under Schedule 1 of the Children Act 1989 for the benefit of the children (which can include the use of a home until they finish education, usually returning to the payer afterwards), and property law claims based on ownership or contributions. The practical consequence is that a long-term cohabiting parent who gave up work has far less protection than a married one, which is why a cohabitation or separation agreement matters so much."
  - question: "Can I ask for a settlement that reflects the career I gave up?"
    answer: "There is a route, but it is narrow. Alongside needs and sharing, the House of Lords in Miller and McFarlane (2006) recognised compensation for relationship-generated disadvantage, meaning the loss of a career that one spouse gave up for the family. In practice, awards on that basis are rare and are largely confined to cases where the lost career was demonstrably high earning and the evidence of what was given up is clear. For most stay-at-home parents the same reality is captured more effectively through needs, by arguing for a longer maintenance term, a larger share of capital, and a pension share that reflects the years of contributions that were never made."

<p>Section 25(2)(f) of the <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1973/18/section/25">Matrimonial Causes Act 1973</a> requires a court dividing finances on divorce to weigh the contribution each spouse has made to the welfare of the family, <strong>expressly including any contribution made by looking after the home or caring for the family</strong>. Since the House of Lords decision in White v White in 2000, there is to be no bias in favour of the money-earner and against the home-maker, and a judge who reaches a division should check it against the yardstick of equality before departing from it.</p>

<p>That principle answers the question most stay-at-home parents ask first, but it does not tell you what you will actually receive. This guide covers how a needs-based award is built for a parent who stopped earning, how spousal maintenance and term orders work, why pension sharing is usually the most valuable protection on the table, and what a court will expect of your earning capacity. It applies to <strong>England and Wales</strong>. Scotland works differently: matrimonial property there is broadly what was acquired during the marriage, and periodical allowance after divorce is normally limited to around 3 years to allow adjustment.</p>

<p>Before you negotiate anything, run your figures through the <a href="/calculators/settlement-range-estimator">settlement range estimator</a>. For a stay-at-home parent it is most useful for one specific test: enter the marriage length and the children under 18, then check whether the capital available can house two households at all. If it cannot, you are in a needs case, and the arguments below matter far more than any share percentage.</p>

<h2>Why raising the children counts equally in a financial settlement</h2>

<p>The law does not discount a contribution because it was unpaid. A parent who ran the home and raised the children for 15 years while the other built a career has contributed to the family's welfare in a way the statute puts on the same footing as the salary, and the earner's ability to earn was itself supported by that work at home.</p>

<p>Two consequences follow. Arguments that one spouse "paid for everything" carry no weight on their own. And a claim of special contribution, that one spouse's earnings were so exceptional they justify an unequal split, succeeds only in rare, very high value cases. Our guide to <a href="/blog/financial-settlements/what-am-i-entitled-to-divorce">what you are entitled to in a divorce</a> covers the sharing principle and the matrimonial versus non-matrimonial distinction in full.</p>

<h2>Needs decide most stay-at-home parent divorce settlements</h2>

<p>Equal sharing is the starting point, but in most cases the asset pot is not big enough for sharing to be the deciding factor. Needs take over, assessed against the standard of living during the marriage, its length, the ages of the children and the resources actually available. For a stay-at-home parent that translates into four planks, worth separating because they are met by different mechanisms and fail in different ways.</p>

<p>The welfare of any child of the family under 18 is the court's first consideration under section 25(1), so housing the children is settled before the rest of the pot is divided. That is why a stay-at-home parent can properly receive more than half the capital: not as a reward, but because two homes have to be funded and one has to hold the children.</p>

<h2>Housing the children and keeping the family home</h2>

<p>Keeping the house is not automatic, and it is not always the right result. The realistic options are an outright transfer (usually paired with a smaller share of pensions or a reduced maintenance claim), a sale and split with a larger share to the parent housing the children, or a deferred sale until a trigger event such as the youngest child finishing full-time education.</p>

<p>The deciding constraint is usually borrowing. A parent with little or no income may not be able to take the mortgage into their sole name, which is a question a lender answers, not the court. Where a transfer is not affordable, deferred sale orders keep the children in place without pretending the mortgage problem does not exist. See <a href="/blog/the-family-home/who-gets-the-house-divorce">who gets the house in a divorce</a> and <a href="/blog/the-family-home/mesher-orders-deferred-house-sale">Mesher orders and deferred house sales</a> for how each option is drafted and what the trigger events do.</p>

<h2>Spousal maintenance and term orders for a parent who stopped earning</h2>

<p>Spousal maintenance fills the gap between your reasonable needs and the income you have or could reasonably earn. It is the plank most exposed to the direction of travel in the courts, which is towards defined terms rather than payments for joint lives. Section 25A obliges the court to consider whether a clean break is possible, and if not, whether maintenance can be limited to a term long enough for you to adjust without undue hardship.</p>

<p>The detail that matters most is whether the term is extendable. A term order without a section 28(1A) bar can be extended by applying to the court before it expires. With the bar attached, it cannot, and when it ends, it ends. For a parent whose return to work is uncertain, that is frequently the most valuable single point in the negotiation. Stepped orders are common too, reducing as the youngest child reaches school age. Our <a href="/blog/maintenance-and-support/spousal-maintenance-guide">spousal maintenance guide</a> covers duration, variation and capitalisation in depth.</p>

<p>Child maintenance runs alongside it on a formula. Take a separated parent, call her Bethan, caring for two children, whose ex-partner has a gross weekly income of £900. The Child Maintenance Service basic rate applies at <strong>16%</strong> on the first £800, giving £128, plus the basic-plus rate of <strong>12%</strong> on the slice from £800.01 to £900, giving £12: <strong>£140 a week</strong>. If the case runs on Collect and Pay, the paying parent pays 20% on top (£168) and Bethan receives 4% less (£134.40). Direct Pay is free to both parents. That is money for the children, and it does not answer the adult income question.</p>

<h2>Pension sharing is the protection most often given away</h2>

<p>Years spent at home are years of pension contributions that were never made, and the gap does not close by itself. Pension sharing orders have been available for divorces started on or after 1 December 2000 under the Welfare Reform and Pensions Act 1999. The order moves a percentage of the cash equivalent value into a pension in your own name, takes <strong>4 months</strong> to implement, and is compatible with a clean break because nothing further is owed afterwards.</p>

<p>The mistake to avoid is trading the pension for house equity without a proper comparison. A pound of pension and a pound of house equity are not the same pound: they are taxed differently, accessed at different times, and a defined benefit cash equivalent can badly understate the income the pension actually buys. With defined benefit schemes, a report from a pensions on divorce expert is often the difference between a fair share and a costly guess. Read <a href="/blog/pensions-and-divorce/pensions-and-divorce">pensions and divorce</a> for how valuation and the three mechanisms work. On the state pension, only the protected-payment element of the new state pension can be shared, so check your own National Insurance record for the years you were receiving Child Benefit.</p>

<h2>Earning capacity arguments in a divorce settlement</h2>

<p>The other side will usually argue you can work more than you say. Section 25(2)(a) asks not only what you earn but what earning capacity it would be reasonable to expect you to take steps to acquire. That test is answered with evidence, not assertion.</p>

<ul>
  <li>What your field pays now, and what has changed since you left it (advertised roles are the simplest evidence)</li>
  <li>The retraining or requalification you would need, its cost and how long it takes</li>
  <li>Childcare costs set against any earnings, including wraparound care and school holidays</li>
  <li>The ages of the children and any additional needs affecting the hours you can work</li>
  <li>Your health and age, which affect retraining and the years left to rebuild a pension</li>
</ul>

<p>An expectation that you will return to some work is normal and reasonable. An expectation that you will immediately earn what you earned before a 12-year break is not, and evidence is what separates the two. If you are co-parenting through this, the <a href="/for/separated-parents">separated parents hub</a> sets out how the money and the arrangements interact.</p>

<p>A stay-at-home parent's settlement is built plank by plank: housing first because the children's welfare comes first, then income for as long as it takes to rebuild earnings, then a pension share that repairs the contribution gap, with child maintenance running separately. The two decisions that cost people most are agreeing a clean break before earning capacity has genuinely recovered, and swapping a pension share for house equity without comparing what each is really worth.
