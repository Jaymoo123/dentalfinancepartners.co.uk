faqs:
  - question: "Am I allowed to represent myself in financial remedy proceedings?"
    answer: "Yes. There is no requirement to instruct a solicitor in family proceedings in England and Wales, and the family courts deal with self-represented parties every day. You can issue Form A yourself, complete and exchange Form E, draft your own questionnaire, attend every hearing and address the judge directly. Judges are expected to make reasonable adjustments to the way a hearing runs so an unrepresented party can participate, for example explaining what a stage is for before it starts. What the judge cannot do is give you legal advice, tell you what to ask for, or run your case. The court will also hold you to the same rules and deadlines as a solicitor, because those deadlines exist to keep the case moving for both sides."
  - question: "What actually happens at the first appointment and the FDR?"
    answer: "The first appointment (FDA) is an administrative hearing, not a decision on who gets what. The judge deals with what information is still missing: which questions in each side's questionnaire must be answered, whether a property or business valuation is needed, whether a pension expert should be appointed, and what the timetable is from here. Many courts will treat the first appointment as an FDR if the disclosure is already complete and both sides agree. The FDR is different in character. It is a settlement hearing where both sides put forward without-prejudice offers and the judge gives a candid, non-binding indication of the likely range of outcomes. That indication carries real weight because it comes from a judge who reads these cases daily. Nothing said at the FDR can be repeated at a final hearing, and the FDR judge is barred from hearing the final hearing, which is precisely why they can speak freely."
  - question: "Do I have to try mediation before applying to court?"
    answer: "In most cases you must attend a Mediation Information and Assessment Meeting (MIAM) before filing Form A, unless an exemption applies, for example where there is evidence of domestic abuse or the matter is urgent. Beyond that, since 29 April 2024 both parties in contested financial remedy proceedings must file and serve Form FM5, a statement of their position on non-court dispute resolution, seven days before the first on-notice hearing. It is not a formality. Rule 28.3(7) of the Family Procedure Rules expressly requires the court to have regard to any failure by a party, without good reason, to attend a MIAM or to attend non-court dispute resolution when deciding whether to make a costs order. The Family Mediation Voucher scheme also contributes £500 per family towards mediation where there is a dispute about a child, and the scheme has been extended to March 2027."
  - question: "Who prepares the court bundle if I am representing myself?"
    answer: "Usually the other side, if they have a solicitor. Practice Direction 27A, in force from 2 March 2026, sets the rules on bundles, and paragraph 4.6 provides that where an applicant is a litigant in person but the respondent is legally represented, the represented respondent must prepare, file and serve the bundle, subject to any direction of the court. If neither of you is represented, the court will direct who does it. An electronic bundle is limited to 350 pages of A4 by default and can only exceed that with permission; a paper bundle is limited to 175 sheets. The bundle itself must be filed no later than five working days before the hearing, with preliminary documents such as the case summary and the schedule of issues due by 11am on the working day before. Missing those deadlines is one of the fastest ways to lose credibility with a judge."
  - question: "What if my ex has a solicitor and I do not? Am I at a disadvantage?"
    answer: "You are at a disadvantage on process, not on principle. Their solicitor knows the timetable, the standard directions and the way a District Judge in that court tends to approach things, and that experience is real. What it does not do is change the law or the judge's duty to be even-handed. The practical response is to spend money where the asymmetry hurts most rather than on full representation. Buy an hour or two of advice before you exchange Form E, so your disclosure is complete and your questionnaire targets the right gaps. Then, if the case reaches an FDR or a final hearing, consider instructing a direct access barrister for that hearing alone. That combination typically costs a fraction of a full retainer and closes most of the gap. It is also worth noting that a represented opponent has professional duties to the court and is not permitted to mislead it or take unfair advantage of your lack of representation."
  - question: "What if I miss a deadline or my Form E is late?"
    answer: "Tell the court and the other side immediately, in writing, with the reason and a realistic new date. Judges are far more tolerant of an early, honest request than of silence followed by an apology in the hearing room. Depending on the stage, you may need to apply for an extension of time, and there is usually a fee for a formal application. The real risk of lateness is not the missed date itself but its knock-on effects: an incomplete Form E delays the questionnaire stage, which delays the FDR, which adds months and cost for both households. Persistent non-compliance also engages rule 28.3(7), under which failure to comply with the rules, a practice direction or a court order is expressly something the court weighs when deciding whether to depart from the usual no-costs rule."
  - question: "Can I take a McKenzie friend into the hearing, and should I pay for one?"
    answer: "You can normally have a McKenzie friend with you. Under the Practice Guidance on McKenzie Friends in the civil and family courts (July 2010), a litigant in person has a presumptive right to reasonable assistance: the McKenzie friend may sit beside you, take notes, help with papers and give quiet advice. They have no automatic right of audience, so they cannot speak for you unless the court grants permission in that case, and they cannot conduct the litigation, sign documents or manage correspondence as a solicitor would. Financial remedy hearings are heard in private, so the court has to be told who they are and what their interest is. Fee-charging McKenzie friends are not a regulated profession: there is no compulsory insurance, no compensation scheme and no regulator to complain to. If you are going to pay for help, an unbundled retainer with a solicitor or a direct access barrister buys regulated, insured advice for a comparable sum."
  - question: "Do I get anything back for my own time if I win?"
    answer: "Rarely, and not much. The general rule in rule 28.3(5) is that the court will not order one party in financial remedy proceedings to pay another party's costs, so most people bear their own costs whatever the outcome. Where the court does make a costs order because of conduct, a self-represented litigant is not compensated at solicitor rates. Practice Direction 46 paragraph 3.4 sets the amount allowed to a self-represented litigant at £24 per hour, raised from £19 with effect from 1 October 2025, unless you can prove actual financial loss such as lost earnings. You can also recover disbursements you have genuinely paid out, for example court fees and expert fees. The lesson is not to treat a possible costs order as a reason to litigate: assume you will carry your own costs, and let that shape how hard you fight over what."

<p>Filing <strong>Form A</strong> to start contested financial proceedings costs <strong>£321</strong>, and from that moment the timetable belongs to the court. Rule 9.12 of the Family Procedure Rules 2010 requires the first appointment to be listed <strong>not less than 12 weeks and not more than 16 weeks</strong> after the application is filed, and rule 9.14 requires each party to exchange and file <strong>Form E at least 35 days before it</strong>, with the questionnaire, chronology, Form G and Form H due at least 14 days before.</p>

<p>You are entitled to do all of that yourself. This guide covers the three-stage court process a litigant in person meets in a divorce financial settlement, what the judge expects at each stage, the bundle and paperwork rules, McKenzie friends and unbundled help, the real costs risk, and where self-representation genuinely works. Before you decide, run your circumstances through the <a href="/calculators/divorce-cost-calculator">divorce cost calculator</a>: seeing the court fees you will pay either way separated from the legal fees you would avoid is usually what settles the question, because the fees are fixed and the advice is the only variable.</p>

<p>This is England and Wales law and process. Scotland works differently: financial provision runs under the Family Law (Scotland) Act 1985, matrimonial property is broadly what was acquired during the marriage valued at the date of separation, and financial claims must be resolved before the divorce itself is granted rather than afterwards.</p>

<h2>The financial remedy court process a divorce applicant faces</h2>

<p>The money and the divorce are two separate cases. The divorce application itself costs <strong>£628</strong> from 13 July 2026 and takes a minimum of <strong>26 weeks</strong> (a 20-week reflection period, then a 6-week-and-1-day wait to the final order) under the Divorce, Dissolution and Separation Act 2020. Our guide to a <a href="/blog/process-and-costs/diy-divorce-without-solicitor">DIY divorce without a solicitor</a> covers that application. Financial remedy proceedings are a separate track with their own fee and their own timetable.</p>

<p>Before you can file Form A you normally have to attend a Mediation Information and Assessment Meeting, unless an exemption applies. If mediation produces a deal, you never enter this process at all: you pay <strong>£62</strong> for a consent order instead of £321 for a contested application, which is covered in <a href="/blog/process-and-costs/consent-orders-and-clean-break-orders">consent orders and clean break orders</a> and in our <a href="/blog/process-and-costs/divorce-mediation-guide">divorce mediation guide</a>. The <strong>Family Mediation Voucher scheme</strong> contributes <strong>£500 per family</strong> where there is a dispute about a child, and runs to March 2027.</p>

<p>The first appointment is the stage litigants in person most often misread. It is not your chance to argue that the house should be sold. It is a housekeeping hearing about missing information, and the judge will want to know precisely which questions in your questionnaire remain unanswered and why each one matters to the outcome. Fifteen focused questions land better than eighty scattergun ones.</p>

<p>The FDR is where most cases end. Both sides make without-prejudice offers, the judge tells you frankly what a court is likely to do, and the FDR judge is then barred from hearing your final hearing, which is exactly why they can speak plainly. Take the indication seriously even when it stings. Pushing on to a final hearing against a judicial steer is how modest asset pots get consumed by costs.</p>

<h2>Court bundles and paperwork when you have no solicitor in a divorce</h2>

<p>Practice Direction 27A, in force from 2 March 2026, governs bundles. An electronic bundle is capped at <strong>350 pages</strong> of A4 without the court's permission, a paper bundle at 175 sheets, and the bundle must be filed <strong>no later than five working days</strong> before the hearing, with preliminary documents (case summary, schedule of issues, chronology) due by <strong>11am on the working day before</strong>.</p>

<p>Paragraph 4.6 helps unrepresented applicants directly: where the applicant is a litigant in person but the respondent is legally represented, the represented respondent must prepare, file and serve the bundle unless the court directs otherwise. You still have to read it, check the index against your own papers and flag anything missing.</p>

<p>Form E is the document that carries the most weight and the most risk. It is signed with a statement of truth, requires 12 months of statements for every account, and understating or omitting an asset is misleading the court, not negotiating. Our <a href="/blog/process-and-costs/form-e-financial-disclosure-guide">Form E disclosure guide</a> works through it section by section.</p>

<h2>McKenzie friends and unbundled advice in a divorce financial settlement</h2>

<p>A McKenzie friend can sit with you, take notes, organise your papers and give quiet advice during the hearing. Under the Practice Guidance on McKenzie Friends in the civil and family courts (July 2010) that is a presumptive right, but it stops there: no automatic right of audience, so they cannot address the judge unless the court permits it in that particular case, and no right to conduct the litigation. Fee-charging McKenzie friends are unregulated and uninsured, with no complaints body behind them.</p>

<p>Unbundled help is usually better value for the same money. Options include a fixed-fee advice appointment before you exchange Form E, a solicitor engaged under a limited retainer to draft or check one document, a direct access barrister instructed for the FDR or final hearing alone, and a single joint expert appointed to value a pension or a business with the cost shared. Ffion, separating after a 19-year marriage, ran her own disclosure and correspondence, then paid for counsel at the FDR only. The case settled that day.</p>

<h2>Costs risk for a litigant in person in a financial settlement</h2>

<p>The general rule in rule 28.3(5) is that the court will not order one party to pay another's costs in financial remedy proceedings. Rule 28.3(6) and (7) let the court depart from that for conduct, and the listed factors include failure to comply with the rules or a court order, failure without good reason to attend a MIAM or non-court dispute resolution, the openness and reasonableness of offers made, and how each party pursued or responded to the application.</p>

<p>Two consequences follow. First, being unrepresented is no shield: missed deadlines and refusal to engage with settlement can produce a costs order against you. Second, winning buys you very little back. Practice Direction 46 paragraph 3.4 allows a self-represented litigant <strong>£24 per hour</strong> for their own time, raised from £19 with effect from 1 October 2025, unless actual financial loss such as lost earnings is proved. Court fees and expert fees you have genuinely paid can be recovered; your evenings cannot. If the fees themselves are the obstacle, <strong>Help with Fees</strong> (form EX160) can reduce or remove them, with income thresholds of <strong>£1,420</strong> for a single applicant and <strong>£2,130</strong> for a couple, plus <strong>£425</strong> per child aged 0 to 13 or <strong>£710</strong> per child aged 14 or over. Help tapers by 50p for each £1 of monthly income above the threshold, capped at the fee, and refunds can be claimed within 3 months.</p>

<h2>Where representing yourself works and where it fails in a divorce</h2>

<p>Self-representation works where the asset pot is transparent and modest, both sides disclose honestly, there is no business and no defined-benefit pension, there is no history of abuse or coercive control, and the disagreement is about the last 10 per cent rather than the shape of the deal. In those cases the process is largely administrative and a careful person can run it.</p>

<p>It fails predictably in five places: pension sharing, where a cash equivalent value is not a like-for-like comparison with cash and an actuary is often needed; business or share valuations; any suspicion of hidden assets, where the questionnaire and third-party disclosure need to be drafted with precision; cases involving abuse, where cross-examining or being cross-examined by the other party raises protective issues the court must manage; and the drafting of the final order itself, where an ambiguous clause on sale, charge-back or indexation surfaces years later as a fresh dispute. A comparison of what each route costs is in <a href="/blog/process-and-costs/cost-of-divorce-uk">the cost of divorce in the UK</a>.</p>

<p>Representing yourself in financial remedy proceedings is a legitimate choice, and for a straightforward pot it is often the sensible one. The deciding factor is not confidence but the shape of the assets: fixed court fees are known and survivable, whereas an error over a pension or a badly drafted final order is not. Where the case is simple, run it yourself and buy advice for the one or two stages that carry real risk. If you want to understand what a court is likely to do with your assets before you commit either way, start with our guide to <a href="/financial-settlements">financial settlements on divorce</a>.</p>
