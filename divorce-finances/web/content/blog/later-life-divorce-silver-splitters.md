faqs:
  - question: "Is pension sharing better than offsetting in a later-life divorce?"
    answer: "Usually, yes, and the older you are the stronger the case. Offsetting means one person keeps more pension and the other keeps more of something else, typically the house. That trade can work at 40, when the person giving up pension has 25 working years to rebuild. At 60 or 65 there is no runway left: whatever pension you walk away with is broadly what you will retire on. Sharing, by contrast, moves a percentage of one person's pension into a pension in the other person's own name, giving both people income for life. Offsetting also hides a valuation trap: £100,000 of pension is not worth £100,000 of cash, because pension money is taxed on the way out and locked up, so a pound-for-pound swap almost always short-changes someone. In long-marriage cases the court's focus is often on equalising retirement income, which offsetting is poorly suited to deliver."
  - question: "Can my ex claim part of my state pension if we divorce after retirement?"
    answer: "Only in a narrow way. If you reached state pension age on or after 6 April 2016 you are under the new state pension, and the standard amount cannot be shared, offset or claimed by an ex-spouse. The single exception is a protected payment, the excess some people receive above the full new state pension because of entitlement built up under the old rules. A court can make a sharing order over a protected payment, but never over the core amount. Under the old system, people who reached state pension age before 6 April 2016 could sometimes use an ex-spouse's National Insurance record to boost a basic state pension, and additional state pension (SERPS or S2P) could be shared. That substitution route does not exist under the new state pension, which makes checking your own National Insurance record and state pension forecast an early job in any later-life divorce."
  - question: "I am 62, my husband has a large workplace pension and I have almost nothing. What happens?"
    answer: "This is the classic silver-splitter pattern, often after years where one person built a career pension while the other raised children or worked part time. After a long marriage the starting point is that the whole pot, including pension accrued before you married, is available to meet both people's needs. In a case like this the likely tool is a pension sharing order: the court transfers a percentage of his pension's cash equivalent value into a pension in your own name, and the scheme has 4 months to implement it once the order takes effect. Where both of you are at or near retirement, the percentage is often set to equalise retirement incomes rather than simply split the fund value in half, because equal capital can produce very unequal incomes at different ages. A pension sharing order can only be made by a court, so you need a financial order even if you agree everything between yourselves."
  - question: "We are both 67. Can one of us keep the house by buying the other out?"
    answer: "Sometimes, but the maths is tighter than it was at 45. Any mortgage needed for the buyout is assessed against retirement income, and mainstream lenders apply maximum age limits. Later-life lending does exist, including retirement interest-only mortgages, but the amounts available on pension income are usually modest. The alternative, offsetting, means keeping the house in exchange for giving up pension, and that is the trade to test hardest: a mortgage-free house is security, but it pays no income, and at 67 you cannot rebuild the pension you gave up. Many later-life divorces end with the home being sold and the proceeds divided so both people can rehouse and keep meaningful pension income. If a transfer between you is ordered, the capital gains tax rules are forgiving: transfers under a court order are on a no-gain-no-loss basis without time limit."
  - question: "Does divorce cancel my will?"
    answer: "No, and this surprises people. Your will remains valid after divorce, but under section 18A of the Wills Act 1837 the final order changes how it operates: any gift to your former spouse fails, and any appointment of them as executor falls away, as if they had died on the day the divorce became final. Everything else in the will stands. That can produce a will with a hole in it, where the main gift fails and the estate falls to backup provisions you never intended to carry the load, or into intestacy if there are none. Between separation and the final order your existing will still operates in full, so a spouse you are divorcing could still inherit everything if you died mid-process. Reviewing your will at the start of a later-life divorce, not the end, is the safer sequence, alongside any pension death-benefit nominations."
  - question: "How does the April 2027 pension inheritance tax change affect divorce?"
    answer: "From 6 April 2027 most unused pension funds and death benefits are due to be brought within the value of the estate for inheritance tax, under the change announced by the government. Until then, unused pension pots usually pass outside the estate. For a later-life divorce this changes the arithmetic of who ends up holding pension wealth: a large retained pot is no longer automatically an IHT-efficient asset to die with, while the nil-rate band stays frozen at £325,000 until April 2031. It also raises the stakes on housekeeping: after a pension sharing order, both the original member and the recipient of the pension credit should refresh their expression-of-wish nominations, because pre-divorce nominations often still point at the ex-spouse, and the final order has already ended the IHT spouse exemption between you."
  - question: "Will my divorce settlement affect my Pension Credit or other benefits?"
    answer: "It can, in both directions. Pension Credit is means-tested: there is no hard capital cut-off, but savings above £10,000 are treated as producing £1 a week of income for every £500 held, so a lump sum from the settlement can reduce or end an award. A pension sharing order that gives you pension income of your own counts in the means test too. On the other side, becoming single can create entitlement you did not have as a couple, so it is worth a fresh benefits check after separation. Court fees have their own means test: under Help with Fees, the disposable capital limit rises from £4,250 to £16,000 once you or your partner are 66 or over, so many later-life applicants pay less than the £628 divorce application fee, or nothing."
  - question: "Do I lose widow's or widower's pension rights when the divorce is final?"
    answer: "Generally, yes. Most occupational schemes pay survivor pensions to a spouse, and an ex-spouse no longer qualifies once the final order is made. State bereavement support is also tied to being married or in a civil partnership at the date of death. This is one of the quiet costs of later-life divorce and one more reason sharing tends to beat offsetting: a pension credit from a sharing order is yours in your own right and does not depend on outliving anyone or staying married, whereas informal arrangements to rely on a former spouse's pension die with the marriage. If you are close to state pension age, get a state pension forecast and factor the loss of survivor benefits into the settlement negotiation rather than discovering it afterwards."

<p>Divorce after 60 has a different financial shape from divorce at 35. The average age at divorce in England and Wales has been climbing for two decades, according to <a href="https://www.ons.gov.uk/peoplepopulationandcommunity/birthsdeathsandmarriages/divorce">ONS divorce statistics</a>, and for these silver splitters the single largest asset in the pot is very often not the house but a pension, built over a 40-year working life. Late in life the settlement rules do not change, but the trade-offs do: there is less time to rebuild, income matters more than capital, and the estate-planning consequences run deeper.</p>

<p>This guide covers the five things that make a later-life divorce different: why the pension dominates, why sharing usually beats offsetting, the state pension's protected-payment rule, housing with a short mortgage runway, and the inheritance tax knock-ons, including the April 2027 change on pension death benefits. It applies to England and Wales. If you are in this position, our <a href="/for/over-50s">over-50s divorce hub</a> collects everything in one place.</p>

<h2>Why the pension dominates a later-life divorce</h2>

<p>Two things push pensions to the top of the asset list after 60. First, scale: decades of contributions, and for many older workers final-salary benefits, mean cash equivalent values that routinely exceed the equity in the family home. Second, the length of the marriage: after 25 or 30 years together, the court treats the whole pot as available to meet both people's needs, including pension built up before the wedding. The ring-fencing arguments that sometimes work in short marriages rarely bite here.</p>

<p>There is also less runway. A 40-year-old who gives up pension can rebuild it over a working lifetime; a 63-year-old cannot. That is why, in long-marriage cases near retirement, the focus shifts from splitting capital to equalising retirement income, so both people can actually live on the outcome. For the mechanics of valuing and dividing pensions, start with our <a href="/blog/pensions-and-divorce/pensions-and-divorce">guide to pensions and divorce</a> and the <a href="/pension-sharing">pension sharing hub</a>.</p>

<h2>Pension sharing vs offsetting: why sharing usually wins after 60</h2>

<p>There are two main routes to dealing with pensions, both under the <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1999/30/contents">Welfare Reform and Pensions Act 1999</a>. A pension sharing order transfers a percentage of one person's pension into a pension in the other's own name, and the scheme must implement it within a 4-month window. Offsetting leaves the pension alone and gives the other person more of something else, usually the house.</p>

<p>Offsetting depends on there being enough non-pension capital to trade, and on both people accepting a conversion rate between pension and cash that is genuinely contested even among experts. Near retirement it carries a second flaw: the person keeping the house has security but no income, and the person keeping the pension has income the other can never replace. Our detailed comparison, <a href="/blog/pensions-and-divorce/pension-sharing-vs-offsetting">pension sharing vs offsetting</a>, works through the numbers.</p>

<h2>State pension and divorce: the protected payment rule</h2>

<p>The state pension follows its own rulebook. If you reached state pension age on or after 6 April 2016, you are under the <a href="https://www.gov.uk/new-state-pension">new state pension</a>, and the standard amount cannot be shared or offset on divorce at all. The one exception is a <strong>protected payment</strong>: the excess some people receive above the full new state pension because of rights built up under the old system. A court can share a protected payment, and nothing else.</p>

<p>The new system also removed an old safety net: it is no longer possible to substitute an ex-spouse's National Insurance record to improve your own basic state pension, as some divorcing spouses could before April 2016. So an early practical step in any later-life divorce is a state pension forecast for each of you, plus a formal state pension valuation for the proceedings, so any protected payment is on the table rather than overlooked.</p>

<h2>The family home when the mortgage runway is short</h2>

<p>Keeping the home means finding the money to buy the other person out, and after 60 the lending maths tightens: affordability is assessed on pension income, mainstream lenders apply maximum age limits, and while later-life products such as retirement interest-only mortgages exist, the sums available are usually modest. Our guide to <a href="/blog/the-family-home/house-buyout-transfer-of-equity-divorce">house buyouts and transfers of equity</a> covers the mechanics.</p>

<p>The deeper trap is paying for the house with pension. Trading your share of a £500,000 pension for the other half of a £400,000 house feels like security, but a house pays no income, and equity release later is an expensive way to unwind the mistake. Many later-life divorces sensibly end with a sale and division, so both people rehouse modestly and keep pension income. Two tax points if property moves between you: transfers under a court order are treated as no gain, no loss for capital gains tax without time limit (otherwise the window is 3 tax years after separation), covered in our <a href="/capital-gains-tax-divorce">CGT and divorce hub</a>, and anyone buying a new home while still named on the old one can face the 5 percent additional-dwelling stamp duty surcharge. The government's overview of <a href="https://www.gov.uk/money-property-when-relationship-ends">dividing money and property when a relationship ends</a> sets out the framework.</p>

<h2>Divorce, your will and the April 2027 pension IHT change</h2>

<p>Later-life divorce rewires your estate planning in three ways. First, your will: divorce does not revoke it, but under <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/Will4and1Vict/7/26/section/18A">section 18A of the Wills Act 1837</a> the final order makes the will operate as if your ex-spouse had died, so gifts to them fail and their appointment as executor lapses. Until the final order, though, your existing will still works in full, which is why reviewing it belongs at the start of the process, not the end.</p>

<p>Second, inheritance tax. The spouse exemption ends with the final order, and the nil-rate band is frozen at £325,000 until April 2031, so wealth that would have passed tax-free between you as a married couple no longer does. Transfers made as part of the divorce settlement itself do not normally create an IHT problem, but everything after that is planned as a single person.</p>

<p>Third, pensions. From 6 April 2027, under the <a href="https://www.gov.uk/government/consultations/inheritance-tax-on-pensions-liability-reporting-and-payment">change announced by the government</a>, unused pension funds and death benefits are due to come within inheritance tax. That shifts the divorce arithmetic: a big retained pot stops being an automatically tax-efficient asset to die with, which slightly strengthens the case for sharing it now. And after any pension share, both people must refresh their death-benefit nominations, because old expression-of-wish forms pointing at an ex-spouse are a common and avoidable mess.</p>

<h2>Benefits, Pension Credit and your divorce settlement</h2>

<p>Means-tested support moves with the settlement. Pension Credit has no hard capital cut-off, but savings above £10,000 are deemed to produce £1 a week of income per £500 held, per the <a href="https://www.gov.uk/pension-credit/eligibility">gov.uk eligibility rules</a>, so a settlement lump sum can shrink an award, and a pension share that raises your own income counts too. Equally, becoming single can create new entitlement, so run a benefits check after separation. Court fees have their own relief: the Help with Fees capital limit jumps from £4,250 to £16,000 once you or your partner are 66 or over, which can cut the £628 application fee to nothing; see <a href="/blog/process-and-costs/help-with-divorce-fees-ex160">how the EX160 scheme works</a>.</p>

<p>Spousal maintenance also features more in later-life cases: after a long marriage with unequal earnings, ongoing maintenance or a larger capital share in lieu is common, and since post-1988 orders it is neither taxable income for the recipient nor deductible for the payer. Our <a href="/blog/maintenance-and-support/spousal-maintenance-guide">spousal maintenance guide</a> covers how amounts and terms are set.</p>

<h2>The later-life settlement checklist</h2>

<p>Everything above points the same way: after 60, income security beats headline capital, and paperwork left undone costs more. A pension sharing order can only be made by a court, so even a fully agreed later-life divorce needs a financial order; a <a href="/blog/process-and-costs/consent-orders-and-clean-break-orders">consent order</a> makes the deal binding for a £62 court fee and closes off future claims.</p>
