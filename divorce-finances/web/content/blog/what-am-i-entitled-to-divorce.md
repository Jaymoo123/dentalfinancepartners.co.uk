faqs:
  - question: "Is my spouse automatically entitled to half of everything in a divorce?"
    answer: "No, and this is the most common misunderstanding on both sides. Half is the starting point for the assets built up during the marriage, not a guaranteed outcome for everything either of you owns. The court begins at equality for the marital pot, then moves away from it wherever needs demand: the parent housing the children may need more than half, and after a short marriage the pot itself may be small because pre-marriage assets stay outside it. Equally, being the higher earner does not entitle you to keep more. The realistic answer is: half of the marital assets as a baseline, adjusted up or down by needs, marriage length and what counts as marital property."
  - question: "My husband had an affair. Am I entitled to more of the money?"
    answer: "Almost certainly not, and it is better to know that before you spend money arguing it. Divorce in England and Wales has been no-fault since April 2022, and even before that the courts almost never adjusted the financial split for adultery or general bad behaviour. Conduct only affects the money in rare and extreme cases, such as serious financial misconduct like deliberately destroying or hiding assets, or violence that has affected the victim's earning capacity. Painful as an affair is, the court's job is to meet both people's needs and divide what was built up during the marriage, and it does that the same way whether the marriage ended through an affair or simply drifted apart."
  - question: "We were only married three years with no children. What am I entitled to?"
    answer: "In a short childless marriage the outcome usually looks very different from the classic half-of-everything picture. The sharing principle still applies, but it bites mainly on what you built up together during those three years: savings from joint earnings, growth in a jointly owned home, pension contributions made while married. Assets each of you brought into the marriage, and kept separate, tend to go back out with the person who brought them. If you earn similar amounts and can each rehouse yourselves, a clean break with each keeping their own assets plus a share of the joint ones is a common outcome. If one of you gave up work or relocated for the marriage, needs can still pull the outcome away from a simple unwind."
  - question: "I gave up my career to raise our children. Does that reduce what I get?"
    answer: "No. If anything, it strengthens your position. The law treats contributions as a homemaker and parent as equal to financial contributions, so the years you spent raising children count exactly as much as the years your spouse spent earning. On top of that, the court's first consideration is the welfare of any children under 18, and your needs will reflect the reality that your earning capacity and pension have taken the hit while your spouse's carried on growing. In practice that often means the primary carer receives more than half of the capital to secure housing, a pension sharing order to repair the retirement gap, and sometimes spousal maintenance while they rebuild earnings."
  - question: "Am I entitled to a share of my ex's pension?"
    answer: "Yes, pensions built up during the marriage are firmly part of the pot, and after the family home they are often the biggest asset in it. Under the Welfare Reform and Pensions Act 1999 the court can make a pension sharing order, which carves out a percentage of one spouse's pension and moves it into a pension in your own name, with the scheme given a 4-month window to implement the transfer once it takes effect. The alternative is offsetting, where you keep more of another asset, often the house, in exchange for leaving the pension alone. Pensions are routinely missed in DIY settlements, which is one of the most expensive mistakes people make."
  - question: "The house and savings are all in my spouse's name. Do I still get a share?"
    answer: "Yes. Whose name an asset is held in makes very little difference in a divorce. The court looks at what the assets are and what each of you needs, not at the label on the account or the title deeds. A family home owned solely by one spouse is still treated as a matrimonial asset, savings accumulated from one salary during the marriage are still marital savings, and a pension in one name is still shareable. Both of you must give full financial disclosure of everything you hold, in either or joint names, usually on Form E, before a fair settlement can be reached."
  - question: "Am I entitled to spousal maintenance, and will I pay tax on it?"
    answer: "You may be, but it is not automatic. Spousal maintenance exists to meet a genuine income need that the capital split cannot cover, typically after a longer marriage where one person's earning capacity is much weaker. Courts increasingly prefer a clean break where the finances allow it, so maintenance tends to be for a defined term rather than for life, and many settlements have none at all. On tax, the position is simple: for post-1988 orders, spousal maintenance is not taxable income for the person receiving it and not tax-deductible for the person paying it. Child maintenance is separate again and follows the CMS formula rather than the divorce settlement."
  - question: "Does my inheritance have to be shared in the divorce?"
    answer: "Not automatically. Inheritances, gifts from family and assets you owned before the marriage are non-matrimonial property, and the sharing principle does not apply to them in the way it applies to what you built up together. But there are two big caveats. First, if needs cannot be met from the marital assets alone, the court can and will reach into non-matrimonial property; needs beat the matrimonial label. Second, if you mingled the inheritance with family finances, for example by putting it into the family home or a joint account, it may have become matrimonial along the way. Keeping inherited money separate matters if you want to keep it out of the pot."

<p>Half of the marital pot is the honest starting point for what you are entitled to in a divorce: there is no fixed list and no formula written into the law. In England and Wales the court starts from an equal division of everything built up during the marriage, then adjusts that 50/50 starting point until both people's needs are met, with housing for any children put first. So the fuller answer to "what am I entitled to" is: half of the marital pot as a baseline, more than half if your needs demand it, and less if your former spouse's needs are greater.</p>

<p>This article is about the outcome: what the split actually looks like in different situations, and the myths that push people into bad deals. The process for getting there, disclosure, negotiation and court orders, is covered step by step in our <a href="/blog/financial-settlements/divorce-financial-settlement-guide">divorce financial settlement guide</a>. Because the outcome turns on the size of your pot, the income gap and the children, a generic answer only goes so far: the <a href="/calculators/settlement-range-estimator">settlement range estimator</a> turns your own assets, incomes and marriage length into a realistic bracket for your case, which is far more useful than a headline percentage before you negotiate or pay for advice.</p>

<h2>The 50/50 starting point in a divorce settlement</h2>

<p>The legal framework is <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1973/18/section/25">section 25 of the Matrimonial Causes Act 1973</a>, which tells the court to consider all the circumstances: each person's income and earning capacity, needs and obligations, the standard of living during the marriage, its length, your ages, contributions made and to come, and any disability. The first consideration is always the welfare of children under 18.</p>

<p>Since the House of Lords decision in White v White in 2000, the courts check any proposed outcome against a "yardstick of equality": assets built up during the marriage are divided equally unless there is a good reason to depart from that. Crucially, there is no discrimination between the breadwinner and the homemaker. The years spent raising children and running the home count equally with the years spent earning, so the higher earner does not keep more simply because the money came through their payslip.</p>

<h2>Needs trump equality in a financial settlement</h2>

<p>Equality is where the analysis starts, not where most cases finish. In the majority of divorces the assets are modest enough that dividing them exactly in half would leave one household, usually the one with the children, unable to rehouse. When that happens, needs win. As the official guidance on <a href="https://www.gov.uk/money-property-when-relationship-ends">money and property when a relationship ends</a> makes clear, the aim is a fair outcome, not a mechanical split.</p>

<p>An illustration of the mechanics. A couple have a home with £250,000 of equity, £30,000 of savings and two children aged 6 and 9 who will live mainly with one parent. That parent earns £22,000 and can borrow little; the other earns £55,000 with real mortgage capacity. Half of the pot each would leave the children's main home unaffordable. A court, or any sensible negotiation, will push capital towards the lower earner, perhaps 60 to 70 percent of the equity, because that is what it takes to house the children, while the higher earner rebuilds through borrowing power and income. The percentages are not a rule; they are the output of the housing arithmetic, which is why running your own numbers matters more than quoting averages.</p>

<h2>Short marriage vs long marriage: what changes in a divorce</h2>

<p>Marriage length changes both what goes into the pot and how it is shared. Note that the clock includes any period you lived together seamlessly before the wedding, which can turn an apparently short marriage into a long one.</p>

<h2>Matrimonial vs non-matrimonial assets in a financial settlement</h2>

<p>Not everything either of you owns sits inside the sharing pot. Matrimonial property is what you built up during the marriage: the family home, savings from earnings, pensions accrued while married. Non-matrimonial property is what came from outside it: inheritances, gifts from family, and assets owned before the wedding. Sharing applies with full force to the first category and only weakly to the second.</p>

<p>Two caveats stop this being a magic shield. Needs beat the label: if the marital assets cannot meet both people's needs, the court will reach into inherited or pre-marriage wealth. And mingling converts: an inheritance paid into the family home or a joint account tends to become matrimonial over time. The family home itself is nearly always treated as matrimonial whatever its history. If protecting pre-marriage or family wealth matters to you, that is precisely what <a href="/blog/financial-settlements/prenuptial-postnuptial-agreements">prenuptial and postnuptial agreements</a> are designed to do.</p>

<h2>The family home, pensions and maintenance: the three big entitlements</h2>

<p>Most settlements are really about three assets, and each has its own mechanics.</p>

<h3>The family home</h3>

<p>The usual options are sale and division of the proceeds, one person buying the other out, or deferred sale while the children grow up. Who keeps the house is a needs question first and a fairness question second, and the buyout maths, mortgage capacity, and transfer mechanics are covered in our guide to a <a href="/blog/the-family-home/house-buyout-transfer-of-equity-divorce">house buyout and transfer of equity</a>.</p>

<h3>Pensions and divorce</h3>

<p>Pensions built up during the marriage are shareable, and they are the asset most often missed in DIY deals. Under the <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1999/30/contents">Welfare Reform and Pensions Act 1999</a> the court can make a pension sharing order, moving a percentage of one spouse's pension into a pension in the other's name, with a 4-month implementation window once the order takes effect. The alternative, offsetting the pension against the house, has real traps, which our guide to <a href="/blog/pensions-and-divorce/pensions-and-divorce">pensions and divorce</a> works through with numbers.</p>

<h3>Maintenance</h3>

<p>Spousal maintenance is an income top-up where the capital split cannot meet a genuine need, most often after long marriages with a big earning gap. It is never automatic, courts lean towards clean breaks where finances allow, and for post-1988 orders it is not taxable for the recipient and not deductible for the payer. See the <a href="/blog/maintenance-and-support/spousal-maintenance-guide">spousal maintenance guide</a> for how amounts and terms are set. Child maintenance sits outside the settlement entirely and follows the <a href="/blog/maintenance-and-support/child-maintenance-cms-formula">CMS formula</a>.</p>

<h2>Divorce entitlement myths that cost people money</h2>

<ul>
  <li><strong>"They cheated, so I get more."</strong> No. Divorce is no-fault, and adultery or general bad behaviour does not change the financial split. Conduct only counts in rare, extreme cases such as deliberately hiding or destroying assets.</li>
  <li><strong>"Everything is automatically 50/50."</strong> No. Equality is the starting point for marital assets, not a rule for everything either of you owns, and needs routinely move the outcome away from half.</li>
  <li><strong>"It is in my name, so it is mine."</strong> No. The court looks straight through whose name assets are held in. Full disclosure of everything, usually on <a href="/blog/process-and-costs/form-e-financial-disclosure-guide">Form E</a>, comes before any fair deal.</li>
  <li><strong>"The mother always gets the house."</strong> No. Housing follows the children's needs and the parents' resources, not gender.</li>
  <li><strong>"Once the divorce is final, the money is settled."</strong> No. Financial claims stay open after the final order unless a court seals them shut.</li>
</ul>

<h2>Turning your divorce entitlement into a binding settlement</h2>

<p>Whatever split you agree, it is worth nothing until a judge seals it in a consent order, which costs a court fee of £62 and can include a clean break dismissing all future claims between you. Skipping that step leaves the door open for an ex-spouse to claim against future earnings, inheritances or pensions years later. How these orders work, and why the £62 is the best money in the whole process, is covered in <a href="/blog/process-and-costs/consent-orders-and-clean-break-orders">consent orders and clean break orders</a>.</p>
