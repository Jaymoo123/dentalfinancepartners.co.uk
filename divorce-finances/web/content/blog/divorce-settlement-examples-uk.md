faqs:
  - question: "Are these divorce settlement examples real cases?"
    answer: "No. Every scenario in this guide is an invented composite, built to illustrate how the section 25 factors interact rather than to report any real family's finances. Financial remedy hearings in England and Wales are usually held in private and the parties are normally anonymised in any published judgment, so worked examples of everyday cases are constructed rather than reported. Where a genuine reported case is mentioned, such as White v White in 2000 or Miller and McFarlane in 2006, it is named and the principle it established is described accurately. Treat the numbers here as illustrations of reasoning, not as predictions for your own case, because a small change in mortgage capacity, pension type or the age of the children can move an outcome a long way."
  - question: "Do courts always split assets 50/50 in a divorce?"
    answer: "No. Equal sharing is a yardstick rather than a starting rule, and it applies most strongly to assets built up during the marriage in a long marriage. The court can depart from equality where one party's needs cannot otherwise be met, which is common in modest-asset cases where a parent has to rehouse children, and it can also depart where a significant asset was owned before the marriage or inherited and has been kept separate. In big-money cases the direction of travel is different again, because once needs are comfortably covered the argument turns on sharing and on what counts as matrimonial property. In practice, the smaller the asset pot the more likely needs will override equality entirely."
  - question: "What if my spouse earned all the money and I raised the children?"
    answer: "The law does not treat earning as more valuable than caring. In White v White the House of Lords held in 2000 that there should be no discrimination between the roles of breadwinner and homemaker, and that a judge should check any proposed division against the yardstick of equality. In Miller and McFarlane in 2006 the court set out three strands running through financial settlements: needs, compensation for relationship-generated disadvantage, and sharing. A career interrupted by childcare is exactly the kind of disadvantage the compensation strand recognises, and in practice it usually shows up as a larger capital share, a pension share, or spousal maintenance for a defined term while earning capacity is rebuilt. Our guide to what you are entitled to on divorce sets out the framework in more depth."
  - question: "What happens in a divorce settlement if the house is in negative equity?"
    answer: "The exercise flips from dividing capital to allocating liabilities and planning housing. A court can decide who lives in the property, who pays the mortgage in the meantime, when it is sold and how any shortfall is shared between you, and it will look at debts alongside assets under section 25. What it cannot do is change who the lender can pursue: a joint mortgage stays joint until it is redeemed or the lender agrees to release one party, so an order saying your ex will pay does not protect your credit file if they stop. That is why negative-equity settlements normally pair a clear payment obligation with a firm sale or remortgage trigger date, and why child maintenance is dealt with separately through the Child Maintenance Service regardless of who holds the debt."
  - question: "How is a pension included in a divorce settlement?"
    answer: "Pensions are matrimonial property in England and Wales and can be divided by a pension sharing order, which moves a percentage of one party's pension into a pension in the other's name. Pension sharing was introduced by the Welfare Reform and Pensions Act 1999 and is available for divorce proceedings started on or after 1 December 2000, and once the order takes effect the scheme has a 4-month implementation period to put the share in place. Only the protected-payment element of the new state pension can be shared. The percentage is often set to equalise capital values, but in later-life cases it may instead be set to equalise the income each party will draw, which can produce a very different figure, particularly with defined benefit schemes where the cash equivalent value can understate what the pension is really worth."
  - question: "How long does it take to get a financial settlement approved?"
    answer: "The divorce itself has a minimum timetable of 26 weeks: a 20-week reflection period from application to conditional order, then a 6-week-and-1-day wait to the final order, under the Divorce, Dissolution and Separation Act 2020 which came into force on 6 April 2022. The finances run on their own track. An agreed settlement is written up as a consent order and lodged with the court for a judge to approve, which costs £62, and it can be submitted once the conditional order has been made although it only takes effect from the final order. A contested application starts with Form A at a fee of £321 and typically takes many months longer, because it moves through a first appointment, a financial dispute resolution hearing and, if nothing settles, a final hearing."
  - question: "What if we agree everything ourselves? Do we still need a court order?"
    answer: "An informal agreement, even one written down and signed, does not end the financial claims either of you can bring. Only a financial order approved by the court does that, and without one a claim can surface years later, including after one of you has built up new assets. The practical route for people who have agreed terms is a consent order recording the split, with a clean break clause dismissing future claims where that is appropriate, lodged at a fee of £62 with a short statement of information about both parties' finances. The judge is not a rubber stamp and can reject terms that look unfair or that leave one party unable to meet basic needs, so agreements that ignore a pension or leave a parent unable to rehouse children are often sent back."
  - question: "Does the same approach apply in Scotland?"
    answer: "No, and the difference is significant enough to change outcomes. This guide covers England and Wales. Scotland applies the Family Law (Scotland) Act 1985, under which matrimonial property is generally limited to assets acquired during the marriage, excluding most gifts and inheritances, and is normally shared fairly, which usually means equally. That narrower definition means a property or pension built up before the marriage is far more likely to be excluded than in England and Wales, and pension values are usually apportioned to the marriage period. Post-divorce spousal support in Scotland is also more restricted, being generally aimed at a period of adjustment of up to 3 years rather than the open-ended or long-term orders possible south of the border. Northern Ireland has its own separate rules."

<p>There is no formula for a divorce settlement in England and Wales. <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1973/18/section/25">Section 25 of the Matrimonial Causes Act 1973</a> gives the court a checklist of factors (income and earning capacity, financial needs, the standard of living, ages and length of marriage, contributions, and conduct in rare cases), with the welfare of any child under 18 as the <strong>first consideration</strong>. Equal sharing is a yardstick, not a rule, and the five worked scenarios below show where and why judges depart from it.</p>

<p>All five are invented composites built for this guide, not reports of real cases, and the figures are illustrative. Before reading, it is worth running your own numbers through our <a href="/calculators/settlement-range-estimator">settlement range estimator</a>, because the examples make far more sense when you can see where your own asset pot, marriage length and childcare position sit against them. For the underlying framework rather than the worked outcomes, see <a href="/blog/financial-settlements/what-am-i-entitled-to-divorce">what am I entitled to in a divorce</a>.</p>

<h2>Example 1: a short childless marriage, where the divorce settlement mostly unwinds</h2>

<p>Nadia and Callum married for 3 years, no children, both in their thirties and both working (£38,000 and £42,000). Callum owned the flat before they met, with £70,000 of equity at the date of marriage and £95,000 now. Joint savings of £14,000 accumulated during the marriage, and each built roughly £12,000 of pension over the same period.</p>

<p>Outcome: the flat stays with Callum, the £14,000 of savings is split equally, each keeps their own pension, and a clean break dismisses future claims. Nadia also receives £5,000 towards a rental deposit and moving costs.</p>

<p>The reasoning is that a short marriage with no children and two working adults gives the court little to share and no ongoing needs to meet. The flat is non-matrimonial in origin and its growth was not generated by the marriage, so the sharing principle barely touches it. Needs can still override that, which is why the moving-costs payment exists, but with both parties able to house themselves on their own incomes there is no basis for more.</p>

<h2>Example 2: a long marriage, children and modest equity in the family home</h2>

<p>Femi and Marguerite, married 22 years, two children aged 14 and 11. Home worth £310,000 with a £130,000 mortgage, so £180,000 of equity. Femi earns £46,000 with a £140,000 defined contribution pension, Marguerite works part time on £19,000 with a £28,000 pension. Total pot around £348,000.</p>

<p>Outcome: the children stay in the home with Marguerite, who takes over the mortgage at a reduced level, subject to a deferred charge giving Femi 25% of the net proceeds (about £45,000 on today's figures) when the younger child turns 18 or Marguerite remarries or sells. A pension sharing order transfers 20% of Femi's pension, about £28,000, to her. She ends with roughly £191,000 of value, he ends with roughly £157,000, so about 55/45 in her favour.</p>

<p>Why not 50/50? Because the children's housing is the first consideration and Marguerite's borrowing capacity on £19,000 will not stretch to rehousing them elsewhere. The deferred charge is the compromise that lets the court prioritise housing now without writing off Femi's share permanently. The mechanics of deferred sale orders are covered in our guide to <a href="/blog/the-family-home/mesher-orders-deferred-house-sale">Mesher orders and deferred house sales</a>.</p>

<h2>Example 3: a single-earner household, needs and spousal maintenance</h2>

<p>Dermot and Bernadette, married 16 years, children aged 9 and 6. Dermot earns £95,000, Bernadette has not worked since the first child was born. Home worth £420,000 with a £150,000 mortgage (£270,000 equity), Dermot's pension £210,000. Pot around £480,000.</p>

<p>Outcome: the home transfers to Bernadette with the mortgage restructured, so she takes £270,000 of the £480,000 (about 56%), Dermot retains his pension and rehouses on his income. Spousal maintenance of £1,100 a month runs on a stepped term of 5 years while Bernadette returns to work, with a bar on extending it. Child maintenance runs separately through the Child Maintenance Service.</p>

<p>On CMS <a href="https://www.gov.uk/how-child-maintenance-is-worked-out">gross weekly income</a> of about £900 after pension contributions, with 2 children, the basic rate of <strong>16%</strong> applies to the first £800 (£128) and the basic-plus rate of <strong>12%</strong> to the £100 above it (£12), giving £140 a week before any shared-care reduction. On Collect and Pay that would cost Dermot <strong>+20%</strong> (£168) and leave Bernadette <strong>4%</strong> lighter (£134.40), which is why most separated parents use Direct Pay, free to both. Our <a href="/blog/maintenance-and-support/child-maintenance-cms-formula">CMS formula guide</a> works through the reductions.</p>

<p>This is the shape White v White (2000) and Miller and McFarlane (2006) produce in a mid-money case: no discrimination between the earner and the carer, and recognition that an interrupted career is a disadvantage the settlement should address. The transfer of the home between spouses falls within the <strong>no gain, no loss</strong> capital gains treatment that applies for up to 3 tax years after the tax year of separation, or without limit where the transfer is made under a formal separation agreement or court order.</p>

<h2>Example 4: a later-life divorce where the pension outweighs the family home</h2>

<p>Tomasz (62) and Lorna (59), married 31 years. House owned outright at £340,000, ISAs of £60,000, Lorna's pension £45,000, and Tomasz's defined benefit scheme with a cash equivalent value of £620,000. Pot around £1.065m, of which two thirds is pension.</p>

<p>Outcome on a capital-equalising basis: Lorna keeps the house, Tomasz takes the ISAs, and a pension sharing order transfers about 24% of his cash equivalent value (roughly £147,500) to Lorna, leaving each with about £532,500.</p>

<p>The complication is that a cash equivalent value can understate a defined benefit pension, because it is a transfer price rather than a measure of the guaranteed, inflation-linked income the scheme pays. A pensions on divorce expert may recommend equalising income in retirement instead of capital, which in a case like this can push the share well above 24%. Once made, the order takes 4 months for the scheme to implement. See <a href="/blog/pensions-and-divorce/pension-sharing-vs-offsetting">pension sharing versus offsetting</a> and our guide to <a href="/blog/pensions-and-divorce/later-life-divorce-silver-splitters">later-life divorce</a>.</p>

<h2>Example 5: negative equity and debt, where the financial settlement allocates liabilities</h2>

<p>Sunita and Gareth, married 7 years, one child aged 5. Flat worth £168,000 against a £182,000 mortgage, so £14,000 of negative equity. Credit cards and a car loan totalling £31,000, mostly in Gareth's name but spent on the household. Combined pensions £16,000. Sunita earns £24,000, Gareth £41,000.</p>

<p>Outcome: Sunita and the child stay in the flat, with Gareth contributing to the mortgage until it can be remortgaged or sold. The debts are allocated 60/40 to Gareth, reflecting his higher income and repayment capacity, and any sale shortfall is shared equally. No clean break yet, with a nominal spousal maintenance order left open until the housing position is resolved.</p>

<p>The point of this scenario is that with no capital to divide, section 25 becomes an exercise in allocating liabilities and sequencing decisions. It is also where people are most often caught out: a court order that says Gareth will pay does not release Sunita from a joint mortgage, and the lender can still pursue either of them. Our guide to a <a href="/blog/the-family-home/joint-mortgage-after-divorce">joint mortgage after divorce</a> covers release and remortgage in detail.</p>

<p>Three patterns run through all five. Short marriages tend to unwind towards each party's starting position; long marriages are treated as a joint enterprise regardless of who earned the money; and needs, particularly housing for children, beat equality whenever the pot is too small to satisfy both. A fourth pattern is procedural: none of these outcomes binds anyone until a judge approves it as a financial order, which costs <strong>£62</strong> for an uncontested consent order by agreement, against <strong>£321</strong> to start a contested application on Form A. Big-money cases follow a different logic once needs are comfortably met, which our guide to <a href="/blog/financial-settlements/high-net-worth-divorce-duxbury">high net worth divorce and Duxbury</a> covers.</p>

<p>These examples are illustrations of judicial reasoning, not predictions, and every one of them would move if the children were older, the mortgage capacity different or the pension a different type. What they should give you is a sense of which factor is likely to dominate in your own case, and therefore where to concentrate your disclosure and your negotiating effort.
