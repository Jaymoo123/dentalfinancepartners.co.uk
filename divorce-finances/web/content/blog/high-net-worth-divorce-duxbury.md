faqs:
  - question: "What is a Duxbury calculation in a divorce?"
    answer: "A Duxbury calculation converts an ongoing spousal maintenance need into a single lump sum, so the paying party can achieve a clean break instead of paying maintenance for years. It comes from the 1987 case of Duxbury v Duxbury, where an accountant modelled the capital sum that, invested and drawn down over the recipient's expected lifetime, would meet their annual income need, allowing for investment returns, inflation and tax. The resulting Duxbury tables are published for practitioners and cross-reference the recipient's age against the net annual income required. Judges describe the tables as a tool and not a rule: they are the usual starting point for capitalising maintenance, but the court can and does depart from them where the assumptions do not fit the case."
  - question: "Why are the Duxbury tables criticised?"
    answer: "Three criticisms come up repeatedly. First, the model assumes a single set of investment returns, inflation and mortality assumptions for everyone, when real portfolios and real lives vary enormously. Second, the fund is designed to amortise to nothing at the end of the recipient's assumed life expectancy, which means someone who lives longer than the tables predict can run out, while someone who dies earlier leaves a windfall. Third is the Duxbury paradox: because a younger recipient has more years to fund, a short marriage to a young spouse can generate a larger capitalised sum than a long marriage to an older one, which sits awkwardly with fairness. A working party of family judges and practitioners published a final report in 2024 recommending refreshed economic assumptions and clearer guidance on when the tables should not be relied on, and courts increasingly stress-test Duxbury figures rather than applying them mechanically."
  - question: "My spouse's wealth is held in a family trust. Can the divorce court touch it?"
    answer: "Often, yes, though the route depends on the trust. If the trust is a nuptial settlement, meaning it was made in contemplation of or during the marriage and makes some form of provision for one or both spouses, the court has a specific power under s.24(1)(c) of the Matrimonial Causes Act 1973 to vary it, which can include transferring assets out of the trust to the other spouse. If the trust is not nuptial, the court cannot rewrite it, but it can treat your spouse's interest as a financial resource under s.25 if the reality is that the trustees would advance funds if asked, and it can frame orders that give the trustees judicious encouragement to do so. Full disclosure of trust interests is required on Form E either way, and offshore structures do not remove the court's jurisdiction over the spouses themselves."
  - question: "I built my company before we married. Does my ex-spouse get half of it?"
    answer: "Not automatically. A business you built before the marriage is non-matrimonial property in principle, and the Supreme Court confirmed in Standish v Standish in 2025 that the sharing principle applies only to matrimonial property. But two things can still bring value into play. First, matrimonialisation: if the business, or its proceeds, were treated as a shared family asset over time, some or all of the value may have become matrimonial, and growth in value during the marriage is often treated differently from the value you brought in. Second, needs: even genuinely non-matrimonial wealth can be invaded if the other spouse's housing and income needs cannot be met from the matrimonial pot. In most cases the pre-marital element is identified, sometimes with accountancy evidence, and the sharing exercise is run on the rest."
  - question: "How are share options and deferred bonuses treated in a divorce settlement?"
    answer: "The court looks at when the value was earned, not just when it pays out. Options and awards that vested during the marriage are usually matrimonial. Unvested awards are commonly apportioned, with the part attributable to work during the marriage shared and the part attributable to post-separation effort retained. Because unvested awards are risky, they are often divided in specie, an approach known as Wells sharing after a 2002 case: instead of guessing a value today, each party receives a proportion of the awards if and when they vest, so both carry the same market and employment risk. Tax matters too, since exercising options usually triggers income tax and possibly national insurance, so settlements should share net-of-tax value rather than headline numbers."
  - question: "We agreed maintenance, but my ex has offered a lump sum instead. Should I take it?"
    answer: "A capitalised lump sum buys certainty and a clean break: no monthly dependence on your ex-spouse, no risk of their income falling, and no future variation applications by either side. The trade-offs are real, though. Once capitalised, the sum is fixed: if you live longer than the Duxbury assumptions, your investments return less than the model assumed, or your circumstances change, you cannot go back for more, whereas ongoing maintenance can be varied up as well as down. Remember also that spousal maintenance is not taxable income in the recipient's hands and not deductible for the payer, so the comparison is between net figures on both sides. The right answer depends on the payer's covenant, your age, your attitude to investment risk and how robust the underlying calculation is, which is exactly where independent advice on the Duxbury figure earns its fee."
  - question: "Can a high net worth divorce be kept private?"
    answer: "Largely, yes, with limits. Financial remedy hearings in England and Wales are heard in private, and where judgments are published they are usually anonymised so the parties are not named, although reported appeal cases are sometimes public. Under the transparency arrangements rolled out through the Financial Remedies Court, accredited journalists may attend and report subject to reporting orders that typically protect the parties' identities and sensitive financial detail. Couples who want stronger confidentiality often keep the whole dispute out of court: private financial dispute resolution hearings and arbitration are confidential by design, and a negotiated agreement turned into a consent order keeps the financial detail off any public record. What you cannot do is use privacy to avoid disclosure: the duty of full and frank disclosure applies however discreet the process."

<p>In a high net worth divorce in England and Wales, the court's starting point for wealth built up during the marriage is an equal division, a principle established by the House of Lords in White v White in 2000. And when ongoing spousal maintenance is converted into a one-off lump sum, the figure usually comes from the <strong>Duxbury tables</strong>, an actuarial model first used in the 1987 case of Duxbury v Duxbury and now the subject of serious criticism.</p>

<p>Big-money cases turn on machinery that ordinary divorces never touch: capitalised maintenance, trusts, share schemes, and the boundary between matrimonial and non-matrimonial wealth. This guide explains each mechanism and where the real arguments happen. Because outcomes in this bracket are ranges shaped by sharing and needs rather than formulas, the <a href="/calculators/settlement-range-estimator">settlement range estimator</a> is a useful first step: it shows how the size and mix of your assets pull a realistic settlement range before you spend money on advice.</p>

<h2>Duxbury tables: turning spousal maintenance into a lump sum</h2>

<p>A Duxbury calculation answers one question: what capital sum, invested sensibly and drawn down over the recipient's expected lifetime, would meet their annual income need? The tables cross-reference the recipient's age against the net income required, using assumed rates of investment return, inflation and life expectancy, and produce a lump sum that amortises to nothing at the end of the assumed term.</p>

<p>The point of capitalising is the clean break. Instead of paying <a href="/blog/maintenance-and-support/spousal-maintenance-guide">spousal maintenance</a> for years, with the variation applications and ongoing entanglement that brings, the paying party hands over capital once and both parties move on. Note the tax shape of the comparison: spousal maintenance is not taxable income for the recipient and not deductible for the payer, so a Duxbury figure is built on net income needs.</p>

<h2>Why the Duxbury maintenance calculation is under fire</h2>

<p>Judges have long called Duxbury a tool, not a rule, and the criticism has sharpened. The model applies one set of economic and mortality assumptions to everyone. It assumes the fund is exhausted precisely at assumed life expectancy, so a recipient who outlives the tables can run out of money. And it produces the well-known <strong>Duxbury paradox</strong>: a younger recipient needs a larger fund to cover more years, so a short marriage to a young spouse can generate a bigger capitalised award than a long marriage to an older one.</p>

<p>A working party of family judges and practitioners re-examined the model and published a final report in 2024, recommending refreshed assumptions and clearer guidance on the tables' limits. In practice, well-advised parties now stress-test any Duxbury figure against real portfolio returns, annuity pricing and the recipient's actual health and circumstances rather than accepting the table figure mechanically.</p>

<h2>Sharing vs needs: how the court frames a high net worth financial settlement</h2>

<p>Every financial settlement is decided under <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1973/18/section/25">section 25 of the Matrimonial Causes Act 1973</a>, and the case law organises the exercise into three strands: needs, compensation and sharing. In most divorces, needs consume everything and the analysis stops there. High net worth cases are different: once both parties' housing and income needs are met with room to spare, the <strong>sharing principle</strong> takes over, and the argument becomes what is in the matrimonial pot to be shared, usually equally.</p>

<h2>Pre-acquired and non-matrimonial property in a divorce</h2>

<p>Non-matrimonial property is wealth that does not derive from the parties' joint endeavour: assets built before the marriage, inheritances, and gifts from third parties. Since Miller and McFarlane in 2006, the courts have distinguished it from matrimonial property, and in 2025 the Supreme Court in <strong>Standish v Standish</strong> confirmed the position squarely: the sharing principle applies only to matrimonial property. Standish also clarified matrimonialisation, holding that whether non-matrimonial wealth has become matrimonial depends on how the couple treated it over time, not simply whose name it ended up in; a transfer between spouses made for tax planning did not, by itself, convert separate wealth into shared wealth.</p>

<p>In practice the fights are forensic: tracing what was brought in, separating pre-marital value from growth during the marriage, and showing whether inherited money was kept separate or poured into the family home. This is one reason <a href="/blog/process-and-costs/form-e-financial-disclosure-guide">Form E disclosure</a> matters so much in this bracket, and why <a href="/blog/financial-settlements/prenuptial-postnuptial-agreements">prenuptial and postnuptial agreements</a> are the standard tool for protecting pre-acquired wealth before the argument ever starts.</p>

<h2>Trusts in divorce: nuptial settlements and variation</h2>

<p>Trusts do not put wealth beyond the court's reach. If a trust is a <strong>nuptial settlement</strong>, made in contemplation of or during the marriage and providing for one or both spouses in their capacity as spouses, the court has power under <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1973/18/section/24">section 24 of the Matrimonial Causes Act 1973</a> to vary it, up to and including transferring trust assets to the other spouse.</p>

<p>Where a trust is not nuptial, the court cannot rewrite it, but it can treat a spouse's interest as a financial resource if the evidence shows the trustees would in reality advance funds on request, and it can structure awards to give trustees what the reported cases call judicious encouragement to do so. Offshore trustees may decline to submit to the English court, but the spouses themselves remain fully within its jurisdiction, and every trust interest must be disclosed.</p>

<h2>Share options and deferred compensation in the financial settlement</h2>

<p>Executive pay rarely arrives as simple salary. Options, restricted stock, deferred bonuses and carried interest all raise the same two questions: when was the value earned, and how should risk be shared? Awards vested during the marriage are usually matrimonial. Unvested awards are commonly time-apportioned, with the slice attributable to work during the marriage shared and the post-separation slice retained.</p>

<p>Because unvested awards can evaporate, courts often prefer <strong>Wells sharing</strong>, dividing the awards in specie so each party receives their proportion if and when vesting happens, rather than fixing a speculative value today. Settlements should also work in net-of-tax numbers, since exercise and vesting usually trigger income tax. Capital assets transferred between the parties benefit from the no gain, no loss window for separating spouses under <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1992/12/section/58">section 58 TCGA 1992</a>, which runs for 3 tax years after the separation year and without time limit under a court order; the <a href="/capital-gains-tax-divorce">capital gains tax and divorce hub</a> covers the detail. Pensions have their own sharing machinery, compared in <a href="/blog/pensions-and-divorce/pension-sharing-vs-offsetting">pension sharing vs offsetting</a>.</p>

<h2>Privacy in a high net worth divorce</h2>

<p>Financial remedy hearings are heard in private, and published judgments are usually anonymised, which is why the reported cases discuss principles rather than recognisable families. Accredited journalists may attend under the Financial Remedies Court transparency arrangements, subject to reporting orders that typically protect identities and sensitive financial detail. Parties who want certainty of confidentiality generally settle out of court altogether: private financial dispute resolution hearings and arbitration are confidential by design, and a negotiated deal embodied in a <a href="/blog/process-and-costs/consent-orders-and-clean-break-orders">consent order</a> keeps the numbers off any public record. Privacy never dilutes the duty of full and frank disclosure.</p>
