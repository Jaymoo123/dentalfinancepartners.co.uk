faqs:
  - question: "Is my inheritance protected in a divorce in England and Wales?"
    answer: "In principle yes, and in practice it depends on two things: whether you kept it separate, and whether the rest of the assets are enough to meet both of your needs. An inheritance is non-matrimonial property because it came from outside the marriage partnership rather than from joint effort, and the Supreme Court confirmed in Standish v Standish in 2025 that the sharing principle applies only to matrimonial property. So an inheritance sitting untouched in a sole account, clearly traceable to the estate it came from, has a strong claim to be excluded from sharing. Two things defeat that. If the money was mingled into joint accounts, joint investments or the family home, the court can treat it as matrimonialised and share it. And if the matrimonial assets alone cannot house and support both of you and any children, the court will invade the inheritance to meet those needs regardless of where it came from. Ring-fencing is a strong argument in a case with surplus assets and a weak one in a case where money is tight."
  - question: "What does mingling mean and how do I know if my inheritance has been mingled?"
    answer: "Mingling, which family lawyers call matrimonialisation, is the process by which money from outside the marriage becomes part of the shared pot because of the way the couple used it. The practical test is whether the inheritance can still be identified and traced, and whether the couple treated it as joint money. An inheritance received last year and left in a sole savings account is plainly separate. An inheritance paid into the joint current account in 2012, spent partly on holidays and school fees and partly on a kitchen extension, is no longer identifiable and will usually be treated as shared. Standish v Standish made clear that the test is how the parties actually treated the asset over time and what they intended, not whose name it happened to be in, so simply moving money into a sole account after the marriage broke down does not undo years of joint use. The longer the marriage and the more thoroughly the money was absorbed into family life, the weaker the ring-fence."
  - question: "I inherited £150,000 from my mother and we used it as the deposit on our house. Do I get it back?"
    answer: "Usually not as a separate slice, although it is worth arguing, and the answer turns heavily on the size of the pot. The family home is the asset courts are least willing to ring-fence, because it is the centre of family life and is normally treated as matrimonial property whatever its origins. Where the equity is needed to rehouse both of you and the children, the source of the deposit is close to irrelevant, and the money will simply be divided by reference to needs. Where there is more equity than the two of you strictly need, a documented deposit traceable to an inheritance is a real argument for an unequal division in your favour, and courts do sometimes credit it, particularly after a shorter marriage. What makes the difference is evidence: the completion statement, the bank transfer from the estate account and the probate paperwork. If the money went in during the marriage with no record, you are asking a judge to take your word for it."
  - question: "Can my ex claim against an inheritance I receive after the divorce?"
    answer: "Yes, unless the financial claims between you were dismissed by a court order. Ending the marriage with a final order does not end financial claims, and there is no time limit on bringing them, so an ex-spouse can in principle apply years later against money you inherited long after separation. Claims like that get harder to win as time passes and circumstances change, but defending even a weak application costs real money and takes months. The fix is straightforward and cheap: a clean break consent order, filed for a court fee of £62, dismisses each party's capital, property, pension and maintenance claims for good. If you divorced without one, and especially if you expect to inherit, getting a clean break order in place is one of the highest-value things you can do, and our guide to consent orders and clean break orders explains the process."
  - question: "Will the court take account of an inheritance I am expecting from a living parent?"
    answer: "Almost never. Under section 25(2)(a) of the Matrimonial Causes Act 1973 the court considers the resources each party has or is likely to have in the foreseeable future, and an inheritance from a living person fails that test in nearly every case: a will can be rewritten at any time, care costs can consume an estate, and nobody can put a date on it. Judges will not divide assets that may never arrive. There are rare exceptions in cases where a testator has already died, or where death is imminent and the entitlement is certain, and in those situations a court can occasionally adjourn a capital claim so it can be revisited once the estate is received rather than guessing now. Expectations can also feed indirectly into the picture: if one spouse is plainly going to be secure in later life and the other is not, that can influence how much of the current pot each of them needs, without the inheritance itself being counted."
  - question: "Do I have to disclose an inheritance I received after we separated?"
    answer: "Yes. The duty of full and frank disclosure runs until the final order is made, and it covers everything you own at the point you complete Form E, not just what you had on the date you separated. Failing to mention an inheritance received during proceedings is exactly the kind of non-disclosure that gets a settlement set aside later, and it is easily discovered through the bank statements the form requires anyway. Disclosing it does not mean losing it. A post-separation inheritance has nothing to do with the joint endeavour of the marriage, so the sharing argument against you is weak. It is treated as a resource available to you, which matters mainly when it affects how much you need from the matrimonial pot, or when the matrimonial pot is too small to meet the other party's needs."
  - question: "Does a prenuptial agreement actually protect an inheritance?"
    answer: "It helps a lot, but it is not a guarantee. Nuptial agreements are not automatically binding in England and Wales. Since Radmacher v Granatino in 2010 courts give decisive weight to an agreement that was freely entered into, with full disclosure and independent legal advice on both sides, provided the outcome is fair and does not leave a spouse or any children in real need. An agreement that records where family money came from and how the couple intend it to be treated is powerful evidence for exactly the question a judge has to answer, which is how the parties treated the asset. Where it fails is at the needs floor: no agreement can leave one party unable to house themselves or the children. A postnuptial agreement made after an inheritance arrives does the same job, and is often easier to raise as a conversation than a prenup is."
  - question: "My spouse inherited a large sum during our 20-year marriage but kept it in his own name. Is it out of reach?"
    answer: "Not necessarily, and there are two routes in. The first is needs. If the assets you have built together will not stretch to housing you both, funding your retirements and supporting the children, the court can and will use inherited money to bridge the gap, and after a long marriage with a genuine disparity in earning capacity that gap is often substantial. The second is mingling: over 20 years, inherited money rarely stays untouched, and if it paid for family holidays, a second property you both used, home improvements or was topped up with joint savings, its separate character is weakened. Start by asking for the full paper trail through the disclosure process, from the estate accounts to the account statements showing what happened to the money afterwards. What you should not do is assume that a sole account settles the question, because it does not."

<p>An inheritance is <strong>non-matrimonial property</strong> in England and Wales: it came from outside the marriage partnership rather than from joint effort, and in <a href="https://www.supremecourt.uk/cases/uksc-2024-0089">Standish v Standish [2025] UKSC 26</a> the Supreme Court confirmed that the sharing principle applies to matrimonial property only. That protection is genuine, but it is conditional on two things: whether the money stayed separate and identifiable, and whether the rest of the pot is big enough to meet both people's needs without it.</p>

<p>This guide covers when an inheritance is ring-fenced from a financial settlement, how mingling loses the protection, why the family home is the hardest asset to protect, what happens to future and post-separation inheritances, and the documents that actually work. Before you argue about any of it, run the numbers twice through our <a href="/calculators/settlement-range-estimator">settlement range estimator</a>, once with the inheritance in the pot and once with it excluded. If both versions still house both of you and the children, ring-fencing is worth fighting for. If the version without it leaves someone unable to rehouse, the court will reach into the inheritance and the argument is largely academic.</p>

<h2>Inherited assets as non-matrimonial property in a divorce</h2>

<p>Courts in England and Wales divide assets under section 25 of the <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1973/18/section/25">Matrimonial Causes Act 1973</a>, applying the equality yardstick set by White v White in 2000. Since Miller and McFarlane in 2006, the source of an asset has mattered: wealth generated by the couple during the marriage is matrimonial and shared, while wealth that arrived from outside it, through inheritance, a third-party gift or pre-marital ownership, sits in a different category.</p>

<p>Standish settled the argument about what follows from that. The sharing principle does not extend to non-matrimonial property at all, and transferring an asset into a spouse's sole or joint name does not by itself convert it, particularly where the transfer was made for tax planning rather than to share it. The court's focus is on <strong>how the couple treated the asset over time and why</strong>.</p>

<p>K v L in 2011 shows how far the protection can stretch. The wife's inherited shareholding, worth tens of millions and untouched throughout the marriage, was excluded from sharing entirely and the husband received a much smaller award set by his needs, which was only possible because the money had been kept separate.</p>

<h2>Mingling: how an inheritance joins the financial settlement pot</h2>

<p>Matrimonialisation is what defeats most ring-fencing arguments. The practical question a judge asks is whether the inherited money can still be identified and traced, and whether the couple behaved as though it was theirs jointly.</p>

<p>Two factors amplify everything in that table. Time: an inheritance received 25 years ago into a long marriage has usually lost its separate identity whatever the paperwork says. Volume: mingling £20,000 into a £900,000 pot is not the same argument as mingling £600,000 into it.</p>

<h2>The family home exception in a divorce settlement</h2>

<p>The family home is treated as matrimonial property in almost every case, regardless of who bought it, whose name is on the title or whose inheritance funded the deposit. It is where the family lived, and where the children need to keep living, so courts start from the position that it is shared.</p>

<p>That does not make an inherited deposit worthless. Where there is surplus equity beyond what both parties need to rehouse, and the deposit is documented through the estate account and the completion statement, courts do credit it in an unequal division, and the argument is strongest after a shorter marriage. Where the equity is only just enough for two households, the source of the deposit stops mattering. Our guide on <a href="/blog/the-family-home/who-gets-the-house-divorce">who gets the house in a divorce</a> covers the rehousing test, and <a href="/blog/financial-settlements/gifts-loans-bank-of-mum-and-dad-divorce">gifts, loans and the bank of mum and dad</a> deals with the different question of money advanced by living parents.</p>

<h2>When needs trump source in a financial settlement</h2>

<p>This is the point that decides most real cases. Ring-fencing only works where there is enough left over. If the matrimonial assets cannot house both people, meet the children's needs and provide a workable income, the court will invade non-matrimonial wealth to close the gap, and the source of the money offers no defence.</p>

<p>Take a couple married 18 years with two children, a home with £280,000 of equity, modest pensions, and an inheritance of £200,000 the wife received four years ago and left in a sole account. Split equally, £140,000 each does not buy a second home in most of the country, so on the matrimonial assets alone one parent cannot rehouse, and the inheritance is in play. In the same case with £1.4m of equity, the ring-fence would very likely hold. Sharing arguments about inherited wealth are a feature of cases with surplus, which is why they dominate <a href="/blog/financial-settlements/high-net-worth-divorce-duxbury">high net worth divorce</a> reports and rarely appear in ordinary ones.</p>

<h2>Future inheritances and the divorce financial settlement</h2>

<p>Money you expect to inherit one day is almost never counted. Section 25(2)(a) directs the court to the resources each party has, or is likely to have <strong>in the foreseeable future</strong>, and an inheritance from a living relative fails that test: wills can be changed, estates can be consumed by care costs, and nobody can say when it will arrive. Judges will not divide assets that may never exist.</p>

<p>The narrow exceptions are cases where the testator has already died and the entitlement is fixed, or where death is imminent and certain, in which case the court can occasionally adjourn a capital claim to be revisited later rather than speculate. An expectation can still colour the wider picture, because a party whose long-term security looks assured may be found to need less from the pot today, but that is a needs judgment, not a share of an inheritance.</p>

<h2>An inheritance received after separation or during divorce</h2>

<p>Timing changes the analysis but not the disclosure duty. Anything you own when you complete Form E has to be declared, including money that arrived after you separated, and the duty continues until the final order. Concealment is what gets settlements reopened years later, and inherited money is easy to find in the bank statements the form requires. See our <a href="/blog/process-and-costs/form-e-financial-disclosure-guide">Form E disclosure guide</a> for what the form asks for.</p>

<p>A post-separation inheritance is a poor candidate for sharing, because it has nothing to do with the joint endeavour of the marriage. It is treated as a resource: relevant to what you need from the pot, and reachable if the other party's needs cannot otherwise be met. If the inheritance is an asset rather than cash, transfers between separating spouses are made on a <strong>no gain, no loss</strong> basis for up to 3 tax years after the tax year of separation, or with no time limit under a court order, so read our <a href="/blog/tax-on-divorce/cgt-divorce-no-gain-no-loss-window">no gain no loss window guide</a> first.</p>

<p>In Scotland the rules are different in a way that matters here. Under <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1985/37/section/10">section 10(4) of the Family Law (Scotland) Act 1985</a>, property acquired by gift or inheritance from a third party is excluded from matrimonial property from the start, so the starting point is more protective than in England and Wales, although converting an inheritance into a jointly used asset such as the family home changes the position.</p>

<h2>Protecting an inheritance around a divorce</h2>

<p>The protections that work are documentary and boring, and all of them are cheaper than the argument they prevent.</p>

<ul>
  <li><strong>Keep it separate and traceable.</strong> A sole account, no joint top-ups, no withdrawals into family spending, and the estate paperwork retained. Traceability is the whole argument.</li>
  <li><strong>Record the intention in writing.</strong> A <a href="/blog/financial-settlements/prenuptial-postnuptial-agreements">prenuptial or postnuptial agreement</a> stating that inherited money is not to be shared is strong evidence of exactly what Standish says the court should look at, subject always to the needs floor.</li>
  <li><strong>Be careful with trusts.</strong> A trust set up by a parent for genuine estate planning long before the marriage is a real obstacle to a claim. A trust created once the marriage is in trouble is not, and can be treated as a resource or varied outright as a nuptial settlement.</li>
  <li><strong>Close the door with a clean break.</strong> A <a href="/blog/process-and-costs/consent-orders-and-clean-break-orders">clean break consent order</a>, court fee <strong>£62</strong>, dismisses future claims. Without one, an ex-spouse can apply against an inheritance you receive years after the divorce.</li>
</ul>

<p>Whether an inheritance is protected comes down to three questions: did it stay separate and traceable, is there enough in the matrimonial pot to meet both people's needs without it, and how long ago did it arrive.
