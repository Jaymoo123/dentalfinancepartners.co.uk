    answer: "A court order specifying that a percentage of one person's pension is debited from their scheme and credited to the other, who holds it as their own pension either within the same scheme or transferred to another.     answer: "No. A pension can only be shared or attached by a court order, even where both of you agree. An informal agreement to pay over part of a pension has no effect on the scheme and is not enforceable against it."

    answer: "Neither is universally better; they solve different problems. <p>Ask people what is being divided in their divorce and they name the house, the savings and the car. Pensions frequently exceed all of them combined, and research over many years has consistently shown they are the asset most often left out of settlements, disproportionately to the disadvantage of the lower-earning spouse, most often women who took time out of work to raise children.</p>

<p>This guide explains, factually, how the law of England and Wales deals with pensions on divorce: the three mechanisms (sharing, offsetting, attachment), how pensions are valued, the state pension, and what actually happens after an order is made. One thing this guide deliberately is not: advice. Nothing here recommends any pension decision, transfer or product. Decisions about your own pension should be checked with a regulated financial adviser, and <a href="https://www.moneyhelper.org.uk/en/family-and-care/divorce-and-separation">MoneyHelper</a>, the government-backed guidance service, is a good free starting point.</p>

<h2>Why pensions are in the pot at all</h2>

<p>Pension rights built up during a marriage are matrimonial assets like any other, and section 25 of the <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1973/18/section/25">Matrimonial Causes Act 1973</a> expressly requires the court to consider benefits, including pensions, that a party will lose the chance to acquire because of the divorce. A marriage where one person built a career and pension while the other ran the home is treated as a joint enterprise: the pension is a product of both contributions.</p>

<p>Two practical points follow. First, disclosure of all pensions, including old workplace schemes and preserved benefits, is part of every settlement; Form E requires cash equivalent values for each arrangement. Second, and less obvious: <strong>only a court order can divide a pension</strong>. However amicable your agreement, a pension share exists only when a judge seals it, which is one of the strongest practical reasons to get a <a href="/blog/process-and-costs/consent-orders-and-clean-break-orders">consent order</a> even in a friendly divorce.</p>

<h2>Mechanism 1: pension sharing orders</h2>

<p>Introduced by the <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1999/30/contents">Welfare Reform and Pensions Act 1999</a> and available for divorces from December 2000, pension sharing is the cleanest of the three mechanisms and now the standard tool where pensions themselves need dividing.</p>

<p>How it works:</p>

<ul>
<li>The order states a <strong>percentage</strong> of the member's pension to be transferred (a pension credit for the recipient, a matching pension debit for the member). It is expressed as a percentage of the cash equivalent, not a fixed sum, in England and Wales.</li>
<li>The recipient becomes a pension holder in their own right. Depending on the scheme's rules, they either become a member of the same scheme (common in public sector schemes) or transfer the credit to a pension of their own choosing.</li>
<li>The result is a <strong>clean break on that asset</strong>: the recipient's pension no longer depends on the ex-spouse's choices, retirement date, health or death.</li>
</ul>

<p>Sharing orders can be made over occupational schemes, personal pensions and self-invested arrangements, and (only in the form of a protected payment) a slice of the new state pension. They take effect only after both the final order of divorce and the sealed order with its annex reach the scheme.</p>

<h2>Mechanism 2: offsetting</h2>

<p>Offsetting divides nothing inside any pension. Instead, one spouse keeps their pension intact and the other keeps a correspondingly larger share of other assets, most commonly the family home. It is popular because it is simple, avoids scheme implementation fees, and matches what many families actually want: the parent housing the children keeps the house, the other keeps the pension.</p>

<p>The well-documented difficulty is <strong>comparing unlike with unlike</strong>. A pound of cash equivalent value is not worth a pound of house equity: pension money is taxed on the way out (beyond the tax-free element), inaccessible until at least normal minimum pension age, and grows differently. Trading them pound for pound tends to flatter whoever keeps the pension. Approaches to adjusting for this vary, there is no single mandated method, and in cases with substantial pensions a Pensions on Divorce Expert (PODE) report is often commissioned to put the trade on a defensible footing. The Pension Advisory Group's guidance, published with the Family Justice Council, is the reference document courts and practitioners use; it is a sign of how unintuitive this area is that a judicial working group needed a couple of hundred pages to standardise it.</p>

<h2>Mechanism 3: attachment orders</h2>

<p>An attachment order (historically called earmarking) directs the pension scheme to pay part of the member's pension, lump sum or death benefits to the ex-spouse <strong>when it comes into payment</strong>. The pension stays in the member's name.</p>

<p>Attachment is now rarely used, because its weaknesses are structural: the ex-spouse waits for the member to retire, payments typically stop if the member dies, income attachment payments generally cease if the recipient remarries, and the member controls the timing. It survives mainly in niche situations, for example directing death-in-service lump sums as security. If a settlement proposed to you relies on attachment rather than sharing, understand exactly why before agreeing; it is a question worth putting to a solicitor directly.</p>

<h2>Valuation: cash equivalents and their limits</h2>

<p>Every pension in a divorce is disclosed with its <strong>cash equivalent (CE)</strong> value, which the scheme must provide (schemes may charge for more than one valuation in a year). For a defined contribution pot, the CE is simply the fund value and is a fair reflection of what exists.</p>

<p>For <strong>defined benefit</strong> schemes (final salary and career average, including the NHS, teachers, civil service, police and armed forces schemes), the CE is an actuarial conversion of a promised income into a capital figure, and it can materially understate what it would cost to buy the same guaranteed, index-linked income on the open market. Two pensions with identical CE values can deliver very different retirements. Consequences in practice:</p>

<ul>
<li>Where defined benefit or public sector pensions are significant, a <strong>PODE report</strong> (commonly £1,500 to £3,000 plus VAT, usually shared) is often commissioned to calculate the sharing percentage that achieves the parties' aim, for example equality of income in retirement rather than equality of CE.</li>
<li>Equalising <strong>income</strong> and equalising <strong>capital value</strong> are different targets that produce different percentages; which is appropriate depends on the case, and is a settlement design question for the professionals advising you.</li>
<li>Some public sector schemes are unfunded and have their own quirks on implementation and internal transfer; the scheme's own divorce guidance notes are worth reading early.</li>
</ul>

<h2>The state pension</h2>

<p>The state pension follows its own rules, summarised on <a href="https://www.gov.uk/state-pension">gov.uk</a>:</p>

<ul>
<li>The <strong>new state pension</strong> (men born on or after 6 April 1951, women born on or after 6 April 1953) cannot be shared or offset by order, with one exception: a <strong>protected payment</strong>, the excess some people have above the standard full amount, can be subject to a sharing order.</li>
<li>Under the <strong>old basic state pension</strong> system, a divorced person who had gaps in their own national insurance record could in some circumstances substitute their former spouse's record to improve their basic pension, without reducing the former spouse's own pension.</li>
<li>Divorce does not change your state pension age, and remarriage can affect entitlements derived from a former spouse's record.</li>
</ul>

<p>Everyone divorcing should get a <a href="https://www.gov.uk/check-state-pension">state pension forecast</a>; it is free, takes minutes, and settlement discussions regularly proceed on wrong assumptions about it.</p>

<h2>Implementation: the four-month period and the paperwork</h2>

<p>A pension sharing order is not self-executing. After the court seals it, the scheme has a <strong>four-month implementation period</strong>, and the clock starts only when the scheme has received everything it needs: the final order of divorce, the sealed pension sharing order with its annex, the recipient's transfer instructions where relevant, and the scheme's implementation fee. Practical points that save months:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Fees:</strong> schemes set their own implementation charges, ranging from nothing to four figures, and can require payment before the clock starts. The order should say who pays.</li>
<li><strong>Sequencing:</strong> the order cannot take effect before the final order of divorce. Conversely, applying for the final order before the pension order is sealed creates the death-timing risk described in the FAQ above. Sequencing is a standard piece of solicitor housekeeping; make sure someone owns it.</li>
<li><strong>Chase it:</strong> implementation stalls are almost always missing paperwork. Diarise, follow up, and get written confirmation when the credit is established.</li>
</ul>

<h2>Common pension mistakes in divorce</h2>

<ul>
<li><strong>Ignoring pensions entirely</strong> because retirement feels distant. The settlement is final; there is no going back for the pension later.</li>
<li><strong>Trading the pension for the house pound for pound</strong> without adjusting for tax and accessibility.</li>
<li><strong>Forgetting old schemes.</strong> The government's <a href="https://www.gov.uk/find-pension-contact-details">Pension Tracing Service</a> finds lost workplace pensions free of charge.</li>
<li><strong>Overlooking death benefit nominations.</strong> After divorce, update expression-of-wish forms on every pension; ex-spouses often remain nominated for years by accident.</li>
<li><strong>Assuming the CE of a public sector pension tells the whole story.</strong> It frequently does not.</li>
</ul>
