faqs:
  - question: "What documents do I need for a divorce financial settlement?"
    answer: "The standard set in England and Wales is: 12 months of statements for every bank, building society and savings account in your sole name and every joint account; a recent market appraisal or valuation and the latest mortgage statement for each property you have an interest in; your most recent P60 and last three payslips; the last two years of accounts if you are self-employed or a company director, plus your latest tax return or SA302; a cash equivalent valuation for every pension you hold, including old workplace schemes you have lost track of; a state pension forecast; the latest statement or surrender value for every investment, ISA, share plan and life policy; and statements for every debt, including credit cards, loans, hire purchase and tax owed. Gather documents for the whole marriage where an asset predates it, because whether something is matrimonial or non-matrimonial often turns on when it was acquired and how it was used."
  - question: "How long does it take to get a pension cash equivalent valuation for divorce?"
    answer: "Longer than most people plan for. Under the Pensions on Divorce etc (Provision of Information) Regulations 2000, a scheme must supply the information within 3 months of receiving the request, or within 6 weeks if you tell it at the point of request that the information is needed in connection with divorce or dissolution proceedings that have already started, or within any shorter period the court orders. Public sector and defined benefit schemes routinely use most of the time available, so send those requests before you do anything else. Schemes can charge for repeat valuations, so ask about fees at the point of request rather than assuming a second one is free."
  - question: "What is the step order from separation to a sealed consent order?"
    answer: "Gather and value everything first, then exchange full disclosure, then negotiate directly or through mediation or solicitors, then have the agreement drafted as a consent order by a family solicitor. On the court side, the divorce application comes first (fee £628 from 13 July 2026), then the 20-week reflection period, then the conditional order. A financial consent order can only be filed once the conditional order has been made, and it does not take effect until the final order. So the last two steps are: file the consent order with the statement of information (fee £62) and wait for a judge to approve and seal it, then apply for the final order."
  - question: "What if I have already applied for the final order and the finances are not sorted?"
    answer: "This is one of the most common and most expensive mistakes. Taking the final order ends the marriage, and with it certain rights that depend on being a spouse, most importantly entitlement to a widow's or widower's pension if your former spouse dies before the settlement is concluded, and in some schemes death in service benefits. Your financial claims themselves survive the final order, so the settlement can still be agreed or decided afterwards, but the safety net has gone. If the final order has already been made and no financial order is in place, deal with the finances urgently and take advice about interim protection, and be aware that remarrying before making a financial application can bar you from claiming altogether."
  - question: "My ex will not give me any documents. Can I still make progress?"
    answer: "Yes. Start with what you can obtain yourself: your own accounts and pensions, joint account statements (a joint account is yours as much as theirs), the title register for any property from HM Land Registry, and company filings from Companies House. Then use the formal machinery rather than self-help. Attending a mediation information and assessment meeting is normally required before applying to court, and if disclosure is still not forthcoming, applying for a financial order on Form A (fee £321) triggers a court timetable with dates for exchanging Form E and answering questionnaires that the other side cannot simply ignore. Do not log into your spouse's accounts or take their private documents; unlawfully obtained material usually has to be returned and can damage your own position."
  - question: "How much do the court fees and valuations add up to?"
    answer: "The fixed court fees in England and Wales are the divorce or dissolution application at £628 (from 13 July 2026), a financial consent order at £62, and a contested financial order application on Form A at £321. On top of that sit variable costs: estate agent appraisals are often free, a formal surveyor valuation of a property typically is not, a pension on divorce expert report is a professional fee agreed before instruction, and a business valuation is priced by the job. If money is tight, check Help with Fees on form EX160, where the income thresholds are £1,420 for a single applicant and £2,130 for a couple, with a per-child uplift of £425 (child aged 0 to 13) or £710 (child aged 14 or over)."
  - question: "Do I need to redo valuations if the case drags on?"
    answer: "Often, yes, which is why gathering too early can cost you. Courts normally expect a pension cash equivalent valuation dated within the last 12 months, and if the case runs beyond that a fresh one may be needed. Property appraisals are treated more loosely but will be challenged if the market has moved noticeably since the figure was produced, and a valuation from a year ago carries little weight at a contested hearing. The practical approach is to sequence the work: request pensions first because they are slow, and leave the property appraisal until you are within a few months of settling."
  - question: "Does this checklist work in Scotland and Northern Ireland?"
    answer: "The document list broadly does, because financial disclosure everywhere in the UK needs the same evidence of what you own, owe and earn. The law and the process do not. Scotland has a different divorce procedure, no 20-week reflection period in the same form, and a matrimonial property regime built around assets acquired between the date of marriage and the date of separation, valued at the relevant date, which makes the date of separation itself a critical fact to record. Northern Ireland has its own court rules and fees. This guide covers England and Wales, so take local advice if your case belongs elsewhere."

<p>Three documents set the pace of a divorce financial settlement, and none of them is the one people start with. A pension scheme can lawfully take up to <strong>3 months</strong> to produce a cash equivalent valuation, or <strong>6 weeks</strong> if you tell it at the point of request that the information is needed for proceedings that have already commenced, under the <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2000/1048/regulation/2/made">Pensions on Divorce etc (Provision of Information) Regulations 2000</a>. Bank statements, a P60 and three payslips take an afternoon.</p>

<p>That gap is the single most useful thing to know before you start gathering. This checklist covers what to collect, where each item comes from, what it costs and how long it takes, the order the steps actually run in from separation to a sealed consent order, and the timing traps that cost real money. It applies to <strong>England and Wales</strong>. For the box-by-box detail of the disclosure form itself, read the <a href="/blog/process-and-costs/form-e-financial-disclosure-guide">Form E financial disclosure guide</a>; this page is the practical pile you need to assemble first.</p>

<p>Two additions that are not on any form but earn their place. First, write down the <strong>date of separation</strong> and anything that fixes it (a message, a change of address, a benefits claim), because it drives the capital gains tax window and, in Scotland, the valuation date itself. Second, build a realistic monthly budget for your household after separation, since the needs section of disclosure and any maintenance discussion both run off it.</p>

<p>Before you request anything, total the fixed costs so the budget is not a surprise. <h2>The pension paperwork that decides your divorce timetable</h2>

<p>Pensions are frequently the largest asset after the family home, and they are the slowest to document, so they go first. Send a written request to every scheme you have ever paid into, including short spells of employment decades ago, and ask specifically for a cash equivalent valuation for divorce purposes. If proceedings have already started, say so in the request, because that is what triggers the shorter 6-week deadline rather than the 3-month default.</p>

<p>Track the schemes you cannot find. Old workplace pensions surface through the free gov.uk pension tracing service, through P60s and payslips from the relevant years, and through the statements that show contributions leaving your account. Schemes can charge for a second valuation within a short period, so ask about fees when you request, and note the date on every valuation you receive: a figure more than 12 months old will usually need refreshing. How the resulting numbers are shared, offset or attached is a separate exercise, covered in <a href="/blog/pensions-and-divorce/pensions-and-divorce">pensions and divorce</a>.</p>

<h2>The step order from separation to a sealed financial settlement</h2>

<p>The sequence below is the one that avoids rework. Steps 1 to 4 are the money side, steps 5 to 8 are the court side, and they run in parallel rather than one after the other.</p>

<ol>
  <li><strong>Request pension valuations</strong> and start downloading statements the week you decide to separate.</li>
  <li><strong>Value the property and list the debts</strong>, so both sides of the balance sheet are real numbers rather than estimates.</li>
  <li><strong>Exchange full disclosure</strong>, formally on Form E if court proceedings are on foot, or voluntarily on the same template if you are settling by agreement or in mediation.</li>
  <li><strong>Negotiate</strong>, directly, through mediation, or through solicitors, until you have heads of agreement covering capital, the home, pensions and any maintenance.</li>
  <li><strong>Apply for the divorce or dissolution</strong> (fee <strong>£628</strong> from 13 July 2026), which starts the <strong>20-week reflection period</strong>.</li>
  <li><strong>Apply for the conditional order</strong> once the 20 weeks have run. A financial consent order cannot be filed before this point.</li>
  <li><strong>File the consent order and statement of information</strong> (fee <strong>£62</strong>) and wait for a judge to approve and seal it, or, if agreement fails, apply for a financial order on Form A (fee <strong>£321</strong>).</li>
  <li><strong>Apply for the final order</strong>, at least <strong>6 weeks and 1 day</strong> after the conditional order, and normally only once the financial order is sealed.</li>
</ol>

<p>The legal minimum for the divorce itself is <strong>26 weeks</strong> under the Divorce, Dissolution and Separation Act 2020, and the finances routinely take longer than that. If agreement fails and you file Form A, the court must fix the first appointment <strong>not less than 12 weeks and not more than 16 weeks</strong> after the application is filed, under <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2010/2955/article/9.12">rule 9.12 of the Family Procedure Rules 2010</a>, so the contested route adds months. Realistic end-to-end timings are set out in <a href="/blog/process-and-costs/how-long-does-divorce-take">how long a divorce takes</a>.</p>

<h2>Timing traps in a divorce financial settlement</h2>

<p>Four traps account for most of the avoidable damage.</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Taking the final order before the financial order is sealed.</strong> Ending the marriage can end pension death benefits that depend on being a spouse. Your financial claims survive, but the protection does not.</li>
<li><strong>Letting valuations go stale.</strong> A pension valuation older than 12 months usually needs redoing, and a property appraisal from last year invites a challenge. Gathering too early costs as much as gathering too late.</li>
<li><strong>Missing the capital gains tax window.</strong> Transfers between separating spouses or civil partners are treated as <strong>no gain, no loss</strong> for up to <strong>3 tax years</strong> after the tax year of separation, or without a time limit where the transfer is made under a formal separation agreement or court order (Finance Act 2023, TCGA 1992 s.58).</li>
<li><strong>Assuming the divorce itself settles the money.</strong> It does not. Without a sealed financial order, claims stay open indefinitely, which is exactly the gap that produces claims years later.</li>
</ul>

<p>A separating couple recently ran into the second and third traps together. Rowena and her former partner gathered everything in the first month, then spent 14 months arguing about the house. By the time they agreed, both pension valuations had expired and the property appraisal was stale, so the whole valuation exercise was repeated at their own cost. Sequencing the requests would have avoided it.</p>

<h2>Getting the order sealed and the family home dealt with</h2>

<p>A judge will not rubber-stamp whatever is put in front of them. The consent order is filed with a statement of information summarising both parties' finances, and orders get rejected where the figures do not add up, where the housing needs of children are not met, or where the drafting is unclear about who does what and by when. Build the practical mechanics into the draft: who pays the mortgage until sale, who covers the fees, the deadline for a transfer of equity, and what happens if a remortgage is refused. The mechanics of approval and clean breaks are covered in <a href="/blog/process-and-costs/consent-orders-and-clean-break-orders">consent orders and clean break orders</a>, and how the underlying division is reasoned in the <a href="/blog/financial-settlements/divorce-financial-settlement-guide">divorce financial settlement guide</a>.</p>

<p>If the court fees are a barrier, Help with Fees on form EX160 can reduce or remove them: the income thresholds are <strong>£1,420</strong> for a single applicant and <strong>£2,130</strong> for a couple, with a per-child uplift of <strong>£425</strong> (child aged 0 to 13) or <strong>£710</strong> (child aged 14 or over), and help tapers by 50p for each £1 of monthly income above the threshold, capped at the fee. Refunds can be claimed within <strong>3 months</strong> of paying. The detail sits in the <a href="/blog/process-and-costs/help-with-divorce-fees-ex160">guide to help with divorce fees</a>.</p>

<p>The checklist rewards sequencing more than speed. Request pension valuations in week one, download everything you can while you wait, value the property only when settlement is genuinely close, and never take the final order until the financial order is sealed.
