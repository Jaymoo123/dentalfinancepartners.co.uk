  - question: "Is common law marriage real in the UK?"
    answer: "No. In England and Wales there is no such legal status as a common law husband or wife, no matter how long a couple have lived together or whether they have children. The belief persists because cohabitation is now the fastest growing family type, and because a minority of legal contexts (like some benefits assessments) do treat couples as a unit. But for property, pensions, maintenance and inheritance, marriage or civil partnership is the switch that turns rights on. An unmarried partner cannot apply for a financial settlement, spousal maintenance or a pension sharing order when the relationship ends. Their claims are limited to property law under TOLATA and, where there are children, Schedule 1 of the Children Act 1989."
  - question: "We lived together for 12 years and the house is in my partner's sole name. I paid the bills. Do I get half?"
    answer: "Not automatically, and possibly nothing. Because you are not married, the starting point is legal ownership: the house belongs to your partner unless you can establish a beneficial interest under trust law. Paying the mortgage directly, or funding an extension, can support a claim that you were intended to have a share; paying utility bills and food shopping generally does not, because the court is looking for a common intention about ownership, not contributions to daily life. A TOLATA claim can ask the court to declare that you have a share and order a sale, but the court cannot give you a share simply because 12 years and fairness suggest you deserve one. This is precisely the situation a cohabitation agreement or declaration of trust is designed to prevent."
  - question: "My ex earns £900 a week gross and our two children live with me. We never married. What maintenance can I get?"
    answer: "For the children, the CMS formula applies exactly as it would for a divorced parent. On £900 gross weekly income with two children, the basic rate is 16 percent of the first £800 (£128) plus 12 percent of the £100 above it (£12), so about £140 a week before any adjustment for overnight stays or other children the payer supports. If your ex pays through Collect and Pay rather than by direct arrangement, they pay 20 percent on top and you receive 4 percent less. For yourself, the answer is starkly different: as an unmarried ex-partner you have no maintenance claim at all. Spousal maintenance only exists between spouses and civil partners."
  - question: "Are cohabitation agreements legally binding?"
    answer: "A cohabitation agreement is a contract, and like any contract it is enforceable if it is made properly: both partners entering freely, with a clear record of what was agreed, ideally with independent legal advice and financial disclosure, and executed as a deed. Courts have upheld them. Its close relative, the declaration of trust, is stronger still for the home itself, because it conclusively records each person's beneficial share of the property. Together they do the job that matrimonial law would otherwise never do for you: fixing who owns what, who pays what, and what happens if you separate. Compare that with a prenuptial agreement, which is persuasive but not strictly binding on a divorce court. For unmarried couples the contract is not the backup plan, it is the only plan."
  - question: "What is the difference between a separation agreement and a consent order?"
    answer: "A separation agreement is a contract between the two of you, usually drawn up as a deed, recording how you will divide money, property and responsibilities while separated. A consent order is a court order: it is made within divorce proceedings, approved by a judge, and it is what actually ends financial claims between spouses, including via a clean break. A separation agreement made with full disclosure and independent advice carries significant weight if one of you later asks a court to depart from it, but it cannot itself dismiss claims. The usual sequence for married couples is separation agreement now, then the same terms converted into a consent order (£62 court fee) when the divorce goes through."

The contrast with married couples is dramatic: on divorce a pension is often the largest asset after the home and can be split by court order. <p>There is no such thing as common law marriage in England and Wales. However long you have lived together, an unmarried partner has no claim to the other's income, savings or pension on separation: no financial settlement, no spousal maintenance, no pension sharing. The law that applies is property law under the <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1996/47/contents">Trusts of Land and Appointment of Trustees Act 1996</a> (TOLATA) and, where there are children, <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1989/41/schedule/1">Schedule 1 of the Children Act 1989</a>. Everything in this guide is about the law of England and Wales; Scotland gives cohabitants materially different rights.</p>

<p>This guide covers both sides of the line: what unmarried couples can and cannot claim, the agreements that fill the gap, and how separation agreements work for married couples who have split but are not yet ready to divorce. If you are in that married group, it pays to know what a court would consider fair before you sign anything: the <a href="/calculators/settlement-range-estimator">settlement range estimator</a> gives you a realistic bracket for your circumstances, so a separation agreement records a deal you would still defend in front of a judge.</p>

<h2>The common law marriage myth: why there is no divorce for cohabitants</h2>

<p>Cohabiting couples are the fastest growing family type in the UK, as the <a href="https://www.ons.gov.uk/peoplepopulationandcommunity/birthsdeathsandmarriages/families/bulletins/familiesandhouseholds/2023">ONS families and households data</a> shows, yet surveys consistently find that a large share of cohabitants believe they acquire marriage-like rights over time. They do not. The financial remedies that divorcing spouses rely on, needs-based division of assets, spousal maintenance, pension sharing orders, exist only inside divorce or dissolution proceedings. End a 20-year cohabiting relationship and the court has no power to redistribute anything based on fairness or need between the adults.</p>

<p>What survives are two narrower routes: a claim about ownership of property, and claims made for the benefit of children. Both are covered below, and both are weaker, slower and less predictable than the <a href="/blog/financial-settlements/divorce-financial-settlement-guide">financial settlement framework</a> married couples use.</p>

<h2>TOLATA claims: how unmarried couples fight over the family home</h2>

<p>For cohabitants, the family home is decided by trust law, not family law. Under TOLATA a court can declare who owns what share of a property and order a sale, but it cannot adjust the shares to reflect needs, contributions to family life or the length of the relationship. The questions are technical:</p>

<ul>
  <li><strong>Joint names:</strong> you each keep the share recorded on the title or in any declaration of trust. If nothing was recorded, equal shares are the starting presumption, rebuttable by evidence of a different common intention.</li>
  <li><strong>Sole name:</strong> the non-owner starts with nothing and must prove a beneficial interest, typically by showing an agreement or understanding about ownership plus reliance on it, or direct financial contributions to the purchase or mortgage.</li>
</ul>

<p>Paying household bills, raising children or giving up a career rarely creates a share on its own. TOLATA litigation is also costly, routinely running into the tens of thousands of pounds, and unlike family proceedings the loser can be ordered to pay the winner's costs. The mechanics of one partner buying the other out, valuations, mortgage capacity, transfer of equity, are the same as for divorcing couples, covered in our <a href="/blog/the-family-home/house-buyout-transfer-of-equity-divorce">house buyout and transfer of equity guide</a>.</p>

<h2>Schedule 1 and child maintenance: support for children, not partners</h2>

<p>Where an unmarried couple have children, two routes provide money, and both belong to the children rather than the ex-partner.</p>

<p><strong>Child maintenance</strong> works identically whether or not the parents were married. The <a href="https://www.gov.uk/calculate-child-maintenance">CMS formula</a> takes the paying parent's gross weekly income: at the basic rate, 12 percent for one child, 16 percent for two and 19 percent for three or more on income up to £800 a week, then 9, 12 or 15 percent on the slice from £800.01 to £3,000. Using Collect and Pay instead of a direct arrangement adds 20 percent for the payer and deducts 4 percent from the recipient. The full mechanics, including reductions for shared care, are in our <a href="/blog/maintenance-and-support/child-maintenance-cms-formula">CMS formula guide</a>.</p>

<p><strong>Schedule 1 of the Children Act 1989</strong> goes further for bigger money cases. A court can order a lump sum for child-related costs, top-up maintenance where the payer's gross income exceeds the CMS ceiling of £3,000 a week, and, most significantly, housing: a home purchased or settled for the child and caring parent to live in. The catch is that the housing is usually held on trust and reverts to the paying parent when the child turns 18 or finishes education. The caring parent gets stability, not capital. Contrast that with <a href="/blog/maintenance-and-support/spousal-maintenance-guide">spousal maintenance</a>, which simply does not exist for unmarried partners.</p>

<h2>Cohabitation agreements: a financial settlement written in advance</h2>

<p>Because no court will impose fairness later, unmarried couples have to contract for it up front. A cohabitation agreement records who owns what, how the mortgage, deposit and bills are shared, what happens to the home and other assets if you separate, and practical points like pets and joint accounts. Paired with a declaration of trust fixing the beneficial shares in the property, it removes almost everything a TOLATA dispute would otherwise argue about.</p>

<p>Properly made, with both partners signing freely, disclosure of finances and ideally independent advice on each side, a cohabitation agreement is an enforceable contract. That gives it a firmer legal footing than a <a href="/blog/financial-settlements/prenuptial-postnuptial-agreements">prenuptial agreement</a>, which a divorce court can depart from. Typical drafting costs run from about £500 to £1,500, a fraction of contested TOLATA proceedings.</p>

<h2>Separation agreements for married couples not yet divorcing</h2>

<p>A separation agreement is the married couple's version of the same idea: a deed recording how you will divide money, property and outgoings now, while the divorce itself waits. Couples use them to delay divorce for religious reasons, to preserve marriage-linked benefits, or simply to settle the finances while emotions are still workable. Courts give substantial weight to a separation agreement made with full disclosure and independent advice, but it is still a contract, not an order: financial claims between spouses stay open until a judge approves a <a href="/blog/process-and-costs/consent-orders-and-clean-break-orders">consent order</a>, which costs £62 in court fees within divorce proceedings.</p>

<p>Timing has a tax edge for this group. Separating spouses keep no-gain-no-loss CGT treatment on transfers between them for 3 tax years after the tax year of separation, and without limit where the transfer happens under a court order. Unmarried partners get none of that: transferring a share of a property to an ex-partner is a normal disposal, set against a £3,000 annual exempt amount for 2026/27, taxed at 18 or 24 percent on residential gains, with any tax due reportable within 60 days. And either partner buying a next home while still holding a stake in the old one can face the 5 percent SDLT additional-dwelling surcharge. For cohabitants, the tax system is one more place where the safety net is missing.</p>

<h2>The bottom line on separation, divorce and financial protection</h2>

<p>Marriage is a legal switch, not a formality. Unmarried couples get property law and child-focused claims, nothing more, so the protection has to be written: a declaration of trust and cohabitation agreement while together, a clearly drafted separation agreement if splitting. Married couples who separate without divorcing hold far stronger claims, but those claims cut both ways and stay open until a consent order closes them.</p>
