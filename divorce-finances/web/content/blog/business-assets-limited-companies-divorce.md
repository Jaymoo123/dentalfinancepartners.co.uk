faqs:
  - question: "Is a limited company protected from divorce in the UK?"
    answer: "No. In England and Wales the court treats shares in a limited company as a financial resource under section 25 of the Matrimonial Causes Act 1973, so they go into the pot to be valued alongside the home, pensions and savings. What the court cannot easily do is reach into the company itself: the company is a separate legal person, so its premises, stock and bank account belong to it, not to either spouse. In practice the court values the shareholding and then builds a settlement around that figure, usually leaving the company with the spouse who runs it and compensating the other from different assets. Putting the company in a spouse's sole name, or holding it through a holding company, does not take it out of the picture."
  - question: "How is a limited company valued in a divorce?"
    answer: "Usually by a single joint expert, an accountant instructed by both spouses together, who reports to the court rather than to either side. For a trading company the most common approach is a multiple of maintainable earnings: the expert works out the sustainable annual profit, adjusts for one-off items and an owner paying themselves below or above market rate, then applies a multiple drawn from comparable sales. Property and investment companies are more often valued on net assets, meaning what the company owns minus what it owes. The expert will also comment on liquidity, how much cash could realistically be extracted and at what tax cost, and may apply a discount for a minority stake. Valuations of private companies are opinions, not prices, and the court knows they are fragile."
  - question: "Will I have to sell my business or hand over shares to my ex?"
    answer: "It is unusual. Courts are reluctant to make orders that destroy the very asset that supports the family, so a forced sale of a trading company is a last resort, and transferring shares to an ex-spouse creates exactly the ongoing entanglement most divorcing couples want to end. The standard outcome is offsetting: you keep the company, and your ex-spouse receives a larger share of the liquid assets, the home or the pensions to balance it. Where the liquid assets are not enough, courts can order a series of lump sums paid over several years, funded from company profits, rather than breaking the business up. A sale generally only happens where the business is really an investment holding easily sold, or where there is no other way to meet both parties' needs."
  - question: "I built my company before we married. Does my spouse get half of it?"
    answer: "Not automatically. Value you created before the marriage is non-matrimonial property, and following the Supreme Court's decision in Standish v Standish in 2025 the sharing principle applies to matrimonial property, not to non-matrimonial property that has kept its separate character. In practice the expert may be asked to estimate what the company was worth at the date of the marriage and how much of today's value reflects growth during it. Two caveats matter. First, needs can override this: if the only way to house the other spouse and children is to draw on business value, the court will. Second, if the business became intertwined with family finances over a long marriage, its pre-marital character can fade. The longer the marriage, the weaker the ring-fence."
  - question: "My spouse and I are 50/50 shareholders and both directors. What happens to the company?"
    answer: "You have three broad options. One of you buys the other out, with the price anchored to the joint expert's valuation and the leaving spouse resigning as director, which is the cleanest outcome and can be written into a consent order alongside the rest of the settlement. Or you sell the company to a third party and divide the proceeds, which crystallises real cash but takes time and may attract Capital Gains Tax, potentially at the 18 percent Business Asset Disposal Relief rate for each of you if you qualify. Or, rarely, you keep running it together, which only works for genuinely amicable ex-couples and still needs a shareholders' agreement to govern dividends, exits and deadlock. Whichever route you take, deal with it inside the financial settlement rather than leaving the shareholding hanging."
  - question: "What tax is paid if shares are transferred as part of the divorce?"
    answer: "Often none at the point of transfer, if you time it properly. Since the Finance Act 2023 changes to section 58 of the Taxation of Chargeable Gains Act 1992, transfers between separating spouses are treated as no gain no loss for up to 3 tax years after the tax year of separation, and with no time limit at all when the transfer is made under a court order, including a consent order. The receiving spouse takes over the original base cost, so tax is deferred rather than abolished: they will face the gain when they eventually sell. Outside those windows a transfer is taxed at market value, with only the £3,000 annual exempt amount to set against it, and gift holdover relief under section 165 can be restricted on divorce because giving up financial claims counts as consideration. The windows are generous, but they reward getting a court order rather than relying on an informal agreement."
  - question: "Does Business Asset Disposal Relief apply if I sell shares to fund my settlement?"
    answer: "It can, and it materially changes the arithmetic. If you sell shares in your personal trading company, broadly one where you hold at least 5 percent of the shares and votes and are an officer or employee, and you have met the conditions for at least 2 years, Business Asset Disposal Relief taxes qualifying gains at 18 percent from 6 April 2026, within a lifetime limit of £1 million of gains. Without it, gains above the £3,000 annual exempt amount are taxed at up to 24 percent. On a £500,000 gain that is the difference between roughly £90,000 and up to £120,000 of tax, which directly changes how much cash a sale actually frees up for the settlement. A spouse exiting a family company as part of the divorce should check their own eligibility before agreeing timing, because resigning as a director too early can break the conditions."
  - question: "Can my ex claim maintenance from my business income instead of a share of the company?"
    answer: "Yes, and in income-heavy cases that is often the real negotiation. If the company reliably produces profit, the court can order spousal maintenance out of that income stream, or it can capitalise the claim: convert it into a single lump sum using a Duxbury-style calculation, which estimates the fund needed to provide a given annual income for life, and end the ongoing obligation with a clean break. Capitalisation suits business owners because it removes the risk of an ex-spouse returning to court for more if profits rise, and it suits recipients who prefer certainty over dependence on a company they no longer see inside. Spousal maintenance is not taxable income for the recipient and not deductible for the payer, so the negotiation is about net figures on both sides."

<p>Your shares in a limited company go into the divorce pot to be valued and divided, however the company is structured and whoever founded it. In England and Wales the court deals with the shareholding, not the company's premises, stock or bank account, because the company is a separate legal person, and in practice courts almost never break up a trading business: they value it, then settle around it. The framework is section 25 of the <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1973/18/section/25">Matrimonial Causes Act 1973</a>, which makes the parties' resources, including business interests, part of the picture.</p>

<p>This guide covers the business-specific mechanics: how companies are valued, why paper value is not cash, the three ways settlements deal with a business, and the tax rules that decide how much value actually survives the process. For how the overall pot is divided between spouses, the <a href="/blog/financial-settlements/divorce-financial-settlement-guide">divorce financial settlement guide</a> covers the general principles. If you want a starting range for your own case before the valuation arguments begin, the <a href="/calculators/settlement-range-estimator">settlement range estimator</a> models how a business-heavy asset pool tends to shift the likely outcome compared with a pot of house and savings.</p>

<h2>Your limited company in divorce: the shares go in the pot</h2>

<p>Two things are true at once. The company is a separate legal entity, so its assets belong to it and the court will not simply order it to hand over cash or property. But your shares in it belong to you, and they are on the table like any other asset. It makes no difference that the company is in one spouse's sole name, that the other spouse never worked in it, or that it sits under a holding company.</p>

<p>Timing does matter. Value built before the marriage is non-matrimonial property, and since the Supreme Court's decision in Standish v Standish in 2025, non-matrimonial property that has kept its separate character is not subject to the sharing principle. A company founded years before the wedding may therefore be partly ring-fenced, with the expert asked to estimate its value at the date of the marriage. The protection is real but not absolute: needs can override it, and over a long marriage a business woven into the family finances loses its separate character. Full disclosure still applies either way, and business interests are exactly what the court expects to see set out properly in <a href="/blog/process-and-costs/form-e-financial-disclosure-guide">Form E financial disclosure</a>.</p>

<h2>How a business is valued in a divorce financial settlement</h2>

<p>Neither spouse's own accountant decides the number. The usual route is a single joint expert, a forensic accountant instructed by both sides, whose duty is to the court. Their report normally covers value, liquidity and tax, using one of three bases:</p>

    </tr>
    <tr>

</table>

<p>The adjustments are where valuations are won and lost. An owner paying themselves £30,000 for a role worth £90,000 is flattering the profits, and the expert will correct for it. A minority stake may be discounted because it cannot force dividends or a sale, though family courts often give such discounts less weight between spouses than a commercial buyer would. And every honest report concedes the same point: a private company valuation is an opinion, not a price.</p>

<h2>Liquidity vs paper value: the divorce negotiation that actually matters</h2>

<p>A company valued at £1 million does not put £1 million on the table. To turn shares into money the owner must sell, borrow against the business, or extract cash as dividends or salary, and every route has a cost. Extraction is taxed as income, a sale takes months and may never complete, and borrowing loads the company with debt that reduces the very profits the valuation was built on.</p>

<p>Courts understand this, which is why risky, illiquid business value is not treated like money in the bank. The family courts have long recognised, in the line of cases following Wells v Wells, that it can be unfair to leave one spouse with all the copper-bottomed assets and the other with all the risk. In practice that produces two adjustments: the business figure is often discounted for risk and illiquidity when set against cash and property, and where no discount can bridge the gap, the non-owning spouse may take a deferred share, paid out of future profits or a future sale, rather than a fictional lump sum today. The same trade-off between certain and uncertain value drives <a href="/blog/pensions-and-divorce/pension-sharing-vs-offsetting">pension sharing versus offsetting</a> decisions, and the logic transfers directly.</p>

<p>Offsetting is by far the most common outcome, because it delivers what most divorcing couples actually want: complete financial separation. Share transfers are rare outside genuinely amicable splits, since few people want an ex-spouse with shareholder rights over their livelihood. Staged lump sums sit in between, common where the company is valuable but the couple's liquid assets are thin. Whichever structure you agree, it only binds anyone once it is sealed in a <a href="/blog/process-and-costs/consent-orders-and-clean-break-orders">consent order</a>, which for business owners has an extra tax benefit covered next.</p>

<h2>Tax on business assets in divorce: CGT windows, holdover and BADR</h2>

<p>Three rules decide how much value survives the settlement:</p>

<ul>
  <li><strong>The no gain no loss window.</strong> Following the Finance Act 2023 changes to <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1992/12/section/58">section 58 of the Taxation of Chargeable Gains Act 1992</a>, transfers of assets, including shares, between separating spouses are made at no gain no loss for up to 3 tax years after the tax year of separation, and with no time limit when made under a court order. The recipient inherits the original base cost, so the tax is deferred, not cancelled. HMRC's <a href="https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/capital-gains-tax-separation-and-divorce-hs281-self-assessment-helpsheet">helpsheet HS281</a> sets out the detail.</li>
  <li><strong>Holdover relief as the fallback.</strong> Outside those windows, a transfer is taxed at market value with only the £3,000 annual exempt amount to set against it. Gift holdover relief for business assets under <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1992/12/section/165">section 165 TCGA 1992</a> can defer some of that gain, but on divorce it is often restricted, because surrendering financial claims counts as consideration for the transfer. The lesson is blunt: use the statutory windows, and get the court order.</li>
  <li><strong>BADR on a sale.</strong> If shares are sold to raise settlement cash, <a href="https://www.gov.uk/business-asset-disposal-relief">Business Asset Disposal Relief</a> taxes qualifying gains at 18 percent from 6 April 2026, within a £1 million lifetime limit, against up to 24 percent otherwise. Eligibility broadly requires a 5 percent stake in a trading company and an officer or employee role for at least 2 years, so a spouse exiting the company should not resign before checking how timing affects their relief.</li>
  </ul>

<p>These interactions are exactly why business settlements are negotiated net of tax, not on headline figures. The <a href="/capital-gains-tax-divorce">capital gains tax and divorce hub</a> covers the wider CGT position, including the family home rules.</p>

<h2>Duxbury capitalisation: turning business income into a maintenance lump sum</h2>

<p>Where a company's real value is the income it pays its owner, the negotiation often shifts from capital to income. The court can order spousal maintenance from that income stream, or capitalise the claim: a Duxbury-style calculation estimates the lump sum that, invested and drawn down, would provide a given annual income for life, and the paying spouse funds it, often from the business, in exchange for a clean break. Owners tend to like capitalisation because it ends the risk of an ex-spouse applying to vary maintenance upwards if profits grow; recipients gain certainty and independence. Since spousal maintenance is not taxable income for the recipient and not deductible for the payer, both sides should negotiate in net terms. The <a href="/blog/maintenance-and-support/spousal-maintenance-guide">spousal maintenance guide</a> explains how these claims are assessed in the first place.</p>

<h2>Getting the business through your divorce intact</h2>

<p>Well-run cases tend to land in the same place: the shareholding goes into the pot, a single joint expert puts a realistic, tax-aware number on it, and the settlement is built around offsetting or staged payments rather than dismembering the company. The owner protects the business by conceding a fair discount honestly rather than fighting the valuation to the last pound, and the other spouse protects themselves by testing liquidity claims rather than accepting that the company can afford nothing. Both are protected only once the deal is sealed in a consent order, which also unlocks the unlimited no gain no loss window for any share transfer.</p>
