faqs:
  - question: "How much does a forensic accountant cost in a divorce in the UK?"
    answer: "There is no official tariff, and any single figure quoted without a scope attached is meaningless. What drives the cost is the task, not the profession. A limited-scope review, for example checking whether declared income can support declared spending, or sense-checking one set of company accounts, commonly sits in the low thousands of pounds. A full valuation of a trading company with a report compliant with Part 25 of the Family Procedure Rules, answers to written questions and possible attendance at a hearing typically starts from around £3,000 plus VAT and runs well beyond £10,000 where there are multiple entities, international elements or disputed add-backs. For scale, the legal aid codified rate for an accountant is £64 per hour whether the work is done in London or outside it (Schedule 5, Civil Legal Aid (Remuneration) Regulations 2013), while commercial forensic rates are several times that figure. Always ask for a written fee estimate against a defined scope, and ask what triggers a variation."
  - question: "What is a single joint expert and is it cheaper than instructing my own?"
    answer: "A single joint expert is one expert instructed by both parties together, whose duty under rule 25.3 is to help the court rather than either side. Where two or more parties want to put expert evidence before the court on the same issue, rule 25.11(1) lets the court direct that the evidence be given by a single joint expert, and in financial remedy proceedings that is the usual outcome. It is almost always cheaper, because one report is produced instead of two and there is no second expert to cross-examine. It is also usually more persuasive, because neither side can dismiss the conclusions as bought. The trade-off is control: you share the instruction letter under rule 25.12(1), and if the report goes against you, the route to challenge it is written questions for clarification under rule 25.10, not a rival report you commissioned yourself."
  - question: "Can I instruct my own expert instead of a single joint expert?"
    answer: "Sometimes, but not freely. You need the court's permission to rely on any expert evidence at all, and permission is granted only where the evidence is necessary to assist the court to resolve the proceedings under rule 25.4(3). Where a single joint expert has already reported, a party asking for their own expert has to persuade the court that the joint report is deficient in a way written questions cannot cure, and that the extra cost and delay are justified. Advice taken privately before proceedings, sometimes called a shadow expert, is a different thing: it is not evidence, it does not go before the judge, and you pay for it yourself. It can still be money well spent on a large business or pension where you need to understand the numbers before agreeing an instruction letter."
  - question: "What does a PODE report cost and when is one needed?"
    answer: "A pensions on divorce expert, usually an actuary, is commonly instructed where there are defined benefit or public sector pensions, where offsetting is being proposed against significant pension value, or where the cash equivalent value is likely to understate what the benefits are really worth. Typical market ranges for a report of this kind run from about £1,500 to £3,000 plus VAT, higher where several schemes are involved or where the expert is asked to model more than one target such as equalising income in retirement as well as capital. The cost is normally shared. The value is that the percentage written into a pension sharing order is set on evidence rather than on a scheme figure that can flatter one side. Our guide to <a href=\"/blog/pensions-and-divorce/pension-sharing-vs-offsetting\">pension sharing versus offsetting</a> explains the decision the report is designed to inform."
  - question: "My ex and I disagree about what the house is worth and we are £60,000 apart. What should we do?"
    answer: "Start with the cheapest step that could resolve it. Three marketing appraisals from local estate agents cost nothing and often narrow the gap on their own, and where the remaining difference is small, splitting it is usually cheaper than proving who is right. If the gap is genuinely material, as £60,000 would be for most families, a formal valuation from a chartered surveyor working to the RICS Red Book is the proportionate next step, typically in the region of £400 to £900 plus VAT for a standard residential property. Instruct it jointly so that neither of you is paying for a number the other will reject. If one of you still disputes the outcome, the mechanism is written questions to the valuer for clarification, not a second valuation. Where the property is unusual, part-commercial or has development value, expect a higher fee and a longer report."
  - question: "What if my former spouse refuses to pay their half of the expert's fee?"
    answer: "This is the risk most people miss. Under rule 25.12(6), unless the court directs otherwise the relevant parties are jointly and severally liable for the payment of the expert's fees and expenses, which means the expert can pursue either party for the whole amount, not just for half. If you pay the full fee to keep the report moving, you can ask the court to reflect that in the final financial settlement or in a costs order, but you are out of pocket in the meantime. Two protections are worth asking for at the directions stage: a cap on the expert's fees under rule 25.12(5), which the court can impose before instructions are sent, and a direction under rule 25.12(4)(a) about how and when each party pays. Raise both before the letter of instruction goes out, not afterwards."
  - question: "Does the expert have to be on my side if I am paying them?"
    answer: "No, and expecting otherwise is a common and expensive mistake. Rule 25.3(1) states that it is the duty of experts to help the court on matters within their expertise, and rule 25.3(2) says that duty overrides any obligation to the person from whom they received instructions or by whom they are paid. An expert who shades a valuation towards whoever is funding it is failing in that duty and risks having their evidence discounted entirely. In practice this is a reason to be honest with the expert about weaknesses in your position: they will find them anyway, and a report that has dealt with them openly is far more useful to you than one that gets dismantled at a hearing."
  - question: "Do these rules apply in Scotland?"
    answer: "No. This guide covers England and Wales, where the Family Procedure Rules 2010 govern expert evidence. Scotland has a separate legal system with its own court rules and a different financial provision regime under the Family Law (Scotland) Act 1985, including the concept of matrimonial property valued at the relevant date, normally the date of separation. That fixed valuation date changes how valuers are used: a Scottish case may need a historic valuation of a business or a property as at a past date, rather than a current one, which is a different and sometimes more expensive exercise. Northern Ireland has its own rules again. If your case is in Scotland or Northern Ireland, take advice locally rather than applying the figures and rule numbers here."

<p>You cannot simply hire an expert in a financial remedy case and put their report in front of the judge. <a href="https://www.justice.gov.uk/courts/procedure-rules/family/parts/part_25">Rule 25.4(3) of the Family Procedure Rules</a> allows the court to give permission for expert evidence <strong>only if it is necessary to assist the court to resolve the proceedings</strong>, and in financial remedy proceedings the application for permission must normally be made <strong>no later than the first appointment</strong> (rule 25.6). Expert evidence is a court-controlled purchase, not a shopping decision.</p>

<p>This guide covers which expert does which job, what each typically costs in the private market, how the single joint expert route works, and who ends up paying. It applies to <strong>England and Wales</strong>. Before commissioning anything, run your route through our <a href="/calculators/divorce-cost-calculator">divorce cost calculator</a>: expert fees sit on top of solicitor costs and the £321 Form A fee, and seeing the combined total is what usually settles whether a report is proportionate or not.</p>

<h2>Which experts appear in a divorce, and what each one is for</h2>

<p>Three types of expert do most of the work in financial remedy cases, and they are not interchangeable.</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Forensic accountant.</strong> Values private company shares, tests whether declared income can fund declared spending, quantifies money run through a business for personal benefit, and quantifies reckless spending for add-back arguments.</li>
<li><strong>Actuary, usually acting as a pensions on divorce expert (PODE).</strong> Values pensions properly where the scheme figure is unreliable, and models what a given sharing percentage actually delivers, whether the target is equal capital or equal income in retirement.</li>
<li><strong>Chartered surveyor.</strong> Produces a formal RICS Red Book valuation of the family home or other property, as distinct from a free estate agent marketing appraisal, which is a sales opinion rather than evidence.</li>
</ul>

<p>Tax counsel, business brokers and single joint experts on foreign law appear in larger cases, but the three above cover the overwhelming majority. What each of them values is a separate question from how the court then divides it, which our <a href="/financial-settlements">financial settlements hub</a> sets out.</p>

<h2>Single joint experts and Part 25 permission in a financial settlement</h2>

<p>Where two or more parties want to put expert evidence before the court on the same issue, <strong>rule 25.11(1)</strong> allows the court to direct that the evidence be given by a <strong>single joint expert</strong>. In financial remedy proceedings that is the normal outcome, and the instructions are contained in a jointly agreed letter unless the court directs otherwise (rule 25.12(1)). If the parties cannot agree the letter, the court can settle its terms.</p>

<p>The economics are straightforward: one report instead of two, one set of questions instead of two, and no second expert to be cross-examined at a final hearing. The persuasive value is just as important, because neither party can dismiss the findings as evidence they paid for. Under <strong>rule 25.3</strong> the expert's duty is to help the court, and that duty overrides any obligation to whoever instructed or paid them.</p>

<p>The trade-off is control. You share the instruction letter, so scope has to be negotiated rather than dictated, and if the report is unfavourable your route is written questions for clarification, not a rival report. Getting your own advice privately before the instruction letter is finalised is often the better spend on a substantial business or pension: it is not evidence, but it tells you which questions belong in the letter.</p>

<h2>Deciding whether the expert is worth it in your divorce</h2>

<p>The test is proportionality, and it is arithmetic rather than instinct. Estimate the sum genuinely in dispute, get a written quote for a defined task, and instruct only where the potential movement is a clear multiple of the fee.</p>

<p>Take an anonymised example. Marta and her former husband disagree about the value of his consultancy company. He says the shares are worth £40,000; her solicitor thinks the true figure could be £150,000 or more. The gap is £110,000, her likely share of that gap is broadly half, and a single joint expert has quoted £6,500 plus VAT for a full valuation, shared, so around £3,900 including VAT to her. Spending roughly £3,900 to test £55,000 is proportionate on any reading. Reverse the facts, and the same calculation gives the opposite answer: where the whole company plausibly sits between £40,000 and £70,000, that same £6,500 quote consumes a large slice of what is being argued about, and a limited-scope review, or simply accepting a mid-point, is the better decision.</p>

<p>Two cheaper moves come first in almost every case. A precise questionnaire after Forms E are exchanged often produces the documents that make the argument evaporate, as our <a href="/blog/process-and-costs/form-e-financial-disclosure-guide">Form E disclosure guide</a> explains. And where the real problem is suspected concealment rather than disputed value, the free public records usually come first, which our guide to <a href="/blog/financial-settlements/hidden-assets-divorce">hidden assets in divorce</a> sets out in detail. The methodology a valuer will actually apply to company shares is covered in our guide to <a href="/blog/financial-settlements/business-assets-limited-companies-divorce">business assets and limited companies in divorce</a>, and business owners on either side of a valuation should read the <a href="/for/business-owners">business owners hub</a> before positions harden.</p>

<h2>Who pays the expert, and how the cost is shared on divorce</h2>

<p>Under <strong>rule 25.12(6)</strong>, unless the court directs otherwise the relevant parties are <strong>jointly and severally liable</strong> for the expert's fees and expenses. In plain terms, a 50:50 split between you and your former spouse is an agreement between the two of you, not a limit on what the expert can recover. If they do not pay, the expert can come to you for the whole amount, and your remedy is to ask the court to reflect that in the settlement or in a costs order later.</p>

<p>Two directions are worth securing at the same time as permission. First, <strong>rule 25.12(5)</strong> lets the court, before the expert is instructed, limit the amount that can be paid in fees and expenses, which is the practical answer to an open-ended bill. Second, rule 25.12(4)(a) lets the court give directions about payment, so the mechanics and timing can be fixed rather than assumed. Where there is a significant income disparity, courts can and do direct the wealthier party to fund the report initially, with the balance revisited at the end.</p>

<p>Scotland works differently. Financial provision there runs under the Family Law (Scotland) Act 1985, with matrimonial property valued at the relevant date, normally separation, so a Scottish case may need a historic valuation rather than a current one. Take local advice if your case is not in England or Wales.</p>

<p>The pattern in well-run cases is consistent. Exhaust the free and cheap steps first, price the expert against the sum genuinely in dispute rather than the size of the whole pot, use a single joint expert with a capped fee and an agreed scope, and treat the report as evidence to build a settlement around rather than a weapon.
