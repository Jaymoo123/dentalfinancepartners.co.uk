faqs:
  - question: "How do I start budgeting after divorce when I do not know what anything costs?"
    answer: "Build the budget from evidence, not memory. Download 12 months of statements for every account you hold and sort every outgoing into three buckets: fixed costs you cannot avoid this month (rent or mortgage, council tax, energy, water, broadband, insurance, travel to work), committed costs you have signed up to (loan and card repayments, subscriptions, childcare, pension contributions), and everything else. Most people underestimate the first two buckets by a wide margin, which is why budgets built from memory fail in month two. If you have already completed Form E for the financial settlement, most of this work is done: the same statements and the income and expenditure sections give you the raw material. Then apply the changes that separation brings: the 25% single person council tax discount if you are the only adult in the home, any child maintenance in or out, and any spousal maintenance in or out. Review it again at three months, because the first budget after a separation is always a draft."
  - question: "Will I be better off financially if I claim benefits after separating?"
    answer: "That is not really the question to ask, because benefits are not optional extras you choose to claim. Means-tested support is assessed on your household, and separating changes the household, so the assessment has to be redone whether or not the result is favourable. If you were part of a couple's Universal Credit claim, that claim ends when you stop living together as a couple and you each need to make a single claim. If neither of you claimed anything before because your joint income was too high, your individual income may now bring you inside the thresholds. Report the change promptly: an out-of-date claim built on the old household usually produces an overpayment that has to be paid back. The gov.uk benefits calculators are the right starting point for an estimate, and MoneyHelper publishes free guidance on what to check after a separation."
  - question: "What if my settlement lump sum pushes me over the savings limit for Universal Credit?"
    answer: "It very often does, and it catches people out. Universal Credit disregards the first £6,000 of money, savings and investments, reduces your award between £6,000 and £16,000, and stops entirely above £16,000 (gov.uk, Benefit and pension rates 2026 to 2027). A settlement lump sum sitting in your account counts as capital from the day it arrives, even though it was awarded to rehouse you. There is no general exemption that lets you ignore it, and deliberately spending it down to restore an award is treated as deprivation of capital, where the decision maker can carry on assessing you as if you still had the money. What is legitimate is using the lump sum for the purpose it was awarded for, typically a deposit on a home, and doing so promptly. This is a genuinely technical area where the sequence and timing matter, so get case-specific advice from a benefits adviser before the money lands rather than after."
  - question: "Do I need to tell the council I have separated?"
    answer: "Yes, and it is worth doing early. Council tax bills are issued on the household as it stands, so if your former partner has moved out and you are the only adult left in the property, you qualify for the 25% single person discount, but only from the point the council knows. Councils apply the discount on application, not automatically, and each council sets its own rules on how far back it will backdate, so a delay can cost you real money. The same conversation covers Council Tax Reduction, the separate means-tested scheme run locally for people on a low income, which can reduce a bill by up to 100% depending on your circumstances. If you have moved out and left your former partner in the family home, tell the council that too, because you are generally liable for the bill at the address where you actually live."
  - question: "Does divorce cancel my will automatically?"
    answer: "No, and this is one of the most common misunderstandings. Marriage revokes an earlier will, but divorce does not. Under section 18A of the Wills Act 1837, once the final order is made your former spouse is treated as having died on that date for the purposes of any gift to them and any appointment of them as executor or trustee, unless the will says otherwise. Two gaps follow. First, nothing happens during the separation itself: between the day you separate and the day the final order is made, your existing will still leaves everything to your spouse and still appoints them as executor. Second, once the ex is stripped out, a simple will that left everything to a spouse can collapse into a partial intestacy where the statutory rules decide who inherits, which may not be who you would choose. Writing a new will as soon as you separate closes both gaps."
  - question: "What if my ex is still named on my pension death benefit form after the divorce?"
    answer: "Then the scheme may still pay them, and no divorce order fixes it by itself. Section 18A of the Wills Act 1837 applies to your will, not to a pension. Most modern pension death benefits are paid at the discretion of the scheme trustees or administrator, guided by your expression of wish or nomination form, and that form sits with the scheme rather than in your divorce paperwork. Trustees will normally take the divorce into account if they know about it, but they are working from what they hold on file, and a stale nomination naming a former spouse is at best an invitation to a dispute at the worst possible moment for the people you actually wanted to benefit. Ask every scheme you belong to, including old ones from previous employers, for a fresh nomination form and return it. This is an administrative task, not a financial decision, and it costs nothing."
  - question: "Will I be able to get a mortgage on my own after divorce?"
    answer: "It depends on your income, your commitments and the lender, and it is assessed from scratch as a single applicant rather than as half of a former couple. Three things tend to decide it. First, your income as evidenced, which for the self-employed usually means filed accounts or tax calculations covering more than one year. Second, your commitments, which include any joint mortgage you are still named on, because a lender will count that liability against you until you are formally released from it. Third, whether maintenance counts as income: lenders treat it inconsistently, and those who accept it usually want it documented in a court order and evidenced as actually being paid, which is one practical reason to have maintenance recorded in an order rather than an informal arrangement. Our guide to a joint mortgage after divorce covers release, transfer of equity and the affordability reassessment in detail."
  - question: "Is it better to pay off debt or build savings first after a divorce?"
    answer: "There is no universal answer, but there is a widely used sequence, and it is not the one most people expect. A small emergency buffer usually comes first, because a household with no buffer meets its next boiler repair or car failure with a credit card at a high interest rate, which undoes the debt repayment you just made. After the buffer, the arithmetic favours clearing the most expensive debt first, since paying off borrowing at 20% or more is a guaranteed return that savings interest will not match. What genuinely differs after a separation is the priority order of the debts themselves: rent, mortgage, council tax and energy arrears are priority debts with much harsher consequences than a credit card, so they are dealt with first regardless of interest rate. Free debt advice is available from several national charities, and it is worth using before entering any formal arrangement."

<p>A single adult living alone gets <strong>25% off their council tax bill</strong>, and that discount is one of the very few automatic gains in a household budget that has just lost a second income (<a href="https://www.gov.uk/council-tax/who-has-to-pay">gov.uk, How Council Tax works</a>). Nearly everything else moves the other way, because the fixed costs of running a home do not halve when one household becomes two. This guide covers the income cliff on both sides of a separation, how to rebuild a workable budget, the benefits and council tax re-check that separation triggers, borrowing and credit as a single applicant, and the two documents almost everybody forgets.</p>

<p>The law and process described here is that of England and Wales. Council tax works the same way across Great Britain, but the divorce process and the matrimonial property regime differ in Scotland, and Northern Ireland has domestic rates rather than council tax.</p>

<h2>The income cliff on both sides of a divorce</h2>

<p>Separation splits one household into two, and the second household never costs half of the first. Council tax, energy standing charges, broadband, insurance, a TV licence and the minimum viable weekly shop all reappear in full at the new address, and rent or mortgage interest is rarely halved either. That is why both people usually feel worse off, including the one who kept the higher income.</p>

<p>Maintenance moves money between the two households, it does not create any. If child maintenance runs through Child Maintenance Service Collect and Pay, the paying parent pays <strong>20% on top</strong> of the calculated amount while the receiving parent has <strong>4% deducted</strong>, so £100 calculated costs one household £120 and delivers £96 to the other. Direct Pay is free to both parents. The mechanics of the calculation are covered in our <a href="/blog/maintenance-and-support/child-maintenance-cms-formula">child maintenance CMS formula guide</a>, and spousal maintenance, which is assessed on need rather than a formula, in our <a href="/blog/maintenance-and-support/spousal-maintenance-guide">spousal maintenance guide</a>.</p>

<p>There are one-off process costs to budget for as well. The divorce or dissolution application fee is <strong>£628</strong> from 13 July 2026 [SI 2026/642], a financial consent order costs <strong>£62</strong> where the terms are agreed, and a contested financial order application on Form A costs <strong>£321</strong>. To see those court fees alongside realistic estimates for the professional support around them, run your circumstances through our <a href="/calculators/divorce-cost-calculator">divorce cost calculator</a> before you build the first year's budget, so the one-off legal spend sits in the plan as a line rather than arriving as a surprise in month three.</p>

<h2>Rebuilding a single-household budget after a financial settlement</h2>

<p>Build the budget from bank statements, not from memory. If you have already completed Form E the work is largely done, because the statements and the income and expenditure sections give you 12 months of actual outgoings. Sort every payment into fixed, committed and discretionary spending, then apply the changes separation brings.</p>

<p>A worked illustration. Verity moves out of the family home and rents. Her salary is unchanged, but her outgoings gain rent, a second council tax bill and a second set of utility standing charges, while her old direct debits still leave the joint account until she moves them. Her budget only balances once the new fixed costs are listed in full and the settlement lump sum, earmarked for a deposit, stops being treated as spending money.</p>

<h2>The benefits and council tax re-check that follows a divorce</h2>

<p>Means-tested support is assessed on the household, so changing the household forces a reassessment whether or not it helps. A couple's Universal Credit claim ends when the couple stop living together, and each person then claims as a single person. Report the change promptly: an out-of-date claim usually produces an overpayment that has to be repaid.</p>

<p>The rule that catches most people is capital. Universal Credit disregards the first <strong>£6,000</strong> of money, savings and investments, tapers the award between £6,000 and £16,000, and stops entirely above <strong>£16,000</strong> (gov.uk, Benefit and pension rates 2026 to 2027). A settlement lump sum counts from the day it arrives, even though it was awarded to rehouse you, and spending it down deliberately to restore an award is treated as deprivation of capital. Using it promptly for the purpose it was awarded for is a different thing. This is technical enough to be worth a conversation with a benefits adviser before the money lands.</p>

<p>The same low income may also open Help with Fees on the court fees above. The monthly income threshold is <strong>£1,420</strong> for a single applicant and £2,130 for a couple, with a per-child uplift of £425 (child aged 0 to 13) or £710 (child aged 14 or over), tapering by 50p for each £1 above it. Our <a href="/blog/process-and-costs/help-with-divorce-fees-ex160">guide to Help with Fees and form EX160</a> covers the capital test and the 3 month refund window.</p>

<h2>Credit, savings and borrowing capacity after a divorce</h2>

<p>There is no such thing as a joint credit file, but there is a financial association: apply for anything jointly and the two files are linked, so the other person's conduct shows up in your assessments. The association survives the divorce. It ends only when the joint accounts are closed or moved into one name and you then ask the credit reference agencies to record a disassociation, which they will not do while a joint account is open.</p>

<p>A joint mortgage is the biggest version of this, because liability is joint and several: each borrower is liable for the whole payment, and a missed payment marks both credit files. A financial order does not release you, only the lender can. Our guide to a <a href="/blog/the-family-home/joint-mortgage-after-divorce">joint mortgage after divorce</a> covers that process and the affordability reassessment behind it.</p>

<p>For rebuilding, the sequence that tends to hold up is a small emergency buffer first, then the most expensive debt, then longer-term saving, with priority debts (rent, mortgage, council tax and energy arrears) handled ahead of everything else regardless of interest rate. Free debt advice from national charities is worth taking before agreeing any formal arrangement.</p>

<h2>The will and pension nomination gap after a divorce</h2>

<p>Once the final order is made, section 18A of the Wills Act 1837 treats your former spouse as having died on that date for any gift to them and any appointment of them as executor or trustee, unless the will says otherwise. That leaves two exposures. Before the final order, your existing will still benefits your spouse in full. After it, a will that left everything to a spouse can fall into a partial intestacy where the statutory rules pick your heirs.</p>

<p>Pension death benefits sit outside all of this. They are usually paid at the discretion of the scheme trustees or administrator, guided by the expression of wish form you completed at the time, which no divorce order amends. Ask every current and former scheme for a fresh nomination form. It is administration, not a financial decision. How pensions themselves are divided is covered in our <a href="/blog/pensions-and-divorce/pensions-and-divorce">pensions and divorce guide</a>.</p>

<h2>State pension and divorce: what is shared and what is not</h2>

<p>Your National Insurance record is yours and is not divided on divorce. Under the new state pension, only the protected-payment element can be shared by a pension sharing order, a mechanism available for divorces started on or after 1 December 2000 under the Welfare Reform and Pensions Act 1999, with a 4 month implementation period once the order takes effect. The full new state pension is <strong>£241.30 a week</strong> in 2026/27 and normally requires 35 qualifying years (gov.uk, Benefit and pension rates 2026 to 2027).</p>

<p>Two practical points follow. Get a state pension forecast, so the retirement line in your budget rests on your actual record. And if you are the parent a child mainly lives with, make sure Child Benefit is in your name, because the National Insurance credits attached to it protect your record. Readers closer to retirement should read our guide to <a href="/blog/pensions-and-divorce/later-life-divorce-silver-splitters">later life divorce</a>, where the pension usually outweighs everything else.</p>

<p>The money side of a separation is mostly arithmetic plus admin: rebuild the budget from real statements, force the benefits and council tax reassessment early rather than late, untangle joint credit as soon as the accounts allow it, and update the will and the pension nominations that no court order will update for you. None of it is dramatic, and all of it compounds.
