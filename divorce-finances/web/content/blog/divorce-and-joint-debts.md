faqs:
  - question: "Are we each responsible for half of our joint debts after divorce?"
    answer: "No, and this is the single most expensive misunderstanding in separation finances. Joint credit is almost always joint and several, which means each borrower is liable to the creditor for the whole balance, not a half share. If a joint loan has £9,000 outstanding and your ex pays nothing, the lender can demand all £9,000 from you, add default charges to your credit file and take enforcement action against you alone. The idea of a half share only exists between the two of you, and only if you have written it down in a court order. Even then the order does not bind the lender, it just gives you a right to claim the money back from your ex if you end up paying their share."
  - question: "Can the court order my ex to take on a joint debt in the divorce settlement?"
    answer: "The court can order your ex to pay it, and can require them to indemnify you, meaning they must reimburse you if the creditor comes after you instead. What the court cannot do is remove your name from the credit agreement, because your creditor is not a party to your divorce and never agreed to release you. That is why well-drafted settlements usually pair an indemnity with a practical exit: a deadline for your ex to refinance the debt into their sole name, a lump sum earmarked to clear the balance outright, or a sale of an asset to pay it off at the point of settlement. Clearing joint debt from the proceeds of an asset sale is nearly always cleaner than relying on years of good behaviour."
  - question: "My ex ran up £12,000 on a joint credit card after we separated. Do I have to pay it?"
    answer: "To the card issuer, yes, because joint and several liability does not pause when you separate. Practically, act on two fronts at once. Contact the card provider immediately, explain that you have separated and ask them to stop further spending on the account, because continuing to let it run makes your exposure worse by the month. Then raise the spending in the financial proceedings: where one spouse has spent recklessly or wantonly, the court can treat the money as if it were still in their hands and attribute it to their share of the pot, which is often called adding back. Keep the statements showing what was spent, when and on what, because the argument depends entirely on documentary evidence rather than on how unfair it felt."
  - question: "Should I close our joint bank account when we separate?"
    answer: "Usually you should at least stop it being used unilaterally, which is a slightly different thing. Most banks will act on one account holder's instruction to convert the account so that both signatures are needed, or to freeze it, which prevents your ex draining the balance or extending the overdraft while the settlement is negotiated. Closing it outright is normally only possible once the balance is clear and both of you agree, and standing orders and direct debits for household bills need moving first so nothing bounces. Before you do anything, take a copy of the last 12 months of statements, because you will need them for financial disclosure anyway, and it is much harder to get them after the account has gone."
  - question: "Who keeps the car if it is on finance?"
    answer: "Whoever the finance company agrees to, which is not necessarily whoever drives it. Under hire purchase or a personal contract purchase, the vehicle belongs to the lender until the final payment is made, so you cannot transfer it in a settlement the way you would transfer a car you own outright. The realistic options are that the named borrower keeps the agreement and the car, that they settle it early and buy the car outright, that the car is sold with the lender's agreement and the finance repaid from the proceeds, or that the agreement is voluntarily terminated under the Consumer Credit Act 1974, which requires you to have paid half of the total amount payable and to return the vehicle in reasonable condition. Check the settlement figure before assuming there is any equity, because on many agreements there is none for the first two or three years."
  - question: "How do I stop my ex's credit problems affecting my credit score?"

  - question: "What if we genuinely cannot afford the joint debts during the divorce?"
    answer: "Speak to a free debt adviser before the position deteriorates, because the options narrow once enforcement starts. A debt adviser can apply for Breathing Space under the Debt Respite Scheme, which gives up to 60 days during which creditors cannot add interest or charges, cannot contact you about the included debts and cannot take enforcement action; a separate version runs for the length of mental health crisis treatment plus another 30 days. Breathing Space is not debt forgiveness, the payments still fall due, but it buys time to work out a realistic plan. Tell your solicitor or mediator too, because a settlement built on debt repayments neither of you can actually make will fail within a year, and a court would rather see the debts cleared from the assets at the point of settlement than left hanging over both of you."

    answer: "Only for the bankrupt person. If your ex is made bankrupt, their liability for a joint debt is released, but yours is not, so the creditor simply pursues you for the entire balance. Bankruptcy also interacts sharply with a financial settlement, because a trustee in bankruptcy can challenge property transfers made before the bankruptcy, and the family home may have to be sold to release the bankrupt spouse's share. <p>A joint credit card, loan, overdraft or joint bank account is a <strong>joint and several</strong> debt, which means the lender can demand the entire balance from either of you regardless of who spent the money, who has moved out, or what the two of you agreed. Divorce does not halve it. A financial order made in the family court binds you and your ex, not your creditors, so the strongest thing a consent order (court fee <strong>£62</strong>) can do about a joint debt is make one of you pay it and indemnify the other.</p>

<p>This guide covers which debts are yours and which are jointly owed, how liabilities are treated in the financial settlement, the specific rules for credit cards, overdrafts and car finance, what to do when an ex stops paying, and how to break the financial association on your credit file. This is England and Wales law and process; Scotland differs, and the key differences are flagged below. One thing worth doing before you negotiate: your settlement is built on <em>net</em> assets, so put the debts as well as the assets into the <a href="/calculators/settlement-range-estimator">settlement range estimator</a>. The divisible pot is often far smaller than assumed once the cards and loans come off it.</p>

<h2>Joint and several liability: what divorce does not change</h2>

<p>Joint and several liability means each borrower owes 100 percent, not 50 percent. If a joint loan has £9,000 outstanding and your ex pays nothing, the lender can pursue you for all £9,000, register the default against your credit file, and take enforcement action against you alone. Your remedy against your ex is a separate matter, and it does not delay the creditor.</p>

<p>Whose debt is whose depends on the paperwork and nothing else:</p>

<ul>
  <li><strong>Joint accounts and joint credit</strong>: both of you fully liable, including the whole of an overdraft on a joint current account.</li>
  <li><strong>Sole debts</strong>: the person named on the agreement is liable, even if the spending benefited the whole family.</li>
  <li><strong>Additional cardholders</strong>: an extra card on your spouse's account does not make you liable for the balance; the account holder owes it all.</li>
  <li><strong>Guaranteed debts</strong>: if you signed as guarantor for your spouse, you are liable if they default, and the guarantee survives the divorce.</li>
  <li><strong>Secured debts</strong>: a second charge or secured loan follows the property, which is a different problem again. See our guide to the <a href="/blog/the-family-home/joint-mortgage-after-divorce">joint mortgage after divorce</a> for the mortgage and remortgage side.</li>
</ul>

<h2>How debts are treated in a divorce financial settlement</h2>

<p>The family court divides <strong>net</strong> assets: everything you own, minus everything you owe. A £40,000 debt shrinks the divisible pot by £40,000 before any question of fairness arises, which is why every liability belongs on Form E with a statement or agreement to evidence it. Understating debts to look poorer is as much a disclosure failure as hiding an asset.</p>

<p>Three distinctions matter in practice. First, <strong>matrimonial debt versus personal debt</strong>: borrowing that funded the family, the house, the car or the holidays is normally treated as a shared burden even where only one name is on it, while a debt run up on one spouse alone, particularly after separation, may be left where it sits. Second, <strong>reckless spending</strong>: money dissipated on gambling, a new relationship or spite spending can be added back, meaning the court treats the spender as though they still hold it. Third, <strong>soft loans</strong>: informal family lending is often treated as unlikely to be enforced and given less weight than a bank debt, which is covered in our guide to <a href="/blog/financial-settlements/gifts-loans-bank-of-mum-and-dad-divorce">gifts and loans from the bank of mum and dad</a>.</p>

<p>Where debts exceed assets there is nothing to divide, and the exercise becomes one of allocating liabilities and, sometimes, income. That is a scenario where free debt advice is more useful than more litigation, because a contested financial order application costs <strong>£321</strong> on Form A before either side pays a lawyer.</p>

<p>Car finance deserves its own note. On hire purchase and personal contract purchase the vehicle belongs to the finance company until the final payment, so it cannot be transferred in a settlement like an owned car. Under the <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1974/39/section/99">Consumer Credit Act 1974</a> the borrower can voluntarily terminate a regulated hire purchase or conditional sale agreement, but only becomes free of further liability once half the total amount payable has been paid, with the vehicle returned in reasonable condition. Get the settlement figure and the half-way figure in writing before you assume there is equity in the car.</p>

<h2>When your ex stops paying a joint debt during divorce</h2>

<p>Take Rhodri, a composite. He and his ex hold a joint loan with £9,600 left at £320 a month and a joint overdraft of £1,800, and three months after she moves out she stops contributing. The lender does not care about the reason: two missed payments produce arrears markers on both credit files, visible for 6 years, and the arrears follow Rhodri personally.</p>

<p>The sequence that limits the damage is:</p>

<ol>
  <li><strong>Keep the payments going</strong> if you possibly can, unfair as that feels, because arrears cost you far more than the money later.</li>
  <li><strong>Tell the creditor you have separated</strong> and ask them to stop further borrowing on the account. Most lenders have a process for this and will convert or freeze a joint account on one holder's instruction.</li>
  <li><strong>Record everything you pay alone.</strong> Payments you make on joint debt after separation are relevant to the settlement and can be reflected in the division.</li>
  <li><strong>Get an indemnity into the order</strong>, plus a hard deadline for refinancing or a lump sum earmarked to clear the debt, rather than trusting to future goodwill. Our guide to <a href="/blog/process-and-costs/consent-orders-and-clean-break-orders">consent orders and clean break orders</a> explains how the drafting works.</li>
  <li><strong>Get free debt advice early</strong> if the payments are genuinely unaffordable. A debt adviser can apply for <a href="https://www.gov.uk/options-for-paying-off-your-debts/breathing-space">Breathing Space</a>, which gives up to 60 days with no interest, charges, creditor contact or enforcement on the included debts.</li>
</ol>

<p>What does not work is stopping payments in retaliation. It damages your own file, weakens any later mortgage application, and gains nothing in the settlement.</p>

<h2>Credit files, financial associations and life after the divorce</h2>

<p>Marriage does not link your credit files. Joint credit does. A joint account or a joint application creates a <strong>financial association</strong>, and while it exists, lenders assessing you will take your ex's credit behaviour into account.</p>

<p>Breaking it is a two-step job in a fixed order. Close or refinance every joint credit line first, then ask each credit reference agency, TransUnion, Experian, Equifax and Crediva, for a <strong>notice of disassociation</strong>. Agencies will refuse while any joint account is live, which is why the association often outlives the divorce by years in cases where a joint card was left open with a small balance. The historic account data, including any missed payments, remains visible for 6 years from closure, so disassociation protects the future rather than rewriting the past. Check all four files afterwards, because the notice has to be raised separately with each.</p>

<h2>Scotland: how joint debts differ from an England and Wales divorce</h2>

<p>Joint and several liability is contract law and applies across the UK: a Scottish joint loan behaves the same way towards the lender. What differs is the division. Under the Family Law (Scotland) Act 1985, matrimonial property is assessed at the relevant date, normally the date of separation, and debts incurred during the marriage are netted off, with the starting point being a fair sharing of that net figure. In England and Wales the court has a broader discretion under section 25 of the Matrimonial Causes Act 1973 and can weigh post-separation conduct and need more freely. Northern Ireland has its own regime. If either of you lives, works or has assets outside England and Wales, take advice on jurisdiction first.</p>

<p>Joint debts do not divide on divorce, they follow the contract, and the only reliable way to end your exposure is to have the debt repaid, refinanced or released by the creditor before the file closes. Build the settlement around net assets, clear joint credit from the proceeds wherever you can, take an indemnity plus a deadline where you cannot, and finish with a notice of disassociation once nothing joint remains.
