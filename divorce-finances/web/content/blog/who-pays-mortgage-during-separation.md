faqs:
  - question: "Do I have to keep paying the mortgage if I move out during separation?"
    answer: "Legally, yes, if you are named on the loan. A joint mortgage is joint and several, which means the lender can pursue either borrower for the entire payment regardless of who is living in the property or who agreed what between themselves. Moving out does not reduce your liability, and it does not remove your name from the loan: only a sale, a remortgage or a lender-approved transfer of equity does that. What can change is the practical split. It is common for the person who has left and is now also paying rent to contribute less than half, or for the occupier to cover the payment in exchange for credit or a larger share later. What you should not do is stop paying unilaterally. Arrears are recorded against both of you, and they are the single most effective way to destroy the sole-name remortgage that most settlements depend on."
  - question: "Does paying the mortgage on my own mean I own more of the house?"
    answer: "Not automatically. In a divorce the court divides property under section 25 of the Matrimonial Causes Act 1973 by reference to needs, resources and fairness, not by adding up who paid what. That said, interim payments are not invisible. The general pattern is that capital repayments made from your own post-separation income have a reasonable claim to credit, because they increase the equity both of you will share, whereas mortgage interest is more often treated as the price of occupying the property and set off against a notional occupation rent. Keep a simple running record: dates, amounts, which account paid, and whether the payment was capital or interest. It costs you nothing and it turns a vague grievance into a schedule your solicitor or mediator can actually use."
  - question: "What is occupation rent and can I claim it?"
    answer: "Occupation rent is a notional rent that one co-owner can be asked to pay to the other for having sole use of a jointly owned property. It is not automatic. It normally arises where the absent owner has effectively been excluded, for example under an occupation order, or where the arrangement would otherwise be plainly unfair. In practice it is usually netted off rather than paid across: if the occupier is meeting the mortgage interest, the interest and the occupation rent frequently cancel each other out, and the court moves on. Between unmarried co-owners the same idea appears as equitable accounting under trusts of land, where there is no wide discretion to rebalance the outcome, so the accounting matters much more."
  - question: "My ex moved out and has stopped contributing. I cannot cover the full payment on my own. What can I do?"
    answer: "Move on two tracks at once. First, contact the lender before the payment is missed rather than after. Lenders have teams for separating borrowers and can discuss a term extension, a temporary switch to interest only or a formal arrangement to pay, all of which are far easier to agree while the account is up to date. Second, if a divorce application has been issued you can apply for maintenance pending suit under section 22 of the Matrimonial Causes Act 1973, which is an interim maintenance order aimed at meeting reasonable outgoings, and the mortgage is normally the largest one. If there are children, get a Child Maintenance Service calculation running in parallel, because that is separate money and it is assessed on the paying parent's gross income rather than on what is left after their own bills. Keep every missed contribution documented, because a pattern of non-payment is relevant both to the interim application and to the final financial settlement."
  - question: "The house is in my husband's sole name and I am worried he will sell it or stop paying. What protects me?"
    answer: "Register your home rights with HM Land Registry. A spouse or civil partner who is not on the title has a statutory right to occupy the family home, and registering a notice means the property cannot easily be sold or remortgaged without you finding out. Two further points matter here. Under section 30 of the Family Law Act 1996 a non-owning spouse's mortgage payments must be accepted by the lender as if they were the owner's, so you can keep the account alive yourself if you need to. And a spouse with registered home rights can ask to be joined to any possession proceedings the lender brings, which gives you standing to propose a payment plan rather than learning about the problem when the court date is set. Registering is a protective step, not an aggressive one, and it does not by itself decide who keeps the house."
  - question: "Can the court order my ex to pay the mortgage while we are separated?"
    answer: "Yes, but the route matters. An order for maintenance pending suit under section 22 of the Matrimonial Causes Act 1973 can be set at a level that covers the mortgage, and it is enforceable in the ordinary way as a maintenance order. A term requiring one party to pay the mortgage can also be attached to an occupation order under section 40 of the Family Law Act 1996, but those obligations were found to lack an effective enforcement mechanism, so a section 40 term is a weaker instrument than it looks. If mortgage payments are the core problem, the interim maintenance route is usually the one to take, and the application is made within the financial remedy proceedings rather than as a separate case."
  - question: "Should the interim mortgage arrangement be written down?"
    answer: "Yes, even if it is only an exchange of emails confirming who pays what from which date and how long it lasts. An informal arrangement that neither of you can evidence is the raw material for a dispute six months later, when one of you remembers a generous concession and the other remembers a temporary favour. If you are mediating, the interim arrangement is a natural first item to agree, because settling it removes the immediate cash pressure that pushes people into bad long-term deals. Anything intended to bind after the divorce needs a court order rather than an agreement: a consent order costs a court fee of £62 by agreement, and a contested financial order application on Form A costs £321."
  - question: "Is it different in Scotland?"
    answer: "Yes, in a way that changes the arithmetic rather than the day-to-day practicalities. Joint and several liability to the lender works the same, but Scots law values matrimonial property at the relevant date, normally the date of separation, so what happens to the mortgage after that date is treated differently from England and Wales, where the court values assets at the date of the hearing and has wide discretion to adjust for post-separation conduct with money. Interim financial support in Scotland is sought as interim aliment, and there is a separate regime of exclusion orders and matrimonial home rights. If your case is Scottish, take advice from a Scottish family law solicitor rather than applying the guidance on this page."

<p>A joint mortgage is joint and several, so from the lender's point of view separation changes nothing at all. The full payment is due every month from either borrower, whoever moved out, and an unpaid half is arrears against both names. Because the no-fault divorce minimum timeline is <strong>26 weeks</strong> (a 20-week reflection period, then a 6-week-and-1-day wait to the final order) and financial settlements routinely run past it, the arrangement you make in week one is likely to be in force for a year or more.</p>

<p>This guide covers the three interim models that work in England and Wales, how those payments are treated in the eventual financial settlement, what occupation rent is, when interim maintenance is the answer, and how to handle the lender before arrears start. Before committing to any split, run the numbers through our <a href="/calculators/settlement-range-estimator">settlement range estimator</a>: an interim deal that quietly hands the occupier the whole equity, or leaves the payer unable to fund a deposit, is far easier to spot against a realistic target range than in isolation. Removing a name from the loan after the divorce is a separate exercise, covered in our guide to the <a href="/blog/the-family-home/joint-mortgage-after-divorce">joint mortgage after divorce</a>.</p>

<h2>Three ways to split the mortgage during separation and divorce</h2>

<p>There is no default rule about who pays during separation. There is only the lender's requirement that the full payment arrives on time, and the expectation that a court will later look at what each of you did with the money.</p>

<p>Whichever model you pick, write it down. A dated email confirming who pays what, from when, and until what event is enough.</p>

<h2>A worked example: an income-proportionate split during a divorce</h2>

<p>Rosalind and Yusuf separated in March. Their joint mortgage is <strong>£1,340 a month</strong>, roughly £760 interest and £580 capital. Rosalind stays in the house with their two children; Yusuf rents a flat at £950 a month. His gross income is £700 a week, so his net income is around £2,400 a month against her £1,600.</p>

<p>An income-proportionate split (60 percent to Yusuf, 40 percent to Rosalind) gives £804 and £536. But Yusuf is also a paying parent. On the <a href="/blog/maintenance-and-support/child-maintenance-cms-formula">Child Maintenance Service basic rate</a>, two children attract <strong>16 percent</strong> of gross weekly income, so £700 a week produces £112 a week, around £485 a month, before any reduction for overnight stays. On Direct Pay that is free to both parents; on Collect and Pay he pays <strong>20 percent</strong> on top and Rosalind has <strong>4 percent</strong> deducted.</p>

<p>Yusuf's committed outgoings would then be £804 of mortgage, £485 of child maintenance and £950 of rent, against £2,400 of net income. It does not work. That arithmetic, rather than any argument about fairness, drives most real interim deals towards the second or fourth option in the table: Rosalind meets the mortgage from her income plus child maintenance, with a written acknowledgement that the capital element is to be credited to her, or the couple ask the lender for a temporary interest-only period.</p>

<h2>How interim payments affect the eventual financial settlement</h2>

<p>Paying the mortgage alone does not buy out the other person's share. The court divides property under section 25 of the Matrimonial Causes Act 1973 by reference to needs, resources and fairness, and in a needs-driven case the accounting of who paid what is often swamped by the question of where the children will live, as our guide to <a href="/blog/the-family-home/who-gets-the-house-divorce">who gets the house in a divorce</a> explains.</p>

<p>Where the pot is larger than bare needs, interim payments start to matter, and the treatment is not uniform:</p>

<ul>
  <li><strong>Capital repayments</strong> from post-separation income have the strongest claim to credit, because they increase the equity both of you will share.</li>
  <li><strong>Mortgage interest</strong> is more often treated as the cost of occupying the family home, and set off against the notional occupation rent described below.</li>
  <li><strong>Payments from a joint account</strong>, or from savings that are themselves matrimonial property, earn little credit: the money was already jointly owned.</li>
  <li><strong>Non-payment</strong> cuts the other way, particularly where arrears or fees resulted.</li>
</ul>

<p>All of this is evidence-driven, so the record is the asset. Interim arrangements and the payments actually made belong in your <a href="/blog/process-and-costs/form-e-financial-disclosure-guide">Form E financial disclosure</a>.</p>

<h2>Occupation rent and the family home during separation</h2>

<p>Occupation rent is the counterweight to the credit argument: a co-owner who is out of the property can, in some circumstances, ask for a notional rent from the one who has sole use of it. It is not automatic, and it does not arise simply because one person chose to move out. It becomes realistic where the absent owner was effectively excluded, for example under an occupation order, or where leaving them to fund housing elsewhere while the other has free use of the asset would be plainly unfair. Notional rent is normally set by reference to the local market rent for the property, halved to reflect the absent owner's share.</p>

<p>In divorce proceedings occupation rent is rarely a separate line in the settlement. More often it is netted off: the interest paid by the occupier and the rent notionally owed by them cancel out, and the court concentrates on the overall division. Between unmarried couples it matters far more, because equitable accounting under a trust of land replaces the court's wide discretion, as our guide to <a href="/blog/financial-settlements/separation-cohabitation-agreements-unmarried">separation agreements for unmarried couples</a> explains.</p>

<h2>Interim maintenance when the mortgage cannot be paid</h2>

<p>If the shortfall is real rather than tactical, the interim period has its own remedy. Once a divorce application has been issued (application fee <strong>£628</strong> from 13 July 2026), the court can order <strong>maintenance pending suit</strong> under section 22 of the <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1973/18/section/22">Matrimonial Causes Act 1973</a>: interim maintenance at a level the court considers reasonable, which usually means a figure built around the mortgage. It is enforceable like any other maintenance order and can be revisited at the final settlement. Our <a href="/blog/maintenance-and-support/spousal-maintenance-guide">spousal maintenance guide</a> covers how those figures are assessed.</p>

<p>A second route looks stronger than it is. Under section 40 of the Family Law Act 1996 the court can attach mortgage or rent obligations to an occupation order, but those terms were held to lack an effective enforcement mechanism, so they are weak where the other party simply refuses to pay. If money is the issue, the maintenance route is the reliable one. A contested financial order application on Form A costs <strong>£321</strong> and the eventual agreed consent order costs <strong>£62</strong>, both potentially covered under Help with Fees.</p>

<h2>Arrears, the lender and protecting the family home</h2>

<p>Contact the lender before the first missed payment, not after. Forbearance is a negotiation, and what is offered to a borrower who is up to date and explaining a separation beats what is offered to one already three months behind. Term extensions, a temporary interest-only period and a formal arrangement to pay are the usual options, and each carries some credit-file consequence, which is a reason to compare them rather than avoid the conversation.</p>

<p>Where the property is in one spouse's sole name, register your <a href="https://www.gov.uk/stay-in-home-during-separation-or-divorce">home rights</a> with HM Land Registry. Under section 30 of the <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1996/27/section/30">Family Law Act 1996</a> the lender must accept mortgage payments from a non-owning spouse as if the owner had made them, so you can keep the account alive yourself, and registered home rights let you ask to be joined to any possession proceedings. If the house is to stay in the family for a period and be sold later, our guide to <a href="/blog/the-family-home/mesher-orders-deferred-house-sale">Mesher orders and deferred sale</a> sets out how running costs are allocated in the order itself.</p>

<p>This guidance applies to England and Wales. Scotland values matrimonial property at the relevant date, usually the date of separation, so post-separation payments are treated differently, and interim support is sought as interim aliment. Take advice locally if your case is Scottish or Northern Irish.</p>

<p>The interim mortgage question is really three questions: what the lender needs each month, what each household can afford once two sets of housing costs exist, and how the payments are accounted for at the end. Settle the first two in writing within days of separating, record the payments for the third, and escalate to interim maintenance early rather than absorbing a shortfall until arrears appear.
