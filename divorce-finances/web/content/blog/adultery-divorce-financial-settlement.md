faqs:
  - question: "Does adultery reduce what my spouse gets in a UK divorce?"
    answer: "In almost every case, no. Since 6 April 2022 the divorce application in England and Wales carries no reasons at all, and the financial side has never worked as a reward and punishment system. The court applies section 25 of the Matrimonial Causes Act 1973: the needs of both parties and any children, the length of the marriage, the resources each has and the contributions each made. Conduct only enters the exercise under section 25(2)(g), and only where it would be inequitable to disregard it, which the courts have interpreted narrowly for more than fifty years. An affair, however painful and however clearly it caused the breakdown, does not usually change the division of the house, the savings or the pensions by a single pound. Where an affair does touch the numbers, it is through money spent rather than through fault: substantial sums funnelled to a new partner can be argued as an add-back."
  - question: "What counts as conduct the court will not disregard in a financial settlement?"
    answer: "The bar is high and deliberately so. The classic formulation from Wachtel v Wachtel in 1973 is conduct that is obvious and gross, and the modern approach set out in Tsvetkov v Khayrova in 2023 asks two questions: has the conduct been proved on the evidence, and did it cause an identifiable financial consequence. In practice the successful categories are serious physical violence that has damaged a spouse's ability to earn or live independently, deliberate destruction or reckless dissipation of assets, hiding assets from the disclosure process, and misconduct in the litigation itself such as ignoring court orders. Notice what these have in common: each has a measurable effect on the pot or on a party's needs. Moral disapproval alone is not enough, because the court is dividing resources rather than passing judgement on a marriage."
  - question: "My husband spent about £40,000 on his affair, on hotels, holidays and a car for his girlfriend. Can I get that back?"
    answer: "You can raise it as an add-back, though the outcome depends on the scale relative to your total assets and on how the spending is characterised. An add-back is a notional adjustment: the court treats the money as though it were still there and credits it to his side of the ledger, so it comes out of his share rather than out of the joint pot. The test is wanton or reckless dissipation, not simply spending you object to. In Vaughan v Vaughan in 2007 the Court of Appeal confirmed the principle, whilst in MAP v MFP in 2015 the court declined to add back very large sums a husband had spent because it was not the court's role to moralise about how he lived. £40,000 against a £2 million pot will rarely be pursued, because the costs of arguing it exceed the recovery. Against a £250,000 pot it is a serious point worth putting in a questionnaire with the documents to prove it."
  - question: "Can I name my spouse's new partner in the divorce or the financial application?"
    answer: "There is no longer any mechanism to name anyone. The old procedure allowed a co-respondent to be named where adultery was the fact relied on, and that disappeared with the Divorce, Dissolution and Separation Act 2020 on 6 April 2022. The new partner is not a party to your divorce and cannot be ordered to pay you anything or to hand back gifts they received. They become financially relevant only in two indirect ways. First, if they live with your spouse and contribute to household costs, that is relevant to what your spouse needs by way of income and can reduce a spousal maintenance figure. Second, if your spouse transferred assets to them to defeat your claim, that is a disclosure and section 37 issue rather than a conduct issue, and the court has power to unwind such transfers."
  - question: "What if my spouse's affair caused me to lose my job or made me ill and unable to work?"
    answer: "That changes the analysis, though not in the way people expect. The route is needs rather than conduct. If you are now unable to work, or working reduced hours, the court assesses your income needs and your earning capacity as they actually are, and a settlement will be built to meet those needs from the available resources, potentially through a larger share of the capital or a period of spousal maintenance. You do not need to prove the affair caused the illness to benefit from that: the court looks at your position now. Running it as a conduct argument instead is usually the weaker path, because you would have to prove both the conduct and a direct financial consequence, at real cost, to reach an outcome the needs analysis often reaches anyway. Medical evidence about your capacity to work is generally more valuable than evidence about the affair."
  - question: "Does adultery affect child maintenance?"
    answer: "No, and this is one of the clearest rules in the whole area. The Child Maintenance Service applies a formula to the paying parent's gross weekly income with no conduct element whatsoever: the basic rate is 12% for one child, 16% for two children and 19% for three or more children, adjusted for the number of nights the child stays overnight and for other children the paying parent supports. Nothing in that calculation asks why the parents separated. The same principle applies to child arrangements: the question the court asks is what is in the child's welfare, not which parent behaved better. Treating maintenance as a way to respond to an affair tends to prolong the dispute and rarely changes the figure, because the figure is arithmetic."
  - question: "Is it worth arguing conduct if I am sure my spouse behaved badly?"
    answer: "Ask three questions before you spend anything on it. Can it be proved with documents or independent evidence rather than your account against theirs? Did it cause an identifiable financial loss you can quantify? And would proving it change the outcome the needs analysis produces anyway? If the answer to any of those is no, the argument will usually cost more than it recovers. Financial remedy proceedings normally run on the basis that each side bears their own costs, but a party who pursues a hopeless conduct allegation risks a costs order against them for litigation conduct. A family solicitor can give you a realistic read on whether your facts clear the bar before you commit to the argument."
  - question: "Does adultery still matter in a Scottish divorce?"
    answer: "It matters to the process but not to the money. Scotland was not covered by the 2020 no-fault reforms, so adultery and unreasonable behaviour remain grounds for divorce there alongside separation of one year with consent or two years without. On the financial side, section 11(7)(a) of the Family Law (Scotland) Act 1985 directs the court to disregard conduct unless it has adversely affected the financial resources, which is a similar filter to the England and Wales approach. The bigger practical difference is that Scotland's regime for dividing matrimonial property is narrower, focusing on property acquired during the marriage, which changes outcomes far more than any conduct question does. Scottish cases need Scottish advice, because the whole framework differs."

<p>Adultery stopped being a ground for divorce in England and Wales on <strong>6 April 2022</strong>, when the Divorce, Dissolution and Separation Act 2020 came into force. The only ground now is irretrievable breakdown, no reasons are given on the application, and it cannot be contested over who did what. This guide covers the narrow route by which conduct can still affect a financial settlement under section 25(2)(g) of the Matrimonial Causes Act 1973, what happens to money spent on an affair, how a new relationship changes maintenance, and where Scotland differs.</p>

<p>Everything below is England and Wales law and process unless stated otherwise. Before you spend anything testing a conduct argument, run your figures through our <a href="/calculators/settlement-range-estimator">settlement range estimator</a>. It gives you the indicative range a court would work from on needs and sharing alone, with no conduct adjustment, and that baseline is the honest starting point for judging whether an argument about an affair could realistically improve on it.</p>

<h2>Adultery and the no-fault divorce process since April 2022</h2>

<p>The application itself gives you nothing to work with. Under the current process one or both spouses apply on the sole ground that the marriage has broken down irretrievably, and the statement of that fact is conclusive: there is no box for adultery, no naming of a co-respondent, and no defence. The minimum timeline is <strong>26 weeks</strong>: a <strong>20-week reflection period</strong> from application to conditional order, then a <strong>6 weeks and 1 day</strong> wait before the final order (Divorce, Dissolution and Separation Act 2020, in force 6 April 2022). The application fee is <strong>£628</strong> from 13 July 2026.</p>

<p>Ending the marriage and dividing the money are separate exercises. The financial side runs either by agreement, sealed by a consent order with a court fee of <strong>£62</strong>, or through a contested application on Form A with a fee of <strong>£321</strong>. Neither route asks why the marriage ended. Our guide to <a href="/blog/process-and-costs/how-long-does-divorce-take">how long a divorce takes</a> sets out the sequencing, and <a href="/blog/financial-settlements/what-am-i-entitled-to-divorce">what am I entitled to in a divorce</a> covers the section 25 factors that actually drive the division.</p>

<h2>When conduct affects a financial settlement</h2>

<p>Section 25(2)(g) of the Matrimonial Causes Act 1973 requires the court to have regard to "the conduct of each of the parties, if that conduct is such that it would in the opinion of the court be inequitable to disregard it". That last clause is the whole story. In Wachtel v Wachtel in 1973 the Court of Appeal set the threshold as conduct that is obvious and gross, and the courts have held that line ever since. The modern approach, set out by Mr Justice Peel in Tsvetkov v Khayrova in 2023, asks whether the conduct is proved on the evidence and whether it produced an identifiable financial consequence.</p>

<h2>Money spent on an affair and the divorce asset pot</h2>

<p>This is where infidelity comes closest to touching the figures. Where a spouse has spent significant sums on a new relationship, the other spouse can argue for an <strong>add-back</strong>: the court treats the money as notionally still in existence and credits it to the spender's column, so it comes out of their share rather than reducing the pot for both. The Court of Appeal confirmed the principle in Vaughan v Vaughan in 2007.</p>

<p>The limit is just as important. In MAP v MFP in 2015 the court declined to add back very substantial sums a husband had spent, on the basis that it is not the court's function to moralise about how someone chooses to live. The successful arguments involve spending that is wanton or reckless and material relative to the total assets, evidenced by bank statements rather than asserted.</p>

<p>Imogen's situation shows the proportionality point. On a total pot of around £400,000, she could evidence roughly £35,000 her husband had moved out of the joint account over eighteen months, on hotels, a holiday and a car deposit. Adding that back to his column shifts about £17,500 in her direction on an equal division, which comfortably justified putting the transfers in a questionnaire with the statements attached. Had the pot been £3 million, the same £35,000 would not have been worth a contested argument, because the costs of running it would have exceeded the recovery. Our <a href="/blog/process-and-costs/cost-of-divorce-uk">cost of divorce guide</a> gives the cost bands to weigh that against.</p>

<h2>A new partner, spousal maintenance and child maintenance</h2>

<p>A new partner cannot be made a party to your case and cannot be ordered to pay you anything. They matter only through the needs arithmetic. If your spouse now lives with someone who contributes to the household, that reduces your spouse's assessed income need, which can cut a spousal maintenance claim they are making against you. The same logic runs in reverse if you are the one with a new partner. Remarriage goes further: a spousal maintenance order ends automatically on the recipient's remarriage, and remarrying before you have made a financial claim can bar the claim altogether, a trap covered in <a href="/blog/financial-settlements/remarriage-trap-and-time-limits">the remarriage trap and time limits</a>.</p>

<p>Child maintenance sits entirely outside all of this. The Child Maintenance Service applies a formula to the paying parent's gross weekly income: the basic rate is <strong>12%</strong> for one child, <strong>16%</strong> for two children and <strong>19%</strong> for three or more children, with reductions for shared care. Nothing in the calculation asks why the parents separated, and nothing in it responds to conduct by either parent. Our <a href="/blog/maintenance-and-support/spousal-maintenance-guide">spousal maintenance guide</a> and <a href="/blog/maintenance-and-support/child-maintenance-cms-formula">the CMS formula explained</a> set out both calculations.</p>

<h2>Adultery, divorce and financial provision in Scotland</h2>

<p>Scotland was not part of the 2020 no-fault reform. Adultery and unreasonable behaviour remain grounds for divorce there, alongside separation of one year with consent or two years without, under the Divorce (Scotland) Act 1976 as amended. That difference is procedural rather than financial: section 11(7)(a) of the Family Law (Scotland) Act 1985 tells the court to leave conduct out of account unless it has adversely affected the financial resources. The much larger difference for anyone with a Scottish case is that matrimonial property in Scotland is defined more narrowly, broadly property acquired during the marriage, which affects the split far more than any conduct question. Northern Ireland also has its own regime. Take advice in the jurisdiction that applies to you.</p>

<h2>The gap between what happened and what a divorce settlement can fix</h2>

<p>The hardest part of this topic is not the law, it is the mismatch. Being told that the affair which ended your marriage is legally irrelevant can feel like the system endorsing it. It is worth separating the two things. The court is carrying out a resource allocation exercise for two households and any children, working out who needs what and what there is to go round. It is not adjudicating the marriage, and it has never been well suited to doing so.</p>

<p>That distinction matters practically as well as emotionally, because conduct arguments are expensive. Proving contested allegations means witness statements, disclosure and hearing time, and a party who pursues an allegation that does not clear the section 25(2)(g) bar risks a costs order for litigation conduct. Where the feelings are the live issue rather than the figures, counselling or a mediator who can hold the difficult conversation usually does more than a court application, and mediation is a cheaper route to the same financial outcome in most cases.</p>

<p>The realistic summary is this: adultery ended as a legal concept in the divorce process in England and Wales in April 2022, and it almost never changes a financial settlement, because the court divides resources by reference to needs and sharing rather than fault. The exception worth pursuing is financial: money genuinely dissipated on an affair, and a new partner's contribution to household costs, both of which can be evidenced and quantified.
