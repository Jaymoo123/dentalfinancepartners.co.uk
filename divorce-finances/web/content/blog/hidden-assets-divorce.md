faqs:
  - question: "What happens if my ex hides assets in a UK divorce?"
    answer: "Four things can happen, in rising order of severity. First, the court can draw an adverse inference: if the judge is satisfied your ex has not disclosed everything, the court can assume the hidden resources exist and divide the visible assets in your favour to compensate. Second, the court can make a costs order, so your ex pays some or all of the legal costs their concealment caused, which is a real sanction because financial remedy cases normally run on a no-order-for-costs basis. Third, any order obtained by material non-disclosure can be set aside, meaning the whole settlement is reopened, even years later. Fourth, because Form E is signed with a statement of truth, deliberate lies can amount to contempt of court, which carries a fine or imprisonment. The practical message is that hiding assets rarely pays: the tools for finding money are stronger than most people expect, and the penalties compound."
  - question: "How does the court know if someone is lying on Form E?"
    answer: "Mostly through cross-checking rather than intuition. Form E requires 12 months of statements for every bank and building society account, plus property valuations, pension values and 2 years of business accounts. Those documents are compared against each other and against the declared lifestyle. Unexplained transfers out, salary that never lands in a disclosed account, loan repayments to accounts nobody has mentioned, or a declared income that cannot fund the mortgage and school fees all raise questions. After Forms E are exchanged, each side serves a questionnaire demanding explanations and further documents, and the court can order disclosure from third parties such as banks or companies. Where answers stay evasive, the judge is entitled to infer that the reluctance hides something and to make findings accordingly."
  - question: "My husband runs a limited company and says it is worth nothing, but it pays for his car, his travel and a good lifestyle. What can I do?"
    answer: "This is one of the most common hidden-value patterns and it is very solvable. Start with the free record: the company's filings at Companies House show its filed accounts, its shareholdings and any charges over its assets, which you can compare against what he has declared on Form E. A company that is genuinely worthless does not usually fund cars, travel and a comfortable lifestyle, and that mismatch is exactly what questionnaires are for. If the sums justify it, a forensic accountant can be appointed, often as a single joint expert whose cost is shared, to value the company properly, assess the sustainable income it can pay, and identify spending run through the business that is really personal benefit. Courts are familiar with directors talking their company down during a divorce and give little weight to a bare assertion of worthlessness against professional valuation evidence."
  - question: "I found transfers from our joint account to an account I do not recognise. What should I do?"
    answer: "Note the dates and amounts, keep your own copies of the joint account statements (you are entitled to those, the account is yours too), and take advice before confronting your spouse. If proceedings are on foot or about to start, the transfers belong in a questionnaire: your spouse must explain where the money went and disclose the receiving account. If the pattern suggests money is being moved beyond reach, a section 37 application can freeze assets or unwind transfers made to defeat your claim, and a transfer made within the 3 years before your application is presumed to have that intention unless your spouse proves otherwise. What you must not do is log into their personal accounts or take their private documents to investigate: since the Imerman case, self-help evidence of that kind usually has to be handed back and can put you on the wrong side of the court."
  - question: "Can a divorce settlement be reopened if hidden assets are found later?"
    answer: "Yes. A financial order obtained by fraud or material non-disclosure can be set aside, and there is no time limit on dishonesty. The Supreme Court confirmed this in 2015 in Sharland v Sharland and Gohil v Gohil, two cases where husbands had concealed the true financial position and the wives were allowed to reopen concluded settlements. The test is materiality: the non-disclosure must be significant enough that the court would have made a substantially different order had it known the truth. Where the concealment was deliberate, materiality is presumed and it is for the dishonest party to show the order would have been the same anyway. This applies to consent orders too, because the statement of information filed with an agreed order carries the same duty of honesty as Form E."
  - question: "Can my spouse hide cryptocurrency in a divorce?"
    answer: "They can try, but crypto leaves more of a paper trail than its reputation suggests. Almost everyone buys crypto through an exchange, and the purchase shows up as a card payment or bank transfer in the very statements Form E requires. Once an exchange account is identified, its transaction history can be ordered, and transfers to private wallets are visible on the blockchain itself. From 2026 UK crypto platforms must also collect and report customer data to HMRC under new international reporting rules, which shrinks the space for quiet holdings further. In proceedings, a spouse must disclose crypto like any other asset, questionnaires can demand wallet addresses and exchange records, and unexplained transfers to exchanges with no disclosed holdings invite exactly the adverse inferences described above."
  - question: "How much does a forensic accountant cost in a divorce, and is it worth it?"
    answer: "There is no fixed tariff: the fee depends on the size and messiness of what is being investigated, from a focused review of bank statements up to a full valuation of a trading company with international elements. The right way to decide is proportionality. Estimate what is plausibly hidden or undervalued, get a quote for the specific task, and only proceed where the potential recovery is a clear multiple of the fee. In court proceedings the expert is often appointed as a single joint expert under the court rules, instructed by both sides with the cost shared, which keeps the expense down and makes the conclusions harder for either side to dismiss. For a modest asset pot, a well-aimed questionnaire is usually better value than an expert report; for a business, a large pension or an opaque trust, the expert frequently pays for themselves many times over."
  - question: "Can I go through my spouse's emails or documents to prove they are hiding money?"
    answer: "No, and this trips up a lot of people who feel they are only levelling the playing field. Since the Court of Appeal's decision in Imerman, taking, copying or reading a spouse's private documents without permission, including logging into their email or online banking, is unlawful. Material obtained that way generally has to be returned, you may have to pay costs, and in serious cases it exposes you to civil claims or criminal liability under computer misuse and data protection law. It can also poison the court's view of you in a case where credibility matters. The lawful routes are the strong ones: the disclosure duty, questionnaires, third-party disclosure orders, Companies House and Land Registry searches, and section 37 where assets are about to move. If you already hold documents you should not have, tell your solicitor immediately rather than deploying them."

<p>In England and Wales, both spouses owe the court a duty of full and frank financial disclosure, normally delivered on <strong>Form E</strong>: a signed statement covering every account, property, pension and business interest, backed by 12 months of bank statements. Hiding assets is not sharp negotiation, it is misleading the court, and the penalties escalate from adverse inferences and costs orders to the settlement being torn up years later and, in the worst cases, imprisonment for contempt.</p>

<p>This guide explains how the disclosure duty works, what courts actually do to non-disclosers, the tracing routes that find most hidden money, and when a freezing order or a forensic accountant is worth it. One quick sense-check before you read on: run the disclosed asset pot through our <a href="/calculators/settlement-range-estimator">settlement range estimator</a>. If the output looks implausibly low for the length of your marriage and the lifestyle you both led, that mismatch between lifestyle and declared assets is often the first sign the pot is incomplete.</p>

<h2>The duty of full and frank disclosure in a divorce</h2>

<p>Disclosure is not optional and it is not a courtesy between solicitors. Each spouse owes the court itself a duty to give a complete and honest picture of their finances, and the duty is ongoing: if circumstances change before the final order, the picture must be updated. In contested financial remedy proceedings (started on Form A, court fee <strong>£321</strong>), both sides complete <a href="https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/form-e-financial-statement-for-a-financial-order-matrimonial-civil-partnership-or-for-financial-relief-after-an-overseas-divorce-or-dissolution-etc">Form E</a>, which requires 12 months of statements for every bank account, valuations of property and pensions, and 2 years of accounts for any business. It is signed with a statement of truth, so a deliberate lie on it is a lie to the court.</p>

<p>The duty applies just as much to agreed deals. A consent order (court fee £62) is filed with a statement of information summarising both parties' finances, and an agreed order procured by non-disclosure can be unseated in the same way as a litigated one. Our <a href="/blog/process-and-costs/form-e-financial-disclosure-guide">Form E disclosure guide</a> walks through the form section by section, and <a href="/blog/process-and-costs/consent-orders-and-clean-break-orders">consent orders and clean break orders</a> covers the agreed route.</p>

<p>The set-aside cases are worth knowing by name. In 2015 the Supreme Court decided Sharland v Sharland and Gohil v Gohil together, and the principle that emerged is blunt: fraud unravels everything. Where non-disclosure was deliberate, the court presumes it was material, and it is for the dishonest spouse to prove the order would have been the same anyway. A cheat who gets away with it at trial is never actually safe.</p>

<h2>Tracing hidden assets before you agree a financial settlement</h2>

<p>Most hidden assets are not found by private investigators. They are found in paperwork the other side is already obliged to produce, cross-checked against public records anyone can search.</p>

<h3>Bank statements</h3>

<p>The 12 months of statements attached to Form E are the richest source. Look for transfers to accounts that have not been disclosed, regular payments that imply an undisclosed loan or property, salary or dividends that never arrive in any disclosed account, and round-sum withdrawals building up somewhere off the page. Each anomaly becomes a question in the questionnaire that follows exchange of Forms E, and the court can order banks and other third parties to produce records where answers do not add up.</p>

<h3>Companies House</h3>

<p>Every UK company's filings are free to search on the <a href="https://www.gov.uk/get-information-about-a-company">Companies House register</a>: accounts, directorships, shareholdings and persons with significant control. Directors who resign in favour of a new partner shortly before proceedings, new companies formed as the marriage failed, or filed accounts that sit oddly with a claimed worthless business are all visible in minutes, at no cost.</p>

<h3>Land Registry</h3>

<p>If you know or suspect an address, the title register from <a href="https://www.gov.uk/search-property-information-land-registry">HM Land Registry</a> shows the legal owner, the price paid and any mortgages secured on it, for a few pounds per title. Property bought in a sibling's or new partner's name still leaves traces: the deposit had to come from somewhere, which loops back to the bank statements.</p>

<h3>Crypto</h3>

<p>Crypto has a reputation for invisibility it does not deserve. Nearly all holdings start with a card payment or transfer to an exchange, which appears in the disclosed statements. Exchange records can be ordered, blockchain transfers are public, and from 2026 UK platforms must collect and report customer data to HMRC under new international reporting rules. Unexplained payments to an exchange with no declared holdings are a classic trigger for adverse inferences. For wallets, exchange evidence, valuation and the CARF reporting regime in depth, see our <a href="/blog/financial-settlements/crypto-digital-assets-divorce">crypto and digital assets in divorce guide</a>.</p>

<p>One hard warning on tracing: do it lawfully. Since the Imerman decision, helping yourself to a spouse's private documents, or logging into their email or online banking, is unlawful, and the material usually has to be returned. The legitimate machinery, disclosure, questionnaires and third-party orders, is stronger than self-help and does not backfire.</p>

<h2>Freezing orders: section 37 and protecting the divorce pot</h2>

<p>Tracing takes time, and sometimes assets are moving now. <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1973/18/section/37">Section 37 of the Matrimonial Causes Act 1973</a> lets the court act in two directions: it can restrain a disposal that is about to happen, freezing accounts or blocking a sale or transfer, and it can set aside a disposal that has already happened if it was made to defeat your financial claim.</p>

<p>The statute contains a presumption that does a lot of work in practice. Where a disposition was made within the <strong>3 years</strong> before your application and it would have the effect of defeating your claim, the court presumes that was the intention, and it is for your spouse to prove otherwise. Transfers of property to relatives at undervalue, sudden gifts to a new partner and offshore movements in the run-up to proceedings all sit squarely inside that presumption. Applications need solid evidence of a real risk, not just suspicion, and courts expect them to be made promptly, so speak to a specialist quickly if money is visibly moving. The wider machinery of claims and orders is covered in our <a href="/blog/financial-settlements/divorce-financial-settlement-guide">divorce financial settlement guide</a>.</p>

<h2>When a forensic accountant earns their fee in a divorce</h2>

<p>A forensic accountant is not a magic wand, they are an expensive instrument that pays off in specific situations. The decision is proportionality: estimate what is plausibly hidden or undervalued, get a quote for the defined task, and instruct only where the potential recovery is a clear multiple of the fee.</p>

<p>They earn their money in three settings. First, <strong>business valuation</strong>: where one spouse owns a company, an expert values the shares, assesses sustainable income and identifies personal spending run through the business, which matters because a director's bare claim that the company is worthless carries little weight against a professional valuation. Second, <strong>add-backs</strong>: where money has been recklessly dissipated, on gambling, a new partner or spite spending, the expert quantifies it so the court can notionally add it back to the spender's side of the ledger. Third, <strong>lifestyle analysis</strong>: reconstructing true income from spending where declared earnings cannot fund the life actually lived. In proceedings the expert is often appointed as a single joint expert with the cost shared, which halves the bill and makes the findings harder to dismiss. Business owners on either side of this problem should read our <a href="/for/business-owners">business owners hub</a> before positions harden.</p>

<h2>Suspicion is not proof: keeping your divorce strategy proportionate</h2>

<p>Finally, a caution in the other direction. Courts penalise fishing expeditions too: demanding endless disclosure on a hunch, running up costs chasing assets that do not exist, or refusing to settle because the outcome feels wrong rather than provably is. The strongest cases pair a specific anomaly (a transfer, a filing, a lifestyle gap) with the right tool for that anomaly, and stop when the numbers reconcile.</p>

<p>The sequence that works is: read the disclosure properly, check the free public records, put precise questions in the questionnaire, escalate to section 37 or an expert only where the sums justify it, and keep the whole exercise anchored to what a court would actually award.
