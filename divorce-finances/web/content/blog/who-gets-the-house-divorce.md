  - question: "Who usually gets the house in a divorce with children?"
    answer: "Usually the parent who will provide the children's main home keeps the house, or stays in it for a defined period, because the welfare of children under 18 is the court's first consideration under section 25 of the Matrimonial Causes Act 1973. That does not mean the other parent walks away with nothing: they normally keep a share of the equity, either paid out now through a buyout, taken as a larger slice of other assets such as pensions, or deferred under a Mesher order until the children are grown and the house is sold. The court also has to house the leaving parent adequately, so the outcome balances both households, with the children's stability given the most weight."
  - question: "Does it matter whose name the house is in?"
    answer: "Between divorcing spouses, far less than people think. The family home is a matrimonial asset whoever holds the legal title, and the court can transfer it, order its sale, or adjust the shares regardless of the deeds. A spouse who owns no legal share also has home rights, a statutory right to occupy the family home, which can be registered against the title at the Land Registry so the property cannot be sold or mortgaged over their head while the marriage lasts.   - question: "Is the house always split 50/50 in a divorce?"
    answer: "No. Equal sharing is the starting point for matrimonial assets after a long marriage, but it gives way to needs, and housing the children is the biggest need of all. Courts routinely order unequal splits of the sale proceeds, for example 60/40 or 70/30, where the parent with day-to-day care needs more capital to rehouse, or where one spouse has much lower earnings and mortgage capacity. The house is also never looked at in isolation: a spouse might take more of the equity and less of the pensions, or vice versa. The overall settlement has to be fair across everything, not each asset individually."
  - question: "I moved out of the family home during the separation. Have I given up my claim to it?"
    answer: "No. Moving out does not forfeit your interest in the property or your financial claims. If you are a joint owner you remain a joint owner, and if the house is in your spouse's sole name your home rights and your settlement claims survive your departure. It is sensible to register home rights at the Land Registry if you are a non-owning spouse, and to keep contributing to the mortgage if you can, since arrears damage both parties. What moving out can affect is practical momentum, because courts are slow to disturb children who are settled in the home, but the legal claim itself is intact."
  - question: "The house is in my husband's sole name and I have the children. Can he sell it or make us leave?"
    answer: "Not without your position being protected. As his spouse you have home rights of occupation in the family home under the Family Law Act 1996, even though you own no legal share, and you can register those rights at the Land Registry (for free) so a buyer or lender would take subject to them, which in practice blocks a sale or remortgage behind your back. Within the divorce itself, the court can transfer the house to you, order him to pay you a share, or postpone any sale while the children need the home. The sole name on the deeds affects the paperwork, not the outcome. Register the home rights notice early, before anything else."
  - question: "What is a Mesher order and when would a court use one?"
    answer: "A Mesher order postpones the sale of the family home until a trigger event, most commonly the youngest child turning 18 or finishing secondary education. The parent with the children stays in the house until then; when the trigger arrives the house is sold and the equity divided in the shares fixed by the order. Courts use it where the children need the home now but an outright transfer to one parent would be unfair to the other, and where neither a sale nor a buyout works today, typically because the staying parent cannot raise a mortgage alone and there is not enough money for two purchases. The price is continued financial entanglement: the leaving spouse waits years for their capital, and both need advice on the tax position at the eventual sale."
  - question: "Can I keep the house if I cannot get a mortgage on my own?"
    answer: "Sometimes, but the mortgage is usually the binding constraint. Keeping the house normally means a buyout, and lenders will only release your ex from a joint mortgage if you pass affordability checks alone, with maintenance and benefits sometimes countable as income. If you cannot, the realistic alternatives are: negotiating a Mesher order so the sale is deferred while you and the children stay; taking a transfer of the house with the mortgage staying joint for a period (some orders provide for this, though lenders and departing spouses resist it); or accepting a sale and using your enlarged share of the equity to buy or rent somewhere smaller. <p>There is no rule in England and Wales that the family home goes to the wife, the husband, or the person named on the deeds. The court's first consideration under <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1973/18/section/25">section 25 of the Matrimonial Causes Act 1973</a> is the welfare of any children under 18, so in practice the parent who will house the children usually has the strongest claim to stay in the house, at least for a period. Everything else, including whose name is on the title and who paid the deposit, is weighed after that.</p>

<p>The house is never decided in isolation. It is one asset inside the overall <a href="/blog/financial-settlements/divorce-financial-settlement-guide">financial settlement</a>, traded against pensions, savings and earning power. Because of that, arguing about the house before you know the shape of the whole deal is usually backwards: our <a href="/calculators/settlement-range-estimator">settlement range estimator</a> gives you a realistic bracket for your overall split first, so you can see how much room there actually is for one of you to keep the property.</p>

<p>This guide covers who ends up with the house and why. If you already know a buyout is the route, the mechanics (equity maths, remortgaging, transfer of equity, the tax rules) live in our separate <a href="/blog/the-family-home/house-buyout-transfer-of-equity-divorce">house buyout and transfer of equity guide</a>.</p>

<h2>The family home is decided by needs, not by names</h2>

<p>The family home is a matrimonial asset whoever legally owns it. On divorce the court can transfer it from one spouse to the other, order it sold, or fix the shares, regardless of the deeds, as set out in the government's guidance on <a href="https://www.gov.uk/money-property-when-relationship-ends">money and property when a relationship ends</a>.</p>

<p>A spouse who owns no legal share is also protected while the divorce runs. Under the Family Law Act 1996 a non-owning spouse has <a href="https://www.gov.uk/home-rights">home rights</a>: a statutory right to occupy the family home, which can be registered against the title at the Land Registry free of charge so the property cannot quietly be sold or remortgaged. Moving out does not surrender these rights, and it does not surrender your claim to a share. Sole ownership only starts to carry real weight in short, childless marriages where the house was brought in by one party.</p>

<h2>The s.25 factors: how a divorce court weighs the house</h2>

<p>Section 25 gives the court a checklist, applied to the whole settlement rather than the house alone:</p>

<ul>
  <li><strong>The welfare of any child under 18</strong>, which is the first consideration and drives most housing outcomes.</li>
  <li><strong>Income, earning capacity and other resources</strong> of each spouse, now and in the foreseeable future, which in house terms means mortgage capacity.</li>
  <li><strong>Needs and obligations</strong>, above all the need of each household to be adequately housed.</li>
  <li><strong>The standard of living</strong> during the marriage, the parties' ages, and the length of the marriage.</li>
  <li><strong>Contributions</strong>, financial and non-financial: raising children and running the home counts alongside paying the mortgage.</li>
  <li><strong>Any disability, and conduct</strong> in rare, extreme cases.</li>
</ul>

<p>Two principles sit on top of the checklist. Sharing says matrimonial assets start at an equal split after a long marriage. Needs says that where equal sharing leaves one household, especially the children's household, without adequate housing, the split moves. Needs win. That is why 60/40 and 70/30 divisions of house equity are routine where one parent must rehouse children on a lower income. Both spouses must give full financial disclosure before any of this can be weighed properly, normally via <a href="/blog/process-and-costs/form-e-financial-disclosure-guide">Form E</a>.</p>

<h2>Children first: housing needs shape the divorce outcome</h2>

<p>Where there are children under 18, the practical question a court asks is: which parent will provide the children's main home, and what does that home need to look like? Stability weighs heavily, so if the children are settled in the family home and it is affordable to keep, the parent caring for them day to day has a strong claim to stay in it. The other parent must still be housed adequately, both for their own sake and so the children can stay with them, which is why the outcome is a balance rather than a winner-takes-all award. Ongoing support for the children is dealt with separately through <a href="/blog/maintenance-and-support/child-maintenance-cms-formula">child maintenance</a>, but the capital question, who keeps the bricks, is settled here.</p>

<h3>Sale and split</h3>

<p>The default where neither spouse can, or wants to, keep the property. The equity (value minus mortgage and sale costs) is divided in whatever shares the settlement fixes. On a £300,000 home with a £120,000 mortgage, £180,000 of equity split 60/40 gives one spouse £108,000 and the other £72,000 to rehouse with. A transfer or sale between separating spouses is normally free of capital gains tax under the no gain, no loss rules in <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1992/12/section/58">section 58 TCGA 1992</a>, which since 2023 run for 3 tax years after separation, and without time limit under a court order; the detail is on our <a href="/capital-gains-tax-divorce">capital gains tax on divorce hub</a>.</p>

<h3>Buyout</h3>

<p>One spouse keeps the home and pays the other for their share of the equity, usually by remortgaging in their sole name. The lender must agree they can afford the borrowing alone, which makes mortgage capacity, not fairness, the usual sticking point. The full mechanics, including the transfer of equity, the SDLT exemption for transfers under a divorce agreement and the CGT position, are in the <a href="/blog/the-family-home/house-buyout-transfer-of-equity-divorce">buyout guide</a>.</p>

<h3>Mesher order</h3>

<p>Where the children need the home but a buyout is unaffordable, the court can postpone the sale until a trigger, most often the youngest child turning 18 or finishing secondary education. The resident parent stays; at the trigger the house is sold and the equity divided in the fixed shares. It preserves the children's home at the cost of keeping the ex-spouses financially tied together for years, and it can leave the leaving spouse with a capital gains bill at the eventual sale; our <a href="/blog/the-family-home/mesher-orders-deferred-house-sale">Mesher orders guide</a> works through that tax trap with a full example.</p>

<h3>Offsetting against pensions</h3>

<p>Where one spouse holds a large pension, a common trade is house for pension: one keeps most or all of the equity, the other keeps more of the retirement fund. It looks clean but the exchange rate is treacherous, because pension value and house equity are not like-for-like money: one is future income, taxed and locked away, the other is spendable capital now. See <a href="/blog/pensions-and-divorce/pension-sharing-vs-offsetting">pension sharing vs offsetting</a> before agreeing anything on this basis.</p>

<h2>Renting vs owning after the divorce</h2>

<p>One household becoming two means the same money must stretch further, and for many couples the honest arithmetic is that one or both will rent for a period. That is not automatically the worse deal. A spouse who takes £70,000 to £100,000 of equity in cash but cannot yet raise a mortgage may be better renting for two or three years while their borrowing capacity recovers than fighting for a house they cannot finance. The trap to avoid is a settlement built on optimism: if keeping the house depends on a mortgage offer you do not yet have, get the offer in principle before you sign. And a spouse buying a new home before the old one is transferred out of joint names should watch the 5 percent SDLT additional-dwelling surcharge, which can catch second purchases in the gap.</p>

<h2>Make the family home outcome binding with a consent order</h2>

<p>Whatever you agree about the house, it is not binding until a court seals it in a financial consent order, which costs £62 and can include a clean break closing off future claims. Without one, either of you can reopen the question of the home years later, whatever was said or even done at the time. How these orders work is covered in <a href="/blog/process-and-costs/consent-orders-and-clean-break-orders">consent orders and clean break orders</a>.</p>
