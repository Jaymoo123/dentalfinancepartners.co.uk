faqs:
- question: What is a good day rate for a self-employed electrician in 2026?
  answer: There is no single correct figure, but published 2026 guides give useful anchors. Consumer cost guides such as Checkatrade and MyBuilder put domestic work at roughly £45 to £60 per hour across most of the UK and £80 to £100 in London and the South East, which implies a domestic day rate of around £250 to £400 for an eight-hour day. Trade-facing guides report commercial day rates of roughly £300 to £500 and specialist rates (testing and inspection, EV charging, data centres, hazardous areas) of roughly £300 to £450. These are trade-reported and consumer-quote ranges rather than official statistics, so treat them as a benchmark to test your own numbers against, not as a published rate card.
- question: Should I charge by the hour, by the day or per job?
  answer: Most self-employed electricians use all three. Hourly suits short reactive work such as a fault find or a socket swap, where the time is genuinely unknown and a minimum call-out charge protects you. Day rate suits subcontract work on site, where a contractor books you for a period and supplies the materials. Per job (fixed price) suits defined domestic work such as a consumer unit change or a rewire, where you know the scope and can carry the risk of it running long in exchange for keeping the gain when it runs short. Per point pricing, a fixed price per socket, light or circuit, is a middle route used on repeat first-fix work.
- question: Does the 20% CIS deduction mean I should charge more?
  answer: No. The CIS deduction is an advance payment against your own income tax and Class 4 National Insurance, not a fee or a commission. Charging more to compensate would simply increase the amount deducted. What it does affect is cash flow, because 20% of your labour leaves your account before you have paid for diesel, cable, test equipment or insurance. The practical response is to hold a tax and cash reserve and file your return promptly, not to inflate your rate.
- question: Is the CIS deduction taken on materials as well as labour?
  answer: No. CIS deductions apply to the labour element of your invoice only. The cost of materials you have purchased for the job is excluded from the deduction base, along with VAT. If you invoice £1,400 with £900 labour and £500 materials, the 20% is applied to the £900, giving a £180 deduction and £1,220 received, not a £280 deduction. If a contractor is deducting on your invoice total, you are being over-deducted and should split your invoices explicitly.
- question: I currently earn £42,000 employed. What day rate do I need to match it self-employed?
  answer: Work backwards rather than comparing headline figures. Assume 200 chargeable days a year once holiday, sickness, quoting, certification and admin are taken out. Add your annual running costs (van, insurance, test equipment calibration, scheme membership, phone, software), which for a working domestic electrician commonly land somewhere between £8,000 and £14,000. To clear £42,000 of profit on 200 days with, say, £11,000 of costs, you need £53,000 of turnover, which is £265 per chargeable day before any margin for growth or bad debt. Note that as a sole trader you also lose employer pension contributions, holiday pay and sick pay, so matching profit to salary is not matching the package.
- question: What happens to my pricing if I go over the VAT threshold?
  answer: Once your taxable turnover passes £90,000 in a rolling twelve-month period you must register for VAT. For commercial and subcontract work this is usually neutral, because your customers are VAT registered and recover the VAT, and the domestic reverse charge often means you do not charge VAT on the invoice at all. For domestic customers it is effectively a 20% price rise you cannot pass on, so the practical response is to review whether the price you quote householders needs rebuilding, and to model the position before you cross the line rather than after.
- question: Do I need to change my prices when I take on an apprentice or a mate?
  answer: Yes, in both directions. A second pair of hands raises your chargeable output, so per-job pricing usually becomes more profitable, but it also introduces employer costs. From April 2025 employer National Insurance is 15% on earnings above the £5,000 secondary threshold, and you will also carry pension auto-enrolment, employers liability insurance and payroll administration. Price the fully loaded cost of the employee (gross pay plus employer NIC plus pension plus their share of van and tooling), not their hourly wage.
- question: How do I set a rate for testing and inspection work?
  answer: "Testing and inspection is normally priced per job or per circuit rather than by the hour, because the deliverable is a certificate and a report rather than time on site. Trade guides put specialist testing and inspection day rates at the upper end of the market, broadly in line with commercial rates. Two things justify the premium: the qualification and calibrated test equipment behind the certificate, and the professional liability you take on when you sign it. Make sure your calibration cycle and insurance cover are priced into the rate rather than absorbed as overhead."
- question: Should I quote fixed prices or day rates for a rewire?
  answer: Fixed price, in almost every case, provided you have surveyed the property. Domestic customers on a large job want certainty, and a day rate on an open-ended rewire invites disputes about pace. The risk transfers to you, so build in a contingency for what you cannot see behind plaster, state clearly what is excluded (making good, decoration, unforeseen structural work) and stage the payments so materials are funded before you buy them. If the property is genuinely unknown, price the survey and first-fix strip-out separately and quote the rest once the fabric is open.
- question: Are my qualification and scheme costs tax deductible?
  answer: Renewals and updates to skills you already hold are deductible revenue expenses, which covers competent person scheme membership, ECS card renewal, assessment visits, test equipment calibration and wiring regulations update courses. Training that gives you a genuinely new qualification outside your existing trade is capital in nature and generally not deductible as a revenue expense. Test instruments themselves are capital items and are normally relieved in full in the year of purchase through the Annual Investment Allowance.

<h2>What self-employed electricians charge in 2026, before CIS and expenses</h2>

<p>Published UK cost guides put a domestic electrician's hourly rate at roughly <strong>£45 to £60</strong> in 2026, rising to <strong>£80 to £100</strong> in London and the South East, with day rates commonly quoted between <strong>£250 and £400</strong>. Those figures come from consumer-facing quote guides, so they describe what a householder is told to expect, not what a subcontractor is paid on site. The gap between the two is where most pricing mistakes live.</p>

<p>Below are the ranges the main public guides report for 2026, what basis each one is quoted on, and where the number comes from. Treat every row as a trade-reported benchmark to test your own numbers against. None of these are official statistics and none of them know your overheads.</p>

<p>Two corrections before you use any of it. First, a labour-only subcontract day rate is usually below the domestic figure, because the main contractor supplies the materials, provides continuity of work and absorbs the cost of winning it. Second, if you are benchmarking against employed operatives, look up the current JIB national standard rates directly rather than working from a blog, because they are revised and a stale figure is worse than no figure.</p>

<h2>Subcontractor day rates versus direct customer pricing</h2>

<p>The commercial trade-off between the two is real, and it is not only about the headline number.</p>

<p>Subcontract work through a main contractor gives you volume, predictable weeks and no sales cost. In exchange you accept a lower rate, payment terms you did not set, retention on some contracts, and the <strong>20% CIS deduction</strong> on your labour. Direct domestic work pays more per hour but you carry the quoting, the chasing, the material purchasing, the warranty calls and the gaps between jobs.</p>

<p>Most self-employed electricians run a mix, and the mix is the actual pricing decision. A subcontract day rate that fills four days a week at a predictable margin can outperform a higher domestic rate that fills two and a half days. Work out your realistic chargeable days under each before you decide which rate is better.</p>

<h2>Pricing models: hourly, day rate, per point and per job for CIS subcontractors</h2>

<p>Four models cover almost all electrical work. Each one puts the risk of a job overrunning in a different place.</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Hourly</strong>, usually with a minimum charge. Best for fault finding, reactive call-outs and small alterations where the time genuinely cannot be known in advance. The customer carries the overrun risk, so keep a written minimum and a clear start time.</li>
<li><strong>Day rate</strong>. Best for subcontract site work, where a contractor books you for a period and supplies materials. The contractor carries the overrun risk. Confirm what happens to a half day and to travel days before you start.</li>
<li><strong>Per point</strong>. A fixed price per socket, light point or circuit. Best for repeat first-fix work on new build and refurbishment where the work is uniform and you can measure your own output per day accurately.</li>
<li><strong>Per job (fixed price)</strong>. Best for defined domestic work: consumer unit changes, rewires, EV charger installations. You carry the overrun risk and keep the gain when it runs short, which is why it is the most profitable model for an electrician who estimates well and the most damaging for one who does not.</li>
</ul>

<p>A practical rule for fixed pricing: never quote a fixed price on a property you have not surveyed. Behind plaster, in loft spaces and under floors is where fixed-price jobs are lost.</p>

<h2>What your rate must cover: expenses, insurance and non-chargeable days</h2>

<p>A day rate is not income. It is turnover from which everything else has to come first, and the two costs electricians most often forget are non-chargeable time and vehicle depreciation.</p>

<p>There are around 253 working days in a year before holiday. A self-employed electrician who takes four weeks off, loses a few days to sickness, and spends one day a fortnight quoting, ordering, certifying and chasing invoices is realistically charging for around <strong>200 days</strong>. That is a 21% reduction in chargeable days against the theoretical maximum, so your rate needs to be roughly 25% higher than a naive salary calculation suggests.</p>

<p>Then the annual running costs. The categories below are the ones that recur for a working domestic and light commercial electrician. The amounts vary widely, so treat the structure as the point rather than the figures.</p>

<h2>The CIS deduction is not a cost, but it is a cash flow problem</h2>

<p>When a contractor pays you, they deduct <strong>20%</strong> if you are registered, <strong>30%</strong> if you are not, or nothing at all if you hold gross payment status. Critically, the deduction applies to the <strong>labour element only</strong>. Materials you have bought for the job are excluded from the deduction base, as is VAT.</p>

<p>Worked through: an invoice of £1,400 made up of £900 labour and £500 materials produces a deduction of 20% on the £900, which is <strong>£180</strong>, and a payment to you of <strong>£1,220</strong>. If the contractor deducts on the full £1,400 you lose £100 of cash flow on that one invoice for no reason. Split labour and materials explicitly on every invoice.</p>

<p>None of that changes your price. The deduction is an advance payment of the income tax and Class 4 National Insurance you would owe anyway, and it is credited back on your Self Assessment return. What it does is remove a fifth of your labour income before you have paid for anything, which is why so many electricians have a profitable year and a difficult cash year at the same time.</p>

<h2>Rate rises, VAT registration and the £90,000 turnover point</h2>

<p>Two thresholds should shape how you plan rate increases rather than surprise you after the fact.</p>

<p>The first is <strong>VAT registration at £90,000</strong> of taxable turnover on a rolling twelve-month basis. For subcontract and commercial work this is largely neutral, because your customers recover the VAT and the domestic reverse charge frequently means no VAT is charged on the invoice at all. For domestic customers it is a 20% price increase you cannot pass on, so an electrician approaching the threshold on mainly domestic work has a genuine strategic decision to make about mix and pricing.</p>

<p>The second is <strong>MTD for Income Tax</strong>. From April 2026, sole traders with gross income above £50,000 are in scope for quarterly digital reporting, and the threshold drops to £30,000 from April 2027. The word that catches CIS subcontractors out is <strong>gross</strong>. In the example above, the test figure is the £56,000 invoiced, not the £44,800 actually received after deductions. Our guide to <a href="/blog/vat-and-mtd/mtd-income-tax-cis">MTD for Income Tax and CIS</a> covers the mechanics.</p>

<p>If your turnover is growing towards these levels, it is also the point at which the trading structure question becomes live. The comparison is set out in <a href="/blog/cis-basics/cis-sole-trader-vs-limited-company">CIS: sole trader or limited company</a>, and the practical difference is that a limited company can recover CIS deductions in real time through the Employer Payment Summary rather than waiting for a year-end refund.</p>

<h2>Pricing mistakes that cost electrician subcontractors money</h2>

<p>Four patterns recur when reviewing the numbers behind an electrical business.</p>

<p><strong>Pricing off a salary figure.</strong> Dividing a target salary by 253 days ignores holiday, sickness, quoting time and the absence of employer pension contributions. Use 200 chargeable days as a starting assumption and adjust from your own diary.</p>

<p><strong>Not splitting labour and materials on invoices.</strong> On a materials-heavy job this is straightforwardly expensive, because the contractor deducts 20% from money that should never have entered the calculation. The correction is a two-line invoice, nothing more.</p>

<p><strong>Holding a rate for years.</strong> Van costs, insurance, calibration and material prices move every year. A rate held for three years is a real-terms pay cut, and the easiest time to raise it is when you take on a new contractor or a new domestic customer, not mid-contract.</p>

<p><strong>Treating the CIS refund as profit.</strong> It is the return of an overpayment, and it is already inside the £36,633 in the example above. Spending it as a windfall means spending money that was always yours and was already counted.</p>

<p>Setting a rate is an arithmetic problem before it is a market problem: work out your realistic chargeable days, your true annual running costs and your tax position, and the floor beneath your price becomes obvious. The published benchmarks tell you whether that floor is competitive in your region, and the gap between the two is your margin. Where the benchmarks and your own numbers disagree, your numbers win.</p>
