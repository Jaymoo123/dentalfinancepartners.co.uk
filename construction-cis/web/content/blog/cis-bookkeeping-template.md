faqs:
- question: What should a CIS subcontractor bookkeeping template include?
  answer: Four records. An income ledger with date paid, contractor, invoice number, labour value, materials value, VAT, CIS rate applied, CIS deducted and net received. An expenses ledger with date, supplier, description, amount, business-use percentage and an SA103F expense category. A mileage log with date, start point, destination, purpose and miles. A deduction statement tracker showing which contractors have issued statements for which tax months. Every figure on a self-assessment return for a CIS sole trader comes out of one of those four.
- question: Do I need accounting software or is a spreadsheet enough for CIS bookkeeping?
  answer: A spreadsheet is legally sufficient for a CIS sole trader until Making Tax Digital for Income Tax applies to you, at which point you need MTD-compatible software or bridging software to submit quarterly updates. The thresholds are £50,000 qualifying income from 6 April 2026, £30,000 from 6 April 2027 and £20,000 from 6 April 2028, based on the income reported two tax years earlier. Below those thresholds a well-structured spreadsheet is fine, and the column structure in this guide transfers directly into software when you do switch.
- question: How do I record CIS deductions in my books?
  answer: Record the gross invoice value as income, not the net amount that hits your bank. Then record the CIS deducted as a separate column. If you enter only the £880 that arrived rather than the £1,000 invoiced with £120 deducted, your turnover is understated and the £120 you are owed disappears from the return. The CIS deducted column totals to box 81 on the SA103F, which is what generates the repayment.
- question: What if a contractor never sends me a CIS deduction statement?
  answer: Contractors are required to give you a written payment and deduction statement within 14 days of the end of each tax month in which they made a deduction. If one does not arrive, request it in writing and record the request date in your tracker. Your remittance advice, invoice and bank statement together evidence the deduction if the statement never comes, but a missing statement is a common reason repayments stall while HMRC reconciles your figure against the contractor's monthly return. Chase during the year, not the following January.
- question: I invoice a single total with no labour and materials split. Does that matter for my bookkeeping?
  answer: Yes, significantly. If your invoice shows one figure, the contractor may apply the 20% deduction to the whole amount including your materials, and materials are excluded from the CIS deduction base. On a £2,000 invoice with £800 of materials, that is £160 deducted that should never have been taken. Split the values on the invoice and carry the same split into your income ledger so the two agree.
- question: How long do I need to keep my bookkeeping records?
  answer: At least five years after the 31 January self-assessment filing deadline for the relevant tax year. For 2026/27, filed by 31 January 2028, that means keeping records until at least January 2033. Digital records satisfy the requirement, and once MTD for Income Tax applies to you, digital records become mandatory rather than optional.
- question: Can I claim mileage and actual van costs in the same year?
  answer: No, not for the same vehicle in the same tax year. You pick one method per vehicle and stay with it for that year. This is why your template needs a mileage log even if you intend to claim actual costs. You need the business mileage figure either to make the flat-rate claim or to calculate the private-use restriction on the actual-costs claim. Keeping the log costs nothing and preserves the choice.
- question: What is the £90,000 figure on the self-assessment expenses section?
  answer: If your annual turnover was below £90,000 you may enter a single total expenses figure in box 31 of the SA103F rather than breaking spending down across boxes 17 to 30. That is a filing shortcut, not a record-keeping shortcut. You still need the underlying categorised records to support the total, and categorising as you go is what shows you which expense lines you have forgotten.

<h2>What a CIS subcontractor bookkeeping template actually has to do</h2>

<p>Four running records cover every figure a CIS sole trader needs for a complete self-assessment return: an income ledger, an expenses ledger, a mileage log and a deduction statement tracker. Between them they carry roughly twenty columns. Everything else that gets added to a subcontractor's spreadsheet over the years is decoration.</p>

<p>The columns below are the working structure, set out as tables you can copy straight into a spreadsheet or use as a checklist against whatever you already run. The order matters less than the fact that each one exists, because the two most expensive bookkeeping failures in construction are both structural: recording the net payment instead of the gross invoice, and recording a single job total instead of a labour and materials split.</p>

<p>This is a subcontractor template. If you pay other subcontractors yourself, you are a contractor as well and have a CIS300 monthly filing obligation on top of everything here, which a spreadsheet cannot discharge.</p>

<h2>Sheet 1: the CIS income and deduction ledger</h2>

<p>One row per payment received, not per invoice raised. Payment date is what determines the tax month a CIS deduction falls into, and it is what your contractor's monthly return is built on.</p>

<table>
<thead>
<tr><th>Column</th><th>What goes in it</th><th>Why it earns its place</th></tr>
</thead>
<tbody>
<tr><td>Date paid</td><td>Date the money reached your account</td><td>Determines the tax month and the tax year the deduction belongs to</td></tr>
<tr><td>Contractor</td><td>Name of the paying contractor</td><td>Groups the year by contractor for statement reconciliation</td></tr>
<tr><td>Invoice number</td><td>Your reference</td><td>Ties the row back to the invoice and the job</td></tr>
<tr><td>Labour (£)</td><td>Labour element, excluding VAT</td><td>This is the only figure the CIS deduction should be applied to</td></tr>
<tr><td>Materials (£)</td><td>Materials you supplied, at cost, excluding VAT</td><td>Excluded from the deduction base. Also a box 17 expense</td></tr>
<tr><td>Other charges (£)</td><td>Plant hire, consumables, recharges</td><td>Treatment varies. Keep separate so it can be classified correctly</td></tr>
<tr><td>Net invoice total (£)</td><td>Labour plus materials plus other</td><td>Feeds your turnover figure</td></tr>
<tr><td>VAT (£)</td><td>VAT charged, or zero under the reverse charge</td><td>Never part of the CIS deduction base</td></tr>
<tr><td>CIS rate</td><td>0%, 20% or 30%</td><td>Flags anything deducted at the wrong rate immediately</td></tr>
<tr><td>CIS deducted (£)</td><td>Amount withheld</td><td>Totals to box 81 on the SA103F. This is the refund</td></tr>
<tr><td>Net received (£)</td><td>Amount actually banked</td><td>Reconciles the row to your bank statement</td></tr>
<tr><td>Statement received</td><td>Yes, no, or date chased</td><td>Turns a January panic into a monthly two-minute check</td></tr>
</tbody>
</table>

<p>The rate column does more work than it looks. A registered subcontractor should see <strong>20%</strong>, an unregistered one <strong>30%</strong>, and a subcontractor with gross payment status <strong>0%</strong>. If a 30% row appears in a ledger that is otherwise 20%, a contractor has failed to verify you or has verified you incorrectly, and every week that goes unnoticed is another 10% of your labour income sitting with HMRC.</p>

<p>The labour and materials columns are what stop over-deduction. If you invoice a single total, a contractor can apply the deduction to the whole amount, materials included. Our guide to <a href="/blog/cis-basics/cis-invoice-splitting-labour-materials">splitting labour and materials on CIS invoices</a> covers what counts as materials and how to structure the invoice, and the <a href="/cis-invoice-template">CIS subcontractor invoice template</a> gives you invoice layouts that already carry the split in the right places, including reverse charge and non-VAT-registered versions.</p>

<h2>Sheet 2: the expenses ledger, mapped to the self-assessment boxes</h2>

<p>The single most useful thing you can do with an expenses sheet is give it a category column whose values are the actual SA103F expense box headings. Categorise as you enter, and the return fills itself. Categorise in January from a carrier bag, and things get missed.</p>

<table>
<thead>
<tr><th>Column</th><th>What goes in it</th></tr>
</thead>
<tbody>
<tr><td>Date</td><td>Date of the receipt or invoice, not the date you paid the card bill</td></tr>
<tr><td>Supplier</td><td>Merchant, wholesaler, insurer, training provider</td></tr>
<tr><td>Description</td><td>Enough detail to justify the claim two years later</td></tr>
<tr><td>Amount (£)</td><td>Net of VAT if you are VAT registered, gross if you are not</td></tr>
<tr><td>Business use %</td><td>100% for most trade spending, lower for mixed-use phone or broadband</td></tr>
<tr><td>Claimed (£)</td><td>Amount multiplied by business use percentage</td></tr>
<tr><td>Category</td><td>The SA103F expense box heading (see below)</td></tr>
<tr><td>Job reference</td><td>Which job the cost relates to, where it relates to one</td></tr>
<tr><td>Receipt held</td><td>Photo filename, or a link if the receipt sits in software</td></tr>
</tbody>
</table>

<p>The category values to use are the expense headings on the SA103F self-employment pages. On the 2026 form (the 2025/26 return) they run from box 17 to box 30, and the ones that carry real weight for construction trades are:</p>

<p>Two practical notes. Box numbers move between the short pages (SA103S) and the full pages, and occasionally between years, so match on the heading rather than memorising a number. And if your annual turnover was below <strong>£90,000</strong>, HMRC lets you enter one total in box 31 instead of splitting across boxes 17 to 30. That is a filing shortcut only. The categorised records still have to exist, and the act of categorising is what shows you the empty rows where a genuine claim should be.</p>

<p>What belongs in each category, and the awkward edges (subsistence, workwear, use of home, capital items above the small-tools line), are covered in full in our guide to <a href="/blog/expenses/allowable-expenses-cis-subcontractor">allowable expenses for CIS subcontractors</a>.</p>

<h2>Sheet 3: the mileage log behind your biggest expenses claim</h2>

<p>Mileage is usually the largest single deduction a subcontractor makes and the one most often lost, because HMRC expects a contemporaneous journey record and a reconstruction from memory does not qualify. Five columns, filled in weekly.</p>

<table>
<thead>
<tr><th>Column</th><th>Example entry</th></tr>
</thead>
<tbody>
<tr><td>Date</td><td>14/09/2026</td></tr>
<tr><td>Start point</td><td>Home</td></tr>
<tr><td>Destination</td><td>Site, Wellington Road development, plot 12</td></tr>
<tr><td>Purpose</td><td>Second fix, Barrett contract</td></tr>
<tr><td>Miles</td><td>34</td></tr>
</tbody>
</table>

<p>Add a running total and a rate calculation at the foot. From <strong>6 April 2026</strong> the Approved Mileage Allowance Payments rate is <strong>55p per mile for the first 10,000 business miles</strong> in a car or van and <strong>25p per mile</strong> thereafter, up from 45p. On 14,000 business miles that is (10,000 x 55p) + (4,000 x 25p) = £5,500 + £1,000 = <strong>£6,500</strong>. Any template still calculating at 45p is understating the claim by £1,000 across the first 10,000 miles.</p>

<h2>Sheet 4: the CIS deduction statement tracker</h2>

<p>The fourth sheet is the smallest and prevents the most delay. One row per contractor per tax month, with a column for the deduction figure on their statement and a column for the deduction figure in your income ledger. Where the two disagree, the difference gets chased in the same month rather than the following January.</p>

<p>Contractors must issue a written payment and deduction statement within 14 days of the end of each tax month in which a deduction was made. Repayments most often stall because a subcontractor's box 81 total does not reconcile to what contractors reported on their monthly returns. Chasing a missing statement in October is a phone call. Chasing it in February, after filing, is a repayment sitting on hold. Retention periods and what HMRC asks for in a compliance check are set out in our <a href="/blog/cis-compliance/cis-record-keeping-guide">CIS record keeping guide</a>.</p>

<h2>MTD-proofing the habit before the thresholds reach you</h2>

<p>Making Tax Digital for Income Tax phases in by qualifying income, measured from the return two tax years earlier:</p>

<table>
<thead>
<tr><th>Qualifying income</th><th>Tax year measured</th><th>You must comply from</th></tr>
</thead>
<tbody>
<tr><td>Over £50,000</td><td>2024/25</td><td>6 April 2026</td></tr>
<tr><td>Over £30,000</td><td>2025/26</td><td>6 April 2027</td></tr>
<tr><td>Over £20,000</td><td>2026/27</td><td>6 April 2028</td></tr>
</tbody>
</table>

<p>For a CIS subcontractor, qualifying income means <strong>gross turnover before CIS deductions</strong>, not the net figure that reached the bank. A joiner banking £48,000 after 20% deductions on £60,000 of labour is measured on £60,000. That distinction catches people out every year and is covered in detail in our guide to <a href="/blog/vat-and-mtd/mtd-income-tax-cis">MTD for Income Tax and CIS</a>.</p>

<p>A spreadsheet stops being sufficient on its own once MTD applies to you, because quarterly updates have to be submitted from compatible software or through bridging software that reads your sheet. The column structure above is what makes that transition trivial: every field maps onto a field in QuickBooks, Xero, FreeAgent or Sage without restructuring anything. The trade-offs between the two approaches are set out in our comparison of <a href="/blog/software-and-tools/spreadsheets-vs-accounting-software-cis">spreadsheets versus accounting software for CIS workers</a>.</p>

<p>Four sheets, about twenty columns, ten minutes a week. That is the whole of CIS bookkeeping for a sole trader subcontractor, and it is the difference between a repayment claim built on evidence and one built on estimates. The gross-not-net rule and the labour-materials split are the two structural points to get right on day one, because neither can be reconstructed later from a bank statement.</p>
