faqs:
- question: Do bookkeepers legally need to be registered in the UK?
  answer: There is no licence or register you must join to use the word bookkeeper, but there is a legal supervision requirement. Anyone providing bookkeeping, accountancy or tax services to clients by way of business must be supervised for anti-money laundering purposes under the Money Laundering Regulations 2017, either through a professional body that acts as a supervisory authority or by registering directly with HMRC as an accountancy service provider. A bookkeeper who has neither is trading unlawfully.
- question: How do I check whether a bookkeeper is AML supervised?
  answer: Ask them directly which supervisor they use and for their registration or membership number, then verify it. If they say HMRC, search the Supervised Business Register published on gov.uk using their trading name and postcode. If they name a professional body such as ICB, IAB, AAT, ACCA or ICAEW, use that body's own member or practice directory. Do not accept a certificate image as proof on its own, because certificates carry an expiry date and lapse quietly.
- question: Is bookkeeper a protected title?
  answer: No. Bookkeeper and accountant are both unprotected in UK law, so anyone can use either term regardless of training, experience or insurance. The protected terms are chartered accountant, chartered certified accountant and statutory auditor, which can only be used by members of the relevant chartered body or firms registered for audit. This is why the supervision check and the qualification check matter more than the job title on the invoice.
- question: What qualifications should a bookkeeper working on CIS have?
  answer: Look for membership of a recognised body such as the Institute of Certified Bookkeepers, the Institute of Accountants and Bookkeepers, AAT, ACCA or ICAEW, because membership brings a code of conduct, mandatory professional indemnity insurance and continuing professional development. On top of that, ask specifically about construction experience, because the CIS rules on labour and materials splits, verification, gross payment status and monthly returns are not covered in general bookkeeping training.
- question: What if my bookkeeper files my CIS return late or gets it wrong?
  answer: HMRC issues the penalty to you, not to your bookkeeper. Late CIS300 filing runs at £100 after one day, £200 after two months, then £300 or 5% of the liability at six months and £300 or 100% at twelve months. You can appeal on the basis that you relied on someone else and they failed to act, but you have to show you supplied everything in time and acted as soon as you discovered the problem. If the bookkeeper carries professional indemnity insurance you may be able to recover the penalty from them, which is one of the main practical reasons to insist on it.
- question: A bookkeeper has quoted me a low monthly fee, is unsupervised and works cash in hand. Should I use them?
  answer: No. An unsupervised bookkeeper is committing a regulatory offence before they touch your books, which tells you what standard the rest of the work will be held to. There will be no professional indemnity insurance behind a mistake, no professional body to complain to, and no continuity if they stop trading mid tax year with your records on their laptop. On a CIS refund position running into thousands of pounds, the saving on the monthly fee is small next to what a rejected claim or a compliance check costs.
- question: My bookkeeper has been keeping my CIS records in a spreadsheet. Is that still allowed from April 2026?
  answer: It depends on your qualifying income. Making Tax Digital for Income Tax applies from 6 April 2026 to sole traders and landlords with qualifying income above £50,000, from 6 April 2027 above £30,000 and from 6 April 2028 above £20,000. Inside MTD you must keep digital records and file quarterly updates through compatible software, so a spreadsheet on its own no longer satisfies the requirement unless it is bridged into compatible software. Qualifying income for a CIS subcontractor is gross turnover before deductions, not the net amount that reached your bank.
- question: Does my bookkeeper need authorisation to deal with HMRC for me?
  answer: Yes. To file returns, see your CIS deductions or speak to HMRC about your affairs, a bookkeeper must be set up as your authorised agent, normally through an agent services account with authorisation granted by you. If someone is logging into your own Government Gateway account with your credentials rather than acting as an authorised agent, that is a warning sign about how the rest of their practice is run, and it leaves you carrying every submission made in your name.
- question: Can I use a bookkeeper and an accountant at the same time?
  answer: Yes, and for many construction businesses that is the sensible split. The bookkeeper handles day to day records, invoices, payment and deduction statements, VAT reverse charge coding and payroll entries. The accountant handles the year end position, the self-assessment or corporation tax return, the CIS refund claim or EPS reclaim, gross payment status and tax planning. What matters is that both are supervised and that the handover of records between them is clean and digital.

<h2>The one registration nobody handling your CIS records can skip</h2>

<p>Any business that provides bookkeeping, accountancy or tax services to clients in the UK must be supervised for anti-money laundering purposes. That supervision comes either from a professional body listed as a supervisory authority under the Money Laundering Regulations 2017, or from HMRC directly, where the business registers as an accountancy service provider. There is no third option and no threshold below which a one-person bookkeeping business falls outside it.</p>

<p>This is the answer to the question most subcontractors are actually asking. There is no bookkeepers' register you must join to use the word bookkeeper, but there is a supervision requirement, and a bookkeeper who holds neither professional body membership nor an HMRC registration is trading in breach of the regulations. This guide covers how to verify that supervision, what the qualifications behind the title actually mean, the practical checks to run before you hand over a year of CIS payment and deduction statements, and where the liability sits when something goes wrong.</p>

<p>HMRC's own registration route gives a sense of how seriously it is treated. On the fees published on gov.uk at the time of writing in August 2026 (current from 1 December 2025), an accountancy service provider registering with HMRC pays a <strong>£300 application fee</strong>, an annual <strong>£400 fee per premises</strong>, and <strong>£40 per person</strong> for the approval check HMRC runs on the responsible individuals. Businesses found trading unsupervised face a financial penalty plus a <strong>£2,000 sanction administration charge</strong>, and HMRC publishes details of businesses that have not complied.</p>

<h2>No protected title: what "qualified" really means for a CIS bookkeeper</h2>

<p>Bookkeeper is not a protected title in UK law. Neither is accountant. Anyone can print either word on a van, a website or an invoice with no examination, no experience and no insurance behind it. The terms that are genuinely restricted are <strong>chartered accountant</strong>, <strong>chartered certified accountant</strong> and <strong>statutory auditor</strong>, which are limited to members of the relevant chartered body or to firms registered for audit work.</p>

<p>What fills the gap is voluntary membership of a professional body. Membership matters for three concrete reasons rather than the letters after a name: the body acts as the AML supervisor, it requires professional indemnity insurance in practice, and it gives you a complaints route with teeth if the work is negligent. The bodies you will most often see behind a UK bookkeeper are:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>ICB</strong> (Institute of Certified Bookkeepers) and <strong>IAB</strong> (Institute of Accountants and Bookkeepers), both bookkeeping-specific</li>
<li><strong>AAT</strong> (Association of Accounting Technicians), common for licensed accountants and bookkeepers in practice</li>
<li><strong>ACCA</strong>, <strong>ICAEW</strong> and <strong>CIMA</strong> for accountants, with practising certificate requirements attached</li>
<li><strong>ATT</strong> and <strong>CIOT</strong> where the work is tax-led rather than record-led</li>
</ul>

<p>A useful sense check for construction work: general bookkeeping training does not cover the Construction Industry Scheme. The labour and materials split, verification of subcontractors, monthly CIS300 filing and the VAT domestic reverse charge are all specialist ground. Membership tells you someone is accountable. Construction experience tells you they will not learn the scheme on your records. For the wider picture on what a construction specialist actually does, see our guide to <a href="/blog/cis-basics/what-is-a-cis-accountant">what a CIS accountant does</a>.</p>

<p>One nuance on the register that catches people out. HMRC's Supervised Business Register only lists businesses supervised by HMRC. A bookkeeper supervised by ICB, AAT or ACCA will not appear on it at all, and that is entirely correct. Absence from the register only tells you something once you have asked which route they use. There is also a publication lag, so a genuinely new registration may not show immediately.</p>

<h2>What a bookkeeper can do on your CIS filings, and what needs authorisation</h2>

<p>A bookkeeper can maintain your records, code your income and expenses, reconcile your bank, log your <a href="/blog/cis-compliance/cis-record-keeping-guide">CIS payment and deduction statements</a>, run payroll and prepare figures. To actually file on your behalf or speak to HMRC about your account, they need to be set up as your authorised agent, normally through an agent services account with authorisation granted by you.</p>

<p>The distinction matters. If someone is logging into your own Government Gateway credentials rather than acting as an authorised agent, every submission is made as you, there is no audit trail separating their work from yours, and you have handed over access to your entire tax account. That arrangement is common and it is a bad idea regardless of how competent the bookkeeper is.</p>

<p>Contractors have an extra layer to think about here, because the <a href="/blog/cis-compliance/cis-monthly-return-guide">monthly CIS300 return</a> is a filing obligation with its own deadline of the 19th of the following tax month, and from 6 April 2026 nil returns are mandatory again for months with no subcontractor payments. A bookkeeper who is not diarised to file those is a penalty waiting to happen.</p>

<h2>MTD for Income Tax raises the bar for CIS bookkeeping</h2>

<p>Making Tax Digital for Income Tax changes what "good enough" bookkeeping means. Sole traders and landlords with qualifying income above <strong>£50,000</strong> are in scope from <strong>6 April 2026</strong>, above <strong>£30,000</strong> from <strong>6 April 2027</strong> and above <strong>£20,000</strong> from <strong>6 April 2028</strong>. In scope means digital record keeping and quarterly updates filed through compatible software.</p>

<p>Two points bite specifically for CIS subcontractors. First, qualifying income is <strong>gross turnover before CIS deductions</strong>, not the net figure that reached your bank, so a subcontractor invoicing £60,000 gross and receiving £48,000 after 20% deductions is measured on the £60,000. Second, a once a year shoebox handover no longer works operationally, because the quarterly rhythm needs records kept as you go. Our guide to <a href="/blog/vat-and-mtd/mtd-income-tax-cis">MTD for Income Tax and CIS</a> covers the mechanics, and <a href="/blog/software-and-tools/best-cis-accounting-software">CIS accounting software</a> covers which packages handle deductions properly.</p>

<h2>Red flags when hiring a bookkeeper for construction work</h2>

<p>Certain answers should end the conversation rather than prompt a follow-up question.</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>No AML supervisor, or a shrug about the question.</strong> This is the single strongest signal. A bookkeeper who does not know who supervises them does not have a supervisor.</li>
<li><strong>No professional indemnity insurance.</strong> Without it, a mistake that costs you a £3,000 refund and a penalty is simply your loss.</li>
<li><strong>Wanting your Government Gateway login instead of agent authorisation.</strong></li>
<li><strong>Guaranteeing a refund figure before seeing your records.</strong> Refunds depend on gross income, deductions suffered and allowable expenses, and nobody can promise a number in advance.</li>
<li><strong>Suggesting expenses you did not incur.</strong> Inflated claims are your liability on your return, and they sit badly with an HMRC compliance check.</li>
<li><strong>Not knowing that CIS deductions apply to labour only.</strong> Materials are excluded from the deduction base, and a bookkeeper who does not know this will systematically miscode your income.</li>
<li><strong>Holding your records hostage at the end of the relationship.</strong> A professional body member is bound by a code of conduct on handover. See our guide on <a href="/blog/cis-basics/switching-cis-accountant-guide">switching CIS accountant</a> for how a clean transfer should work.</li>
</ul>

<h2>Your return, your penalties: where liability actually sits</h2>

<p>This is the part that surprises subcontractors most. Whoever prepares the figures, the self-assessment return, the CIS300 and the MTD quarterly update are legally yours. You sign the declaration and HMRC assesses any penalty against you.</p>

<p>HMRC does list relying on someone else who then failed to act as a potential reasonable excuse for a late return, but it is an appeal you have to make after the penalty has already been issued to you, and it only holds if you gave them everything in good time and acted as soon as you found out. For inaccuracies rather than lateness, the test is tighter still: an agent's error is treated as yours unless you can show you took reasonable care to avoid it, which in practice means choosing a supervised and qualified bookkeeper and reviewing what they produce rather than signing blind.</p>

<p>The practical consequences of a bookkeeper's error are worth seeing in pounds. Take a subcontractor invoicing £2,000 with £1,200 of labour and £800 of materials. Handled correctly, the contractor deducts 20% of the labour element, £240, and the subcontractor receives £1,760. If the bookkeeper never sets up the invoice split, the deduction is taken on the full £2,000, which is £400, and £160 of cash flow leaves the business on that invoice alone. Repeat that across a year of invoicing and the amount tied up with HMRC until the return is filed runs into thousands.</p>

<p>Where a penalty does arise from the bookkeeper's failure rather than yours, professional indemnity insurance is the route to recovery. That only exists if they hold it, which is why the insurance question belongs in the same conversation as the supervision question.
