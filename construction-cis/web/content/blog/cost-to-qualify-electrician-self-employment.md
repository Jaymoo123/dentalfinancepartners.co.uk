faqs:
- question: How much does it cost to become an electrician in the UK?
  answer: "By the adult (non-apprenticeship) route, training providers publishing 2026 prices put the total at roughly £9,700 to £11,200. That covers the Level 2 and Level 3 electrical installation diplomas, the 18th Edition wiring regulations course, the Level 3 NVQ portfolio and assessment, the AM2 practical assessment, an ECS card, a first set of hand tools and a multifunction tester. Individual providers price these very differently, so the range is wide and worth shopping around. By the apprenticeship route the training itself is funded through the employer and the apprenticeship levy, so your direct cost is close to nil, but you spend three and a half to four years on apprentice pay."
- question: Is an electrical apprentice paid through CIS or PAYE?
  answer: "PAYE. An apprentice is an employee of the firm training them, on a contract of employment, with income tax and Class 1 National Insurance deducted through payroll. The Construction Industry Scheme applies to payments made to genuinely self-employed subcontractors, not to employees. If a firm tries to put an apprentice through CIS to avoid employer National Insurance and holiday pay, that is an employment status problem for the firm, not a shortcut for you."
- question: Can I claim my electrical training costs against tax?
  answer: "It depends on whether the trade already exists. HMRC's guidance at BIM35660 tests whether the training relates to your existing business area. Renewals, refreshers and upskilling in the trade you already carry on (an 18th Edition update, EV charge point or battery storage training for a working electrician, a competent person scheme reassessment, an ECS card renewal) are normally allowable revenue expenses. The initial cost of qualifying, incurred before you have started trading as an electrician, is generally not deductible because there is no trade to set it against. Keep the invoices regardless, because the position can differ if you were already trading in a closely related capacity."
- question: I am a self-employed plumber retraining as an electrician. Is that training deductible?
  answer: "Take advice before claiming it. HMRC rewrote BIM35660 to allow new skills that keep pace with technological advances and changes in industry practice in your existing business area, which is more generous than the old position. But the guidance is explicit that training which allows you to start a new business or expand into a new, unrelated area of business is unlikely to be deductible, and the published examples (a makeup artist taking a tattooing course, a taxi driver taking a painting and decorating course) sit on the disallowable side. A full electrical qualification taken by a plumber is a genuinely arguable case that turns on how the two activities sit together in one business, so it needs a considered view rather than an assumption."
- question: What does it cost to run as a self-employed electrician once qualified?
  answer: "The recurring costs most newly self-employed electricians carry are a competent person scheme registration (published NICEIC domestic installer fees start from around £670 plus VAT a year and NAPIT typically sits lower, commonly quoted in the £400 to £600 range), public liability and tools insurance, ECS card renewal every three years, test equipment calibration, van running costs, phone, and replacement consumables and hand tools. Budget for the scheme assessment fee in year one on top of the annual subscription."
- question: How much of my invoice does the contractor take under CIS?
  answer: "If you are registered as a CIS subcontractor, 20% of the labour element. Materials you have supplied and invoiced separately are excluded from the deduction base entirely, so an invoice of £1,400 labour plus £600 materials produces a deduction of £280, not £400. If you have not registered, the rate is 30%. If you hold gross payment status, it is 0% and nothing is withheld. The deduction is an advance payment against your income tax bill, not an extra tax."
- question: Why do self-employed electricians usually get a tax refund?
  answer: "Because the 20% is taken off your labour income before a single expense has been counted. Your actual liability is calculated on profit, meaning income after van costs, tools, insurance, scheme fees, PPE and everything else, and that figure is almost always lower than the advance already collected. In the worked example in this guide, £11,200 was deducted through the year against an actual tax and National Insurance bill of £8,559, leaving a refund of £2,641. The size of your refund depends on your gross income and how completely your expenses are recorded."
- question: I go self-employed in November. What do I need to do about tax?
  answer: "Register as self-employed with HMRC and register separately as a CIS subcontractor before you take your first payment from a contractor, because an unregistered subcontractor has 30% deducted rather than 20%. Your first Self Assessment return will cover the tax year in which you started, filed by the following 31 January. Start keeping mileage and expense records from day one rather than reconstructing them later, and keep every CIS payment and deduction statement your contractors issue, because those statements are the evidence for the refund claim."
- question: Should a newly qualified electrician set up a limited company straight away?
  answer: "Usually not on day one. In the first year or two, income is uneven, startup costs are high and profits are often modest, which is where sole trader simplicity works well and where a Self Assessment refund arrives without any payroll infrastructure. A limited company becomes worth modelling once profits are consistently well above the level you actually draw, or where contractors will only engage limited companies. The company route also changes how CIS is recovered, through the Employer Payment Summary in real time rather than through a year-end return."
- question: Does the mileage rate really cover the cost of running a van?
  answer: "The Approved Mileage Allowance Payments rate from 6 April 2026 is 55p per mile for the first 10,000 business miles and 25p per mile after that, for a car or van. It is a flat allowance covering fuel, insurance, servicing, tyres and depreciation together, so on a cheap older van it can exceed what you actually spend, and on a newly financed van it can fall well short. The alternative is to claim actual running costs plus capital allowances on the purchase price, which is more record-keeping but often produces a larger deduction in the first year. You pick one method per vehicle per tax year and cannot mix them."

<h2>What the qualification routes cost before any CIS work starts</h2>

<p>Training providers advertising 2026 prices put the adult route to a full electrical qualification at roughly <strong>£9,700 to £11,200</strong>, once the diplomas, the NVQ, the AM2 assessment, an ECS card and a first set of tools and test gear are all counted. The apprenticeship route costs almost nothing in fees but takes 42 to 48 months on apprentice pay. Which one is affordable depends far more on your age and your outgoings than on the total figure, and the answer changes again once you look at what a self-employed electrician actually clears after CIS deductions and allowable expenses.</p>

<p>The qualification stack itself is well defined. To reach ECS Gold Card standard as an installation and maintenance electrician you need the Level 2 and Level 3 electrical installation diplomas (City and Guilds 2365), the 18th Edition wiring regulations qualification (2382), the Level 3 NVQ in competence (2357), the AM2 practical assessment, and a current ECS health, safety and environmental assessment. Apprentices normally take an integrated qualification (5357) that folds the knowledge and competence units together, which is why 2365 and 2357 as separate purchases are largely an adult-learner concern.</p>

<p>The figures below are <strong>published commercial training prices as advertised in 2026</strong>, not a regulated fee scale. Providers differ by several thousand pounds on the same route, and packages bundle items in different combinations, so treat these as a budgeting range and get written quotes before committing.</p>

<p>The number that never appears on a provider's price list is the earnings you give up while training full time. For anyone with a mortgage and dependants, that figure usually dwarfs the course fees, and it is the real reason the two routes suit different people.</p>

<h2>The apprenticeship route: employed on PAYE, not a CIS subcontractor</h2>

<p>An electrical apprenticeship runs three and a half to four years and the training is funded through the employer and the apprenticeship levy rather than out of your pocket. The trade-off is pay. From <strong>1 April 2026</strong> the apprentice National Minimum Wage is <strong>£8.00 per hour</strong>, which applies if you are under 19 or in the first year of the apprenticeship. After that first year, an apprentice aged 19 or over moves onto the age-based rate: <strong>£10.85</strong> for ages 18 to 20 and <strong>£12.71</strong> for 21 and over. Many electrical employers pay above these floors on the JIB stage scale, which rises through each stage of the apprenticeship.</p>

<p>One point matters for anyone reading this on a construction tax site. An apprentice is an <strong>employee on PAYE</strong>, with income tax and Class 1 National Insurance taken through payroll and the employer paying employer National Insurance at 15% on earnings above £5,000 a year. You are not a CIS subcontractor and there is no CIS deduction to reclaim. If a firm proposes putting an apprentice through CIS, that is an employment status problem for the firm rather than an opportunity for you, and it is the sort of arrangement HMRC looks at closely.</p>

<h2>Which training costs are allowable expenses, and which are not</h2>

<p>The question that costs newly qualified electricians the most money is whether the £10,000 they spent qualifying can be claimed. Usually it cannot, and the reason is timing rather than the nature of the spend.</p>

<p>HMRC's position sits at BIM35660 in the Business Income Manual, which was rewritten to be materially more generous than the old rule. The old position allowed only training that updated existing knowledge, and treated anything giving a new skill as capital. The current guidance says that costs of <strong>acquiring new skills or knowledge to keep pace with advancements in technology and changes in industry practices, related to the owner's existing business area, will usually be allowable</strong>. Training ancillary to the main trade, such as bookkeeping or digital skills, is allowable on the same basis. What remains outside is expenditure that lets someone start a new business or expand into a new, unrelated area.</p>

<p>The practical consequence for electricians is a clean split.</p>

<p>Keep every invoice from the qualifying period even though the deduction is unlikely. If you were already trading in a closely related capacity, for example as a self-employed electrical improver doing second-fix work under supervision, the analysis is genuinely arguable and worth putting to an accountant rather than assuming either way. The wider position on what a working subcontractor can claim is set out in our guide to <a href="/blog/expenses/allowable-expenses-cis-subcontractor">allowable expenses for CIS subcontractors</a>.</p>

<p>If you trade through a company, the treatment differs again. A company paying for a director's work-related training normally gets a corporation tax deduction and, under the work-related training exemption in ITEPA 2003, the director is not taxed on it as a benefit in kind. That is one of several reasons the structure question is worth revisiting once you are established, covered in <a href="/blog/cis-basics/cis-sole-trader-vs-limited-company">CIS sole trader or limited company</a>.</p>

<h2>Employed salary against a self-employed CIS day rate: the 2026/27 numbers</h2>

<p>Here is the comparison that actually decides the question. On the employed side, the JIB national rate for a graded Electrician following the January 2026 increase is <strong>£18.38 per hour</strong>, which on a 37.5 hour week across 52 weeks is <strong>£35,841</strong> a year. On the self-employed side, trade-reported day rates for a qualified electrician in 2026 typically run <strong>£250 to £350 for domestic work and £300 to £500 for commercial</strong>, with London at the top and the North East, Wales and Northern Ireland at the bottom. The example below uses <strong>£280 a day across 200 chargeable days</strong>, which deliberately builds in holidays, quoting time, weather and the days nobody pays you for.</p>

<p>The £10,512 of business costs in that column is built from 12,000 business miles claimed at the 2026/27 Approved Mileage Allowance Payments rate of <strong>55p per mile for the first 10,000 miles and 25p thereafter</strong> (£6,000), tools and test equipment (£2,000), insurance (£700), a competent person scheme fee (£600), PPE (£300), card and registration renewals (£250), phone at 70% business use (£350) and use of home (£312). Anything still quoting 45p per mile is using the pre April 2026 rate.</p>

<p>The gap is about <strong>£7,600 a year</strong> in favour of self-employment on these assumptions, and it is real. What it does not include is the employed side's holiday pay, statutory sick pay, employer pension contributions and the fact that a quiet fortnight costs an employee nothing. Strip out four unpaid weeks of illness and the difference narrows sharply. A day rate is not a salary with a bigger number on it, and the 200 day assumption is the single figure most worth stress-testing against your own diary before you hand in notice.</p>

<h2>What the CIS deduction does to your cash flow in year one</h2>

<p>The £11,200 in the table above is not a tax on top of your bill. It is an <strong>advance payment against income tax</strong>, taken by the contractor and paid to HMRC on your behalf, and it is applied to the <strong>labour element only</strong>. Materials you buy and invoice separately are excluded from the deduction base entirely, which is why splitting labour and materials clearly on every invoice matters from your first job. The mechanics of the 0%, 20% and 30% rates are covered in <a href="/blog/cis-basics/cis-deduction-rates-explained">CIS deduction rates explained</a>.</p>

<p>For a newly self-employed electrician the timing is brutal. You spend on tools, test gear, a van and a scheme registration in the first months, while 20% of every labour invoice leaves before you see it, and the refund that squares the account does not arrive until after the tax year ends and the return is filed. Registering as a CIS subcontractor before the first payment is therefore not paperwork for later: an unregistered subcontractor has <strong>30%</strong> deducted instead of 20%, which on £56,000 of labour is £5,600 of extra cash withheld for a year. The registration steps are in <a href="/blog/cis-basics/how-to-register-for-cis">how to register for CIS</a>.</p>

<h2>Startup costs and the expenses a newly self-employed electrician can claim</h2>

<p>Beyond the qualification spend, going out on your own carries a second wave of costs, most of which are deductible from the moment the trade starts.</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Van.</strong> Either flat rate mileage at 55p and 25p, or actual running costs plus capital allowances on the purchase price. Vans with a payload above one tonne avoid the private-use restrictions that apply to cars, so the Annual Investment Allowance can take the whole purchase price in year one.</li>
<li><strong>Test equipment.</strong> A multifunction tester, plus periodic calibration, which is an ongoing revenue cost.</li>
<li><strong>Competent person scheme registration.</strong> Required to self-certify notifiable domestic work rather than paying building control per job. Published NICEIC domestic installer fees start from around £670 plus VAT a year; NAPIT is commonly quoted lower, in the £400 to £600 range. Expect an assessment fee in year one on top.</li>
<li><strong>Insurance.</strong> Public liability as a minimum, plus tools and van cover, and employers liability the moment you take anyone on.</li>
<li><strong>Card and qualification renewals.</strong> ECS card every three years, health and safety reassessment alongside it.</li>
<li><strong>Software and record-keeping.</strong> Making Tax Digital for Income Tax obligations bite by gross turnover, not by what lands in your bank after CIS, so a subcontractor invoicing £60,000 of labour is measured on £60,000 rather than the £48,000 received.</li>
</ul>

<p>The capital side of that list, particularly the van and the larger test gear, is where first-year claims are most often understated. Our guide to <a href="/blog/expenses/vans-tools-capital-allowances-trades-2026-27">vans, tools and capital allowances for trades in 2026/27</a> sets out how the purchase price itself is relieved rather than just the running costs.</p>

<h2>Registering as a CIS subcontractor once you go self-employed</h2>

<p>Two registrations, not one. You register as self-employed with HMRC for Self Assessment, and you register separately as a CIS subcontractor so that contractors verify you at 20% rather than 30%. Contractors are required to verify your status with HMRC before the first payment, so the registration needs to be done in advance rather than in the same week the invoice goes in.</p>

<p>From there the annual rhythm is straightforward: keep every payment and deduction statement your contractors issue, log mileage as you go, record expenses against jobs, and file the Self Assessment return that reconciles the CIS already deducted against the tax actually due. Once turnover is consistently above £30,000 net of materials, gross payment status becomes worth applying for, at which point the deduction drops to 0% and the cash flow problem disappears entirely.</p>

<p>Qualifying is the expensive part and it is mostly not deductible, because the trade does not exist yet. Everything after that point is: the renewals, the upskilling, the van, the tools, the scheme fees and the insurance all reduce the profit your tax is calculated on, which is exactly why most CIS electricians are owed money back at the end of the year. The decision between a JIB salary and a day rate is a genuine one, but it should be made on the numbers after tax and after costs, not on the headline rate.</p>
