faqs:
- question: What is an EICR certificate?
  answer: An EICR (Electrical Installation Condition Report) is a periodic inspection and test of a fixed electrical installation, carried out and reported against BS 7671, the IET Wiring Regulations. It is a condition report rather than a certificate of new work. The inspector examines the consumer unit, the wiring, the earthing and bonding, accessories and protective devices, carries out dead and live tests on each circuit, and then classifies any departures from the current standard using the codes C1, C2, C3 and FI. The overall result is recorded as satisfactory or unsatisfactory.
- question: How long is an EICR valid for?
  answer: There is no single statutory expiry. For private rented and social rented homes in England the maximum interval is five years, and the same five-year maximum applies in Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland. The report itself can specify a shorter interval, and where it does, that shorter interval is the one that binds. Owner-occupied homes have no statutory requirement at all, with ten years being the commonly quoted industry recommendation. Commercial and industrial installations are usually inspected on a three-year or five-year cycle depending on the environment and the duty holder's risk assessment.
- question: Who is allowed to carry out and sign off an EICR?
  answer: The regulations require a qualified and competent person. In practice that means an electrician holding a current BS 7671 qualification, a Level 3 inspection and testing qualification such as City and Guilds 2391-51 or 2391-52, calibrated test equipment, and either registration with a competent person scheme or a signed competence checklist covering their qualifications, experience and insurance. From 1 October 2026 the Electrotechnical Assessment Specification closes the route that allowed periodic inspection work to be signed off on documented experience alone, so confirm your own position directly with your scheme operator.
- question: How much should I charge for an EICR?
  answer: Trade-reported cost guides in 2026 put typical domestic EICR prices at roughly £120 to £200 for a one or two bedroom flat, £180 to £300 for a three bedroom house, and £250 to £400 for a larger four or five bedroom property, with London and the South East sitting at the upper end of each band. Those are market observations, not a rate card. Price your own work from your target hourly recovery, the number of circuits, the age and accessibility of the installation, and your travel and report-writing time, then sense-check the result against the local range.
- question: A letting agent has offered me twelve EICRs a month at a fixed £110 each. Is that worth taking?
  answer: Work out your recovery per site hour before you answer. If a typical flat takes two hours on site plus thirty minutes of travel and twenty minutes of report writing, £110 is roughly £38 per productive hour before any expenses. Once you deduct fuel at the AMAP rate of 55p per mile for the first 10,000 business miles from 6 April 2026, certification software, test equipment calibration and your scheme fees, the margin is thin. Volume arrangements can still work if the properties are clustered, the paperwork is standardised and the agent reliably passes you the remedial work, because the remedial work is where the margin usually sits. Model it on a full month of realistic mileage before committing.
- question: I did the EICR for a main contractor and then the remedial rewiring. How should the invoice be split for CIS?
  answer: "Split the invoice into three lines: the inspection and testing fee, the remedial labour, and the materials. HMRC treats inspection and reporting alone as being in the nature of a survey and outside the scheme, whereas the remedial work is a construction operation. The CIS deduction should be applied to the remedial labour element only, at 20% if you are registered or 30% if you are not, with materials excluded from the deduction base. If the contractor applies 20% to your whole invoice total, you are funding HMRC on income that should never have entered the calculation, and the correction can be slow."
- question: Does an unsatisfactory EICR mean the property is unsafe to occupy?
  answer: Not necessarily. A report is unsatisfactory if any C1, C2 or FI code is recorded. A C1 means danger is present and there is a risk of injury, which normally requires immediate action and a written notification to the client before you leave site. A C2 means potentially dangerous, which is unsatisfactory but not an immediate hazard. An FI means further investigation is needed without delay. C3 codes recommend improvement and do not on their own make a report unsatisfactory. In England the landlord then has 28 days, or a shorter period if the report specifies one, to complete the remedial work.
- question: Do I need to be NICEIC or NAPIT registered to issue an EICR?
  answer: Scheme registration is not the only lawful route. The English guidance allows a landlord to rely on an inspector who signs a checklist confirming their qualifications, experience and insurance. In practice most letting agents, managing agents and social landlords will only instruct scheme-registered electricians, and many procurement frameworks make it a condition of the contract. Treat the annual scheme fee as a cost of accessing the compliance market. It is an allowable business expense either way.
- question: Is my test equipment and calibration tax deductible?
  answer: Yes. Multifunction testers, clamp meters, proving units and the annual calibration are all incurred wholly and exclusively for the trade. Calibration and repair are revenue expenses claimed in the year. A new multifunction tester is a capital item, but the Annual Investment Allowance normally allows the full cost to be deducted in the year of purchase for a sole trader or a limited company. Certification software subscriptions, scheme membership fees and the report-writing element of your admin time all sit in the same allowable pool.
- question: Will EICR income push me over the VAT registration threshold?
  answer: It counts toward taxable turnover in the same way as any other work. The VAT registration threshold is £90,000 of taxable turnover on a rolling twelve month basis, and a volume EICR contract can move a sole trader across it faster than expected because the fee is invoiced gross with no CIS deduction reducing what arrives in the bank. Watch the rolling twelve month figure monthly rather than at year end. If you register and then work for VAT-registered contractors on remedial work, the domestic reverse charge rules will apply to that element.
- question: If EICR income is outside CIS, does that change my tax bill?
  answer: The total tax due is the same, but the timing is completely different. On CIS work, 20% of your labour income has already been paid to HMRC before you see it, which is why most subcontractors are owed a refund at year end. On EICR fees paid gross, nothing has been paid in advance, so the liability builds up quietly until the Self Assessment deadline. Electricians who shift a large share of their turnover to direct compliance work often get a nasty surprise in their first January afterwards. Move a fixed percentage of every gross invoice into a separate tax account as it lands.

<h2>What an EICR is, and what the subcontractor signing it is certifying</h2>

<p>The maximum financial penalty a local authority in England can impose on a landlord who breaches the electrical safety standards rose from £30,000 to £40,000 under the 2025 extension regulations. That single figure explains why demand for EICRs has held up while other electrical work has softened, and why a self-employed electrician or CIS subcontractor with the right qualification can build a predictable book of compliance work around it.</p>

<p>An Electrical Installation Condition Report is a periodic inspection and test of an existing fixed electrical installation, carried out and reported against BS 7671, the IET Wiring Regulations. It is not a certificate for new work. An Electrical Installation Certificate (EIC) or a Minor Electrical Installation Works Certificate (MEIWC) covers work you have carried out. An EICR records the condition of an installation that already exists, whoever installed it.</p>

<p>The inspection covers the origin of the supply and the consumer unit, earthing and protective bonding, the condition of cables, accessories and enclosures, the suitability of protective devices, and the presence of RCD protection where the current standard requires it. It combines visual inspection with dead tests (continuity, insulation resistance, polarity) and live tests (earth fault loop impedance, RCD operation), circuit by circuit, and normally requires the supply to be isolated for part of the visit.</p>

<p>One point worth tracking in 2026: BS 7671:2018+A4:2026 was published on 15 April 2026, and the previous version, BS 7671:2018+A2:2022 incorporating A3:2024, is not withdrawn until 15 October 2026. Your report has to state the edition and amendment you inspected against, so check what your certification software is defaulting to.</p>

<h2>C1, C2, C3 and FI: the codes that decide what the subcontractor reports</h2>

<p>The classification codes are where the commercial consequences sit, because they determine whether the report comes back satisfactory and whether the client faces a 28 day remedial clock.</p>

<table>
<thead>
<tr><th>Code</th><th>Meaning</th><th>Effect on the report</th></tr>
</thead>
<tbody>
<tr><td>C1</td><td>Danger present, risk of injury</td><td>Unsatisfactory. Immediate action required and the client must be notified in writing before you leave site</td></tr>
<tr><td>C2</td><td>Potentially dangerous</td><td>Unsatisfactory. Remedial work required</td></tr>
<tr><td>C3</td><td>Improvement recommended</td><td>Does not make the report unsatisfactory and does not require remedial work</td></tr>
<tr><td>FI</td><td>Further investigation required without delay</td><td>Unsatisfactory until the investigation is completed</td></tr>
</tbody>
</table>

<p>In England, where a report is unsatisfactory the landlord must complete the remedial work within <strong>28 days</strong>, or sooner if the report specifies a shorter period, and obtain written confirmation from a qualified person that the standards are now met. The report must reach an existing tenant within 28 days of the test, a new tenant before occupation, a prospective tenant within 28 days of a request, and the local council within 7 days of a request.</p>

<p>Over-coding is a genuine commercial risk. Recording as C2 what the current Best Practice Guidance treats as C3 generates remedial work the client did not need, and agents notice. Under-coding is worse, because a C1 you did not raise is your professional exposure. Keep your coding defensible against the published industry guidance rather than against what wins you the next job.</p>

<h2>Qualifications to sign off an EICR, and the 1 October 2026 change for every electrical subcontractor</h2>

<p>The regulations across all four nations require the work to be done by a qualified and competent person, without listing a qualification in the statutory text. The substance sits in industry standards instead.</p>

<p>The practical baseline is a current BS 7671 qualification, a Level 3 inspection and testing qualification (City and Guilds 2391-51 for periodic inspection, or 2391-52 covering both initial verification and periodic inspection), calibrated test equipment, and either competent person scheme registration or a signed competence checklist. In England the guidance expressly allows the checklist route, covering qualifications, experience and insurance, as an alternative to scheme membership. In commercial reality, agents and registered providers usually insist on scheme registration.</p>

<p>The change to plan for: under the Electrotechnical Assessment Specification 2024, the route that allowed periodic inspection and testing to be signed off on documented experience alone closes on <strong>1 October 2026</strong>. Scheme operators are assessing against individual qualification rather than firm-level competence, which means an electrician on your books without a 2391 series qualification may lose scope for EICR work at that point. This is a scheme requirement rather than a statutory one, so confirm your specific position with your own scheme operator before you commit to a contract that runs past that date. Course fees and the assessment cost are allowable as training that maintains or updates an existing skill.</p>

<h2>Is EICR work inside CIS? The inspection and remedial deduction split</h2>

<p>This is the part most electricians get wrong on the invoice. HMRC's guidance on commissioning work (CISR14120) treats work that comprises only inspecting an installation, adjusting controls and reporting to the client as being in the nature of a survey, which falls within the exclusion for the professional work of consultants in building and engineering. On that basis a pure inspection and testing engagement, which is exactly what an EICR is, sits <strong>outside</strong> the Construction Industry Scheme.</p>

<p>Remedial work that follows the report is different. Replacing a consumer unit, upgrading bonding, rewiring a circuit or fitting RCD protection is a construction operation, and where you are paid by a contractor the CIS deduction applies at <strong>20%</strong> if you are registered or <strong>30%</strong> if you are not, on the labour element only. Materials are excluded from the deduction base. The rates and the labour-only rule are covered in full in our guide to <a href="/blog/cis-basics/cis-deduction-rates-explained">CIS deduction rates</a>, and if you are new to the scheme, start with <a href="/blog/cis-basics/what-is-cis">what the Construction Industry Scheme is</a>.</p>

<p>Two practical consequences follow. First, invoice the inspection fee, the remedial labour and the materials as three separate lines, so the contractor applies the deduction only where it belongs. Our guide to <a href="/blog/cis-basics/cis-invoice-splitting-labour-materials">splitting labour and materials on CIS invoices</a> covers the mechanics, and the <a href="/calculators/cis-invoice-splitter">CIS invoice splitter</a> will work the deduction out on a mixed invoice for you.</p>

<p>Second, most EICR work is instructed directly by a landlord, a letting agent or a managing agent rather than by a contractor, so no deduction arises at all. Note the exception: an agent, housing association or property business spending more than £3 million on construction operations in a rolling twelve month period becomes a deemed contractor and must operate the scheme on the construction operations it pays for, though the inspection element still sits outside. For the landlord-facing side of this market, including what to quote a letting agent and how the certification paperwork is presented to the client, see our guide to <a href="/blog/cis-compliance/landlord-electrical-safety-checks-pricing">landlord electrical safety checks and what to charge</a>.</p>

<h2>The recurring-revenue case for EICRs, and the VAT threshold to watch</h2>

<p>Almost no other work in the electrical trade comes with a legally mandated repeat date attached. Every satisfactory domestic rented EICR you issue in 2026 is a diarised job in 2031, for a client who is legally required to have it done. Building that diary is worth more than any individual job margin, and the remedial work the reports generate typically carries a better rate than the inspection itself.</p>

<p>Two financial consequences follow from scaling it. The first is VAT. Taxable turnover of <strong>£90,000</strong> on a rolling twelve month basis triggers compulsory VAT registration, and EICR fees invoiced gross reach that threshold faster than CIS labour of the same value feels like it should, because nothing is being deducted before the money lands. Once registered, remedial work for VAT-registered and CIS-registered contractors falls under the domestic reverse charge, covered in our guide to the <a href="/blog/vat-and-mtd/vat-reverse-charge-construction">VAT reverse charge in construction</a>. Inspection fees billed to an end user landlord follow normal VAT rules.</p>

<p>The second is Making Tax Digital for Income Tax. Sole traders with gross income above £50,000 are in scope from April 2026, dropping to £30,000 from April 2027, and gross income means turnover before any CIS deduction. A growing EICR book counts toward that threshold in full. The practical implications for a construction sole trader are set out in our guide to <a href="/blog/vat-and-mtd/mtd-income-tax-cis">MTD for Income Tax and CIS</a>.</p>

<p>Whether to keep trading as a sole trader or incorporate is a separate question, and the answer changes once a meaningful share of turnover arrives gross rather than net of deductions. Our comparison of <a href="/blog/cis-basics/cis-sole-trader-vs-limited-company">CIS as a sole trader against a limited company</a> works through both positions.</p>

<p>EICRs are the most reliably repeatable job an electrical subcontractor can sell, with statutory five-year cycles across every UK nation's rented sector and a concentrated block of social housing inspections still to complete before 1 November 2026. Price them from your own recovery per hour rather than the local going rate, keep your coding defensible, get the 2391 series qualification in place before the 1 October 2026 scheme deadline, and split inspection income from remedial labour on every invoice so the CIS deduction lands only where it belongs.
