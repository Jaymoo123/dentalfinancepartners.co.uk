- question: Is a bookkeeper cheaper than an accountant for a CIS subcontractor?
  answer: "Per hour, usually yes. In scope, they are not doing the same job. A bookkeeper records and categorises transactions, reconciles the bank, and keeps your ledgers current. An accountant prepares and files the Self Assessment return, calculates the tax position, prepares the CIS refund claim, and advises on structure and Gross Payment Status. Comparing a bookkeeper's monthly fee against an accountant's monthly fee is comparing two different deliverables. Many subcontractors use both, or use software for the recording and an accountant for the annual work."
- question: Do bookkeepers handle CIS deductions?
  answer: "Some do and some do not, which is worth establishing before you engage one. A bookkeeper working regularly with construction clients will record the labour and materials split on each invoice, log the CIS deducted, and match your contractor payment and deduction statements to the money received. A general small business bookkeeper with no construction clients may simply record the net amount that hit your bank, which understates your turnover and loses the deduction record your refund claim depends on. Ask directly how many CIS clients they act for."
- question: What if my bookkeeper only records the net payment I received?
  answer: "This is the most common and most expensive error in trade bookkeeping. If a contractor pays you £880 on a £1,000 invoice with £200 labour deducted at 20% on a £600 labour element, and your books show £880 of income, your turnover is understated and the £120 of CIS deducted is invisible. At year end the refund claim is then built from bank data rather than from your deduction statements. The correction is to record gross invoice value, the labour and materials split, and the CIS deducted as a separate item, then reconcile to the net receipt."

<h2>What a bookkeeper costs a self-employed subcontractor in 2026/27</h2>

<p>Trade-reported bookkeeping rates for a one-person construction business fall broadly between <strong>£15 and £45 per hour</strong> for a freelance bookkeeper, and roughly <strong>£25 to £60 per hour</strong> where the work sits inside an accountancy practice. On a fixed monthly package, most sole trader subcontractors with modest transaction volumes land somewhere between <strong>£40 and £150 per month</strong>.</p>

<p>Those figures are market observations rather than published prices. Bookkeeping is not a regulated service with a fee scale, so every quote is built from the specific job. What follows sets out what actually drives the number for a CIS subcontractor, where a bookkeeper stops and an accountant starts, the construction-specific records that make trade bookkeeping different from ordinary small business bookkeeping, and the point at which software does the same work for a fraction of the price.</p>

<h2>What drives the price for a CIS subcontractor</h2>

<p>Bookkeeping is priced on transaction volume and complexity, not on turnover. A subcontractor invoicing £70,000 to two main contractors with 30 purchase receipts a month is a cheaper job than one invoicing £45,000 across ten small jobs with materials bought daily from three merchants.</p>

<p>The variables that move the fee most:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Number of transactions.</strong> Bank lines and receipts are the unit of work. Everything else follows from this.</li>
<li><strong>Whether you are VAT registered.</strong> Quarterly returns plus the domestic reverse charge decision on every invoice adds real time.</li>
<li><strong>Whether you pay anyone.</strong> Payroll, or paying your own subcontractors and filing CIS300 monthly returns, is a separate service and priced separately.</li>
<li><strong>How the records arrive.</strong> Receipts photographed weekly cost less to process than a year of paper delivered in January. Catch-up work is routinely quoted at a premium.</li>
<li><strong>Whether the bookkeeper knows CIS.</strong> Construction experience costs more per hour and normally saves money overall, for the reasons in the next section.</li>
</ul>

<h2>The CIS records that make trade bookkeeping different</h2>

<p>Ordinary small business bookkeeping records money in and money out. Construction bookkeeping has three extra obligations that a generalist will often miss, and each one has a direct cost if it goes wrong.</p>

<p><strong>The labour and materials split on every invoice.</strong> CIS deductions apply to the labour element of a payment only. Materials are excluded from the deduction base. A £1,000 invoice made up of £600 labour and £400 materials carries a 20% deduction of £120, not £200. If your books do not carry that split, you cannot check whether a contractor deducted correctly, and overdeductions go unnoticed.</p>

<p><strong>Gross recording, not net.</strong> A contractor pays you the net figure after deduction. If the books record only what hit the bank, your turnover is understated by the whole of the CIS deducted and the deduction itself disappears from the record. The correct treatment is to record the gross invoice, the deduction as a separate item, and reconcile down to the net receipt.</p>

<p><strong>Payment and deduction statements matched to payments.</strong> Contractors must give you a statement for each tax month in which they deducted. Those statements are the evidence base for the refund. Filing them as they arrive, and matching each one to the payment it relates to, is a monthly bookkeeping task, not a January archaeology exercise. Our <a href="/blog/cis-compliance/cis-record-keeping-guide">CIS record-keeping guide</a> sets out the full document set and the retention periods.</p>

<p>Alongside those, the ordinary expense discipline still applies: a contemporaneous mileage log, now at <strong>55p per mile for the first 10,000 business miles</strong> from 6 April 2026 and 25p thereafter, plus receipts for tools, PPE and materials. Our guide to <a href="/blog/expenses/allowable-expenses-cis-subcontractor">allowable expenses for CIS subcontractors</a> covers what qualifies.</p>

<h2>When software beats paying a subcontractor bookkeeper</h2>

<p>For a large share of sole trader subcontractors, CIS-aware accounting software at roughly £10 to £30 per month does what a bookkeeper would be doing by hand: bank feeds, receipt capture from a phone, mileage tracking, and invoices that split labour and materials and calculate the deduction for you.</p>

<p>Software is usually the better value option when all of the following hold:</p>

<ul>
<li>You invoice a small number of regular contractors rather than many one-off clients</li>
<li>You are not VAT registered, or your VAT position is simple</li>
<li>You do not pay your own subcontractors</li>
<li>You are willing to spend 30 minutes a week on it rather than letting it pile up</li>
</ul>

<p>Paying someone starts to win when the volume climbs, when the domestic reverse charge starts applying to your invoices, when you take on CIS300 monthly filing for your own subcontractors, or simply when the hours you spend on records exceed what you could bill in the same time. A subcontractor on a £250 day rate spending five hours a month on paperwork is spending roughly £150 of billable capacity to avoid a fee that may be lower than that.</p>

<p>What software does not do is the annual tax work. It produces the records; it does not prepare the Self Assessment return, calculate the position, or build and file the refund claim. That remains a separate job whichever way you handle the day to day.</p>

<h2>How MTD for Income Tax changes the maths</h2>

<p>From <strong>6 April 2026</strong>, sole traders and landlords with qualifying income over <strong>£50,000</strong> must keep digital records and send quarterly updates under Making Tax Digital for Income Tax. The threshold drops to <strong>£30,000 from 6 April 2027</strong> and to <strong>£20,000 from 6 April 2028</strong>, which brings almost every full-time subcontractor into scope within the next two years.</p>

<p>Two points matter for the bookkeeping decision. First, qualifying income for a CIS subcontractor is gross turnover before deductions, not the net figure that reaches your bank. A subcontractor banking £48,000 after 20% deductions on £60,000 gross is tested on the £60,000 and is in scope. Second, MTD converts bookkeeping from an annual catch-up into a quarterly rhythm, which removes the option of doing nothing until January. Full detail is in our guide to <a href="/blog/vat-and-mtd/mtd-income-tax-cis">MTD for Income Tax and CIS</a>.</p>

<p>Practically, that means the choice is no longer between paying a bookkeeper and doing nothing. It is between paying a bookkeeper and maintaining compliant digital records yourself with software. The cost gap narrows once the software subscription is a requirement rather than an option.</p>
