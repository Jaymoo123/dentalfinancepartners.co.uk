- question: How long do you have to keep VAT records?
  answer: "At least 6 years. HMRC's guidance in VAT Notice 700/21 states that, generally, you must keep all your business records for VAT purposes for at least 6 years. Some records need to be kept longer where they are also relevant to another tax, and specific schemes carry their own periods, but 6 years is the working rule for a VAT-registered construction business. The period runs from the date of the record, not from the date of the VAT return it fed into."
- question: How long must a CIS subcontractor keep Self Assessment records?
  answer: "Five years after the 31 January submission deadline for the relevant tax year. For 2026/27, the filing deadline is 31 January 2028, so records must be kept until at least 31 January 2033. There is an exception for returns filed very late (more than four years after the deadline), where records must be kept for 15 months after the return is actually submitted. Because CIS-specific records carry their own shorter statutory floor and the VAT rule runs to 6 years, keeping everything for 6 years from the end of the tax year is the simplest safe standard."

  answer: "Yes, if you are VAT-registered. The VAT account is the summary that connects your day-to-day records to the figures on your VAT return. HMRC does not prescribe a format, but it must show output tax due on sales, input tax you are entitled to reclaim, any amounts accounted for under the reverse charge procedure, and any corrections or adjustments. In practice, accounting software produces this automatically and most subcontractors never look at it. It becomes important the moment HMRC opens a check, because it is the document that explains how you got from the invoices to the boxes."
- question: Do I need to keep paper receipts or will photographs do?
  answer: "Digital copies are acceptable provided the image is complete and legible, and you can produce it on request. Scanned invoices, receipt-capture apps and cloud accounting attachments all satisfy the requirement, and the original paper does not have to be retained once a clear copy exists. If you are inside Making Tax Digital for VAT, the underlying transaction data has to be in software in any case, so the receipt image is a supporting document rather than the record itself."
- question: What extra records does the VAT domestic reverse charge create?
  answer: "Three things. First, the invoice itself must carry the reverse charge legend and show the amount of VAT the customer has to account for, even though you are not charging it. Second, where a customer claims end user or intermediary supplier status, you need their written confirmation on file, because that confirmation is what justifies you having charged VAT normally. Third, the reverse charge amounts have to appear correctly in your VAT account, since they sit in different boxes for the supplier and the customer."

  answer: "Contractors must issue a payment and deduction statement by the 19th of the month following the payment. If statements are missing, request them in writing from the contractor and keep the request. Where the contractor has stopped trading or will not respond, the fallback is to reconstruct the position from your invoices, your bank statements showing the net amounts received, and HMRC's own record of deductions reported against your UTR. <h2>What VAT records a construction business must keep</h2>

<p>Once your taxable turnover passes <strong>£90,000</strong> in any rolling 12 month period you must register for VAT, and from that point HMRC expects a specific and fairly long list of records. VAT Notice 700/21 sets it out: annual accounts and profit and loss statements, bank statements and paying-in slips, cash books and account books, credit and debit notes, orders and delivery notes, purchase and sales books, purchase invoices and copies of every sales invoice you issue, records of daily takings where relevant, and general business correspondence.</p>

<p>For a construction trade the list is less intimidating than it reads. Practically it means: every invoice you raise, every supplier invoice and builders merchant receipt you pay, your bank feed, and the correspondence that explains anything unusual. The additions that catch trades out are the copies of your own sales invoices (people keep what they receive and lose what they send) and the correspondence file (the email in which a contractor confirmed end user status is a VAT record).</p>

<p>Alongside all of that sits the VAT account, covered below, and any evidence supporting a claim for input tax. HMRC's position on input tax is that a valid VAT invoice is the primary evidence. Alternative evidence can be accepted, but it is discretionary, and a merchant receipt that does not show the supplier's VAT number is a weak substitute for the one that does.</p>

<h2>How long you must keep VAT and CIS records</h2>

<p>Two clocks run at once, and they do not tick at the same speed. On the VAT side, HMRC's guidance states that generally you must keep all your business records for VAT purposes for at least <strong>6 years</strong>. On the Self Assessment side, a self-employed subcontractor must keep business records for <strong>5 years after the 31 January submission deadline</strong> of the relevant tax year. That second rule is the one people misquote as "five years", when the practical effect is closer to six years from the end of the tax year.</p>

<p>Because the longest of these is 6 years and none of them is shorter in a way that matters, the simplest policy for a construction business is a single 6 year rule applied to everything, with an override for anything under enquiry. <h2>The VAT account: the record most subcontractors do not know they need</h2>

<p>The VAT account is a statutory requirement, not an optional summary. It is the bridge between the underlying records and the nine boxes on the VAT return, and HMRC deliberately does not prescribe a format so long as it contains the right information.</p>

<p>It must separate, for each VAT period:</p>

<ul>
<li>Output tax due on your sales</li>
<li>Output tax due under the <strong>reverse charge procedure</strong>, which for construction means the domestic reverse charge amounts you account for as a customer</li>
<li>Input tax you are entitled to reclaim on business purchases</li>
<li>Corrections and error adjustments, in both directions</li>
<li>Any other adjustments required by the VAT rules, such as partial exemption or scheme-specific entries</li>
</ul>

<p>If you run cloud accounting software, the VAT account exists whether you look at it or not. The reason it matters is what happens in a compliance check: the inspector works backwards from the return figures, and the VAT account is the document that shows how those figures were built. Where the account has been reconstructed after the fact rather than maintained, the reconstruction is usually where the errors surface.</p>

<h2>Digital records and digital links under MTD for VAT</h2>

<p>All VAT-registered businesses are now inside Making Tax Digital for VAT, regardless of turnover. That means records must be kept digitally in functional compatible software, and data must move between programs by digital link.</p>

<p>The digitally required records fall into four groups: designatory data (business name, principal place of business, VAT registration number, and any VAT schemes used); the per-supply records; summary totals by VAT rate; and adjustments. The per-supply detail is the part that determines how you set up your invoicing.</p>

<p>A digital link is a transfer or exchange of data between software programs that happens without manual intervention. An API connection is a digital link. Linked cells between spreadsheets are a digital link. Emailing a spreadsheet for import is a digital link. Reading a total off one screen and typing it into another is not, and neither is copy and paste. This is the single most common MTD for VAT failure in small construction businesses, usually where a merchant statement or a subcontractor payment summary is being keyed in by hand at quarter end.</p>

<p>Making Tax Digital for Income Tax is a separate regime with a separate test, and it arrives for many subcontractors from April 2026 at <strong>£50,000</strong> of gross income, dropping to <strong>£30,000</strong> from April 2027. The trap for CIS workers is that gross means turnover before deductions. Our guide to <a href="/blog/vat-and-mtd/mtd-income-tax-cis">MTD for Income Tax and CIS</a> works through the threshold arithmetic.</p>

<h2>Reverse charge paperwork: the VAT records the DRC creates</h2>

<p>The domestic reverse charge has applied to specified construction services since <strong>1 March 2021</strong>, and it generates paperwork that would not otherwise exist. Three documents matter for record-keeping purposes.</p>

<p>The <strong>invoice</strong> itself. A reverse charge invoice shows the net value with no VAT charged, states the rate that would have applied, shows the amount of VAT the customer must account for, and carries the reverse charge legend. Keep your copy, as with any sales invoice.</p>

<p>The <strong>end user or intermediary confirmation</strong>. Where a customer tells you they are an end user (a property owner, a tenant, a developer building for their own portfolio) you charge VAT normally, and that written confirmation is what justifies the decision if HMRC later asks why the reverse charge was not applied. An email is sufficient. What is not sufficient is a verbal assurance that nobody wrote down. File the confirmation with the customer record, not with the individual invoice, and refresh it if the relationship changes.</p>

<p>The <strong>VAT account entries</strong>. Reverse charge amounts sit differently for the two parties: the supplier excludes the VAT from output tax but includes the net sale in Box 6, and the customer accounts for the output tax and, where fully recoverable, reclaims the same amount as input tax. Getting the entries wrong is a return error even when the net VAT position is nil. The mechanics for each side are covered in our guide to the <a href="/blog/vat-and-mtd/vat-reverse-charge-construction">VAT domestic reverse charge in construction</a>.</p>

<h2>CIS statements: the records that produce your refund</h2>

<p>On the CIS side, one document does most of the work. Every contractor who deducts CIS from your payments must issue a payment and deduction statement by the <strong>19th of the month following</strong> the payment. It shows the gross payment, the materials element excluded from the deduction base, the rate applied (0% for gross payment status, 20% registered, 30% unregistered), the deduction taken and the net paid.</p>

<p>Those statements are the evidence for the CIS credit claimed on your return. A subcontractor with a full set can reconcile deductions to the pound. A subcontractor with nine months out of twelve is claiming a number they cannot fully support, which is exactly the position that turns a routine <a href="/blog/cis-refunds/cis-tax-refund-how-to-claim">CIS refund claim</a> into a six month exchange of letters.</p>

<p>The practical discipline is to check statements monthly rather than annually. A missing statement chased in the same month it arose is a two minute conversation with a contractor you are still working for. Chased eighteen months later, it is a request to a firm that may have changed its payroll provider, its bookkeeper or its trading status. Alongside the statements, keep the expense evidence that determines the size of the refund: receipts, and a contemporaneous mileage log, since the AMAP rate is <strong>55p per mile for the first 10,000 business miles</strong> from 6 April 2026 and 25p thereafter. The categories are set out in our guide to <a href="/blog/expenses/allowable-expenses-cis-subcontractor">allowable expenses for CIS subcontractors</a>.</p>

<h2>Penalties for VAT and CIS record failures</h2>

<p>The direct penalties are modest. Failure to keep or preserve VAT records carries a penalty of up to <strong>£500</strong> under section 69 of the Value Added Tax Act 1994. Failure to keep adequate Self Assessment records carries up to <strong>£3,000 per tax year</strong> under section 12B of the Taxes Management Act 1970. Neither figure is the reason to take records seriously.</p>

<p>The real cost is assessment by best judgement. Where records are missing, HMRC estimates the tax due on the information available, and you lose the ability to argue against the estimate with anything better than assertion. For a VAT-registered subcontractor, that can mean output tax assessed on gross bankings with input tax allowed only where an invoice survives. For a sole trader, it can mean expenses disallowed wholesale because no receipt supports them, turning an expected refund into a liability.</p>

<p>The second-order cost is the assessment window. HMRC can generally assess four years back, extending to six years where tax has been lost through carelessness and twenty years for deliberate conduct. Records that would have closed a query in a fortnight, once destroyed, leave several years exposed to estimation.</p>

<h2>A working record system for a VAT-registered construction business</h2>

<p>Nothing above requires an elaborate system. What it requires is that four things happen consistently.</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Capture at the point of spend.</strong> Photograph the merchant receipt in the van, not at the year end. Receipt-capture in accounting software attaches the image to the transaction, which is what makes it findable three years later.</li>
<li><strong>Invoice from the software, not from a template.</strong> A sales invoice raised inside the accounting system creates the digital record, applies the reverse charge treatment, and stores your copy in one action. A Word template creates one document and no record.</li>
<li><strong>Reconcile monthly.</strong> Match the bank feed, check that every contractor who paid you that month has issued a deduction statement, and file any end user confirmations received. Twenty minutes a month removes almost all of the year-end archaeology.</li>
<li><strong>Archive on a 6 year rule.</strong> One retention period for everything, with a flag on anything under enquiry or supporting an unfiled claim. Cloud storage makes the marginal cost of an extra year effectively nil, so the safe answer is usually to keep it.</li>
</ul>

<p>The point of all of this is not compliance for its own sake. Records are what turn a refund entitlement into a refund received, and what turn a VAT check into a short exchange rather than a long one.</p>

<p>VAT records go back 6 years, Self Assessment records go 5 years past the 31 January deadline, and both clocks are extended by anything still in dispute. The domestic reverse charge and the MTD digital link rules each add requirements that a paper-and-shoebox system cannot meet, and CIS deduction statements remain the single document your refund depends on.
