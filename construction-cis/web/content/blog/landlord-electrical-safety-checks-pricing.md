faqs:
- question: How often does a landlord need an electrical safety check?
  answer: At least every 5 years. The Electrical Safety Standards in the Private Rented Sector (England) Regulations 2020 require the landlord to ensure that every electrical installation in the property is inspected and tested by a qualified person at least every 5 years, and more frequently if the report itself specifies a shorter interval. The requirement applied to new tenancies from 1 July 2020 and to all existing tenancies from 1 April 2021. If the report you issue specifies a 3-year interval because of the condition of the installation, that shorter interval becomes the landlord's legal obligation, not a suggestion.
- question: What is a landlord electrical safety certificate?
  answer: There is no document in the regulations actually called a landlord electrical safety certificate. The legal requirement is for a report of the inspection and test, which in practice is an Electrical Installation Condition Report (EICR) produced to the model form in BS 7671, the 18th Edition Wiring Regulations. The report must give the results of the inspection and the date of the next inspection due. When a landlord or letting agent asks for a landlord safety certificate, an EICR marked satisfactory is what they mean, and it is worth saying so on your quote so nobody is expecting a different document.
- question: How much should I charge for a landlord EICR?
  answer: Published 2026 UK cost guides put a typical domestic EICR in the region of £100 to £300, with a standard 3-bedroom house commonly quoted around £150 to £250 and London and the South East running roughly 15% to 30% above the national figure. These are consumer-facing directory ranges rather than a survey of trade invoices, so treat them as a market signal, not a rate card. Price the actual job on circuit count, the age and accessibility of the installation, and your travel time, then check the resulting hourly recovery against your own target day rate before you commit to a portfolio price.
- question: Who is a qualified person for a landlord electrical safety check?
  answer: The regulations define a qualified person as someone competent to undertake the inspection and testing, and to take any further investigative or remedial work in accordance with BS 7671. They do not name a particular certification scheme. Government guidance points landlords towards the Registered Competent Person Electrical register and Electrical Safety First when checking competence, which is why membership of a scheme such as NICEIC or NAPIT is effectively the commercial entry ticket even though it is not, strictly, the legal test. Carry your scheme registration number on your reports and quotes.
- question: What happens if the report comes back unsatisfactory?
  answer: An unsatisfactory outcome (any C1, C2 or FI coding) triggers a 28-day duty on the landlord to carry out the further investigative or remedial work, or a shorter period if your report specifies one. The landlord then needs written confirmation from the qualified person who carried out that work, and must supply it to the tenant and the local housing authority within 28 days of the work being completed. C1 codings mean danger is present and should be made safe before you leave site.
- question: If a letting agent instructs me and pays me, do CIS deductions apply?
  answer: It depends on what the agent is and what you are being paid for. CIS applies where a contractor pays a subcontractor for construction operations. A letting agent acting purely as the landlord's agent, paying out of client money on a small maintenance job, is usually not a CIS contractor. A property maintenance company that subcontracts the electrical work to you, or a business that has spent over £3 million on construction in the previous 12 months and has become a deemed contractor, may well be. Ask for their CIS position in writing before the first invoice rather than after the first deduction.
- question: A landlord has three flats and wants one price for all of them. How should I quote?
  answer: Quote each unit separately on the invoice even if you agree a bundled total, because each property has its own report, its own five-year clock and potentially its own remedial work. Price the second and third units below the first to reflect the travel and set-up you are only doing once, and put the renewal dates in a diary as soon as you issue the reports. A three-property landlord is a five-year, three-report annuity plus whatever remedial work falls out of it, so the volume discount is usually worth giving.
- question: Can I claim my test equipment and scheme fees as expenses?
  answer: Yes. A multifunction tester, its annual calibration, your scheme registration and assessment fees, insurance, certification software subscriptions and the mileage to and from site are all allowable business expenses. Larger equipment purchases go through capital allowances, and the Annual Investment Allowance normally allows the full cost in the year of purchase. Mileage at the AMAP flat rate is 55p per mile for the first 10,000 business miles from 6 April 2026 and 25p thereafter, so a tester who covers a wide area of rented stock has a substantial mileage claim.
- question: Does the domestic reverse charge apply to landlord electrical work?
  answer: Usually not. The VAT domestic reverse charge applies between VAT-registered and CIS-registered parties where the customer is not an end user. A private landlord having their own property inspected is consuming the service rather than selling it on, so they are an end user and normal VAT rules apply. Where you are subcontracting to a building contractor or a maintenance firm that is itself supplying the landlord, the reverse charge can apply and you should get the end user statement in writing.
- question: What if the tenant will not let me in to do the test?
  answer: "The landlord is not treated as having breached the duty where they can show they took all reasonable steps to comply, and the 2025 amendment regulations clarified that a refusal of access by the tenant does not by itself put the landlord in breach. That defence depends on evidence, so document your side of it: log every appointment offered, every no-access visit and every attempt to contact the tenant, and put it in writing to the landlord or agent the same day. It protects your client and it protects your invoice for the abortive visit."

<h2>What the regulations require, and why it matters for CIS trades</h2>

<p>Every private rented home in England has required a valid electrical inspection report since 1 April 2021, when the Electrical Safety Standards in the Private Rented Sector (England) Regulations 2020 finished phasing in across existing tenancies. The maximum financial penalty a local housing authority can impose on a landlord in breach was raised from £30,000 to £40,000 by the 2025 amendment regulations, which also extended the regime into the social rented sector from 1 November 2025. For an electrician, that is not a compliance story. It is a legally mandated, calendar-driven job that repeats every five years across a client's entire portfolio.</p>

<p>This guide sets out the duties the landlord is buying help with, what the market is paying in 2026, how to build a landlord and letting agent client base, and how CIS deductions, VAT and expenses apply once the work starts flowing. If you want the product-level detail on the report itself, its validity period and how the coding works, see our companion guide to <a href="/blog/cis-basics/eicr-certificates-pricing-guide">EICR certificates and how to price the job</a>. This page is about the landlord market specifically.</p>

<p>The duties sit on the landlord, not on you. But the landlord almost never knows what they are, and the electrician who can recite them accurately on a first phone call converts far better than the one quoting a price alone. The table below is the whole regime in one place.</p>

<p>The standard being tested against is BS 7671, the 18th Edition Wiring Regulations. The regulations do not name a certification scheme, and they do not use the phrase "landlord electrical safety certificate" at all. What they require is a report, and in practice that is an EICR on the model BS 7671 form.</p>

<h2>The 28-day remedial rule is where a subcontractor's margin lives</h2>

<p>The inspection itself is a low-margin, high-competition product. The remedial work is not. When a report comes back unsatisfactory because of a C1 (danger present), a C2 (potentially dangerous) or an FI (further investigation required), the landlord has 28 days to get it put right and then needs written confirmation from the qualified person who carried out the work.</p>

<p>That written confirmation requirement is the commercial point. The landlord is not shopping around for a second electrician to do the repairs when the clock is running and the paperwork has to tie back to a competent person. If you are on site, have the fault list, and can quote the remedial work before you leave, the job is usually yours.</p>

<p>Three habits turn that into money rather than goodwill:</p>

<ul>
<li>Quote the remedials on site, in writing, the same day. A verbal "that consumer unit needs doing" is not a quote and does not create urgency.</li>
<li>Separate the report fee and the remedial work on the invoice. It keeps the pricing honest, it makes the labour and materials split visible for CIS purposes, and it stops a landlord treating the whole thing as one inflated inspection bill.</li>
<li>Make a C1 safe before you leave and bill the making-safe separately. It is a distinct piece of work and it is not part of an inspection fee.</li>
</ul>

<p>The trap to avoid is coding defensively to manufacture remedial work. Scheme assessors and landlord bodies both watch for it, and one contested report can cost you an agent relationship worth several years of steady jobs.</p>

<h2>What to charge, and the expenses that shape the price</h2>

<p>Published 2026 UK cost guides put a domestic inspection somewhere between £100 and £300, with a standard three-bedroom house typically quoted at £150 to £250 and London and the South East running roughly 15% to 30% higher. Those are consumer-facing directory figures rather than a survey of trade invoices, so use them as a market signal and price the actual job from the circuit count, the age of the installation and the access.</p>

A typical domestic inspection is commonly quoted at two to four hours on site, which is the number that matters: an inspection at £180 that runs to four hours plus an hour of report writing and an hour of travel is not a £180 job, it is roughly £30 an hour before any cost at all.</p>

<p>The costs that quietly eat this work are travel between scattered properties, abortive visits where nobody answers the door, and the report write-up time nobody quotes for. Build all three into the price. Your multifunction tester, its annual calibration, scheme registration and assessment fees, insurance and certification software are all allowable, and larger equipment purchases normally qualify in full in the year of purchase under the Annual Investment Allowance. <h2>Building a landlord client base as a CIS electrician</h2>

<p>One-off inspections found through a directory are the lowest-value version of this work. The volume sits with the people who hold multiple properties: letting agents, block managers, portfolio landlords and, since November 2025, housing associations and councils working through the social rented sector extension.</p>

<p>What those buyers want is not the cheapest price. It is a tester who turns up, issues the report the same week, uses consistent coding, and hands them a document they can forward to a tenant without editing. Practical ways in:</p>

<ul>
<li>Approach letting agents with the compliance calendar, not a price list. An agent managing 200 properties has 40 reports falling due a year and usually no system for spotting them.</li>
<li>Offer to audit an agent's existing reports for expiry dates. It is an hour of work and it hands you a diarised pipeline.</li>
<li>Put the next-inspection date in your own diary the moment you issue a report, and contact the landlord 90 days before it expires. Five-year renewals are worthless if you have to be reminded by the client.</li>
<li>Take gas and EPC renewal dates too where the landlord will share them. You are not doing that work, but knowing the compliance calendar makes you the person they call.</li>
<li>Agree an abortive visit fee in writing before the first job. No-access visits are the single biggest margin leak in rented stock.</li>
</ul>

<p>On access: the 2025 amendment regulations confirmed that a landlord who took all reasonable steps is not in breach simply because a tenant refused entry. That defence runs on evidence, so document every appointment offered and every failed visit and send it to the client the same day. It protects them and it justifies your fee.</p>

<h2>Do landlord inspection jobs carry a CIS deduction?</h2>

<p>This is where electricians get caught out, and the honest answer is that it depends on who is paying you and for what.</p>

<p>CIS applies where a contractor pays a subcontractor for construction operations. A private landlord instructing you directly is normally not a contractor at all, so no deduction arises and you invoice in full. A letting agent paying out of client money on the landlord's behalf is usually in the same position. But a property maintenance company that subcontracts the work to you is a contractor, and any business that has spent over <strong>£3 million</strong> on construction operations in the previous 12 months can become a deemed contractor, which catches larger letting and property management groups.</p>

<p>The second question is whether the work itself is a construction operation. Remedial and installation electrical work plainly is. Pure inspection, testing and report writing is less clear cut, and HMRC's list of excluded operations does not settle it either way. The safe operating position is to assume the remedial element is within CIS, split labour and materials on every invoice, and ask the payer for their CIS status in writing before the first job rather than after the first deduction lands. Remember that deductions apply to the <strong>labour element only</strong>: materials are excluded from the deduction base, so a £900 remedial job with £500 labour and £400 materials carries a 20% deduction of £100, not £180.</p>

For the wider picture of which jobs fall inside the scheme, start with <a href="/blog/cis-basics/what-is-cis">what the Construction Industry Scheme actually covers</a>.</p>

<h2>VAT: landlords are usually end users</h2>

<p>The VAT domestic reverse charge applies to specified construction services between parties who are both VAT-registered and CIS-registered, where the customer is not an end user. A private landlord having their own rented property inspected and repaired is consuming the service rather than selling it on, so they are an end user and you charge VAT in the normal way.</p>

<p>Where you are subcontracting to a building contractor or a property maintenance firm that is itself supplying the landlord, the reverse charge can apply and the VAT treatment flips. Get the end user statement in writing and keep it on file. Our guide to the <a href="/blog/vat-and-mtd/vat-reverse-charge-construction">VAT reverse charge in construction</a> covers the end user exception and the 5% de minimis rule in full.</p>

<p>One further point of timing. If your gross turnover crossed £50,000 for the 2024/25 tax year you have been inside Making Tax Digital for Income Tax since 6 April 2026, and the threshold drops to £30,000 for 2025/26 income (from April 2027) and £20,000 for 2026/27 income (from April 2028). A steady book of landlord compliance work is exactly the kind of income that pushes a sole-trader electrician over those lines, and gross means turnover before any CIS deduction, not what lands in the bank.</p>

<h2>Turning a legal duty into a limited company revenue line</h2>

<p>The strength of this work is that it is not discretionary and it is not weather-dependent. A landlord cannot decide to skip it, and every satisfactory report you issue creates a dated obligation to come back. Once the volume justifies it, the structure question follows: a growing testing book with retained agent relationships often reaches the point where operating through a limited company, and reclaiming CIS deductions in real time through the Employer Payment Summary rather than waiting for a Self Assessment refund, changes the cash flow materially. Our comparison of <a href="/blog/cis-basics/cis-sole-trader-vs-limited-company">CIS as a sole trader versus a limited company</a> sets out where that line usually sits.</p>

<p>Landlord electrical safety work rewards the electrician who treats it as a compliance service rather than a job. Know the duties better than the landlord does, quote the remedials before you leave site, diary every five-year renewal, and get the CIS and VAT position agreed in writing before the first invoice. The reports are the entry point; the portfolio relationship is the business.</p>
