faqs:
- question: How long does it take to change a consumer unit?
  answer: A straight like-for-like swap in a sound, modern installation with accessible tails and existing main protective bonding is commonly 4 to 6 hours including testing and certification paperwork. A board change on an older property where bonding has to be run, circuits need to be identified and labelled from scratch, or faults are found on test, routinely runs to a full day and sometimes a second visit. Quote-aggregator guidance for 2026 describes the job as around one day, but that figure assumes nothing is found on test, which in pre-1990s housing stock is optimistic.
- question: How much should an electrician charge to replace a consumer unit?
  answer: Consumer-facing quote data for 2026 puts the total job at roughly £350 to £500 for a six-circuit board in a small property, £480 to £640 for a ten-circuit board, and £610 to £850 for twelve circuits or more, including materials. The labour element within those totals is commonly quoted at £220 to £350. Those are homeowner-facing market figures rather than a rate card, and they exclude any remedial work. Your own price should be built from your day rate, the actual board and device cost, your certification and notification cost, and a contingency for what testing uncovers.
- question: Is replacing a consumer unit notifiable under Part P?
  answer: 'Yes. In England and Wales, replacement of a consumer unit is notifiable work under Part P of the Building Regulations. There are three compliance routes: carry out the work as a member of a registered competent person scheme and self-certify, use a registered third-party certifier, or notify building control before work starts and pay their fee. Scotland operates a separate building warrant system rather than Part P, so check the local position before quoting Scottish work.'
- question: Do CIS deductions apply to the consumer unit and the protective devices?
  answer: No. CIS deductions apply to the labour element of your payment only. Materials are excluded from the deduction base, and the board, the RCBOs, the SPD, the bonding cable, glands and consumables are all materials. If you invoice a main contractor with a single total and no split, the contractor may apply the 20% deduction to the whole figure, which means the tax taken on your materials becomes an advance you have to reclaim through Self Assessment or, for a limited company, through the Employer Payment Summary.
- question: What if I find no main protective bonding once I open up the job?
  answer: This is the most common mid-job discovery on older properties and it is why a fixed price quoted from a doorstep conversation is risky. Main protective bonding to the incoming gas and water services, sized under Regulation 544.1.1, is a prerequisite for a compliant board change, and running it can mean lifting floors, chasing walls or a long external run. Price the inspection first, issue a written variation with its own labour and materials figures before continuing, and record the variation as a separate line on the invoice so the CIS split stays clean.
- question: Should I do an EICR before quoting a board change?
  answer: For anything other than a recent installation with existing certification, yes. A periodic inspection tells you the condition of the circuits you are about to reconnect and protects you from inheriting somebody else's defects at your own cost. Charging for the inspection, then offsetting some or all of it against the board change if the customer proceeds, is a common and defensible commercial structure. It also gives you the documented basis for any remedial work you recommend.
- question: Does the VAT reverse charge apply to a consumer unit replacement?
  answer: It depends who is paying you. If you are working for a VAT-registered and CIS-registered main contractor who will sell the work on, the domestic reverse charge applies and you invoice without charging VAT, stating that the customer accounts for it. If you are working directly for a homeowner, a landlord using the property, or any other end user, normal VAT rules apply and you charge VAT if you are registered. The end-user test, not the type of work, is what decides it.
- question: Do I need an AFDD in a domestic consumer unit?
  answer: Under BS 7671, arc fault detection devices are required on single-phase AC final circuits supplying socket-outlets rated up to 32 A in higher risk residential buildings, houses in multiple occupation, purpose-built student accommodation and care homes. In an ordinary dwelling they are recommended rather than required, and the designer is expected to consider and record the decision. Because AFDD or AFDD-RCBO devices carry a materially higher unit cost than standard RCBOs, present them as a priced option on the quote rather than absorbing them into a headline figure.
- question: How do I record a board change for Self Assessment and MTD?
  answer: Record the sale, the material purchases and the certification or notification fee against the same job reference so the labour and materials split is evidenced if HMRC queries it. Keep the CIS payment and deduction statement from any contractor who deducted from you. From April 2026 sole traders and partnerships with gross income above £50,000 are within Making Tax Digital for Income Tax and must keep digital records and file quarterly updates, so job-level categorisation in software is now the practical minimum rather than a nice-to-have.
- question: Can I claim the cost of my test equipment against tax?
  answer: Yes. A multifunction tester, a socket tester, an earth loop impedance tester and calibration costs are all business expenditure. The instruments themselves are plant and machinery, normally deductible in full in the year of purchase under the Annual Investment Allowance, and annual calibration is a revenue expense. Mileage to and from site is claimable at the AMAP rate of 55p per mile for the first 10,000 business miles and 25p per mile after that, from 6 April 2026.

<h2>What the market pays, and what the CIS subcontractor keeps</h2>

<p>Quote-aggregator data for 2026 puts a UK consumer unit replacement at roughly <strong>£350 to £850 including materials and labour</strong>, with a headline average around £525 and labour alone commonly quoted at <strong>£220 to £350</strong>. Those are homeowner-facing figures gathered from job postings, not a trade rate card, and they describe a job with nothing wrong behind it.</p>

<p>The number that decides whether the job was worth doing is what lands in your account afterwards. On a domestic job paid direct by a householder that is the full invoice less your material cost. On a board change carried out for a main contractor it is the invoice less materials less the <strong>20% CIS deduction on the labour element</strong>, which you recover later. This guide covers realistic time on site by scenario, the specification decisions that move the price under the current wiring regulations, the Part P notification route, and how to invoice so the deduction only ever touches labour.</p>

<h2>Time on site by scenario: what a subcontractor should actually price</h2>

<p>The search question "how long does it take to change a consumer unit" has one honest answer for the customer and a more granular one for the person quoting it. The variable is almost never the board itself. It is what the existing installation forces you to do before you can energise the new one.</p>

<table>
<thead>
<tr><th>Scenario</th><th>Realistic time on site</th><th>What drives it</th></tr>
</thead>
<tbody>
<tr><td>Like-for-like swap, modern installation, existing certification</td><td>4 to 6 hours</td><td>Isolation, board fit, reconnection, full test schedule, certificate</td></tr>
<tr><td>Upgrade with main protective bonding to be run</td><td>Full day, occasionally two visits</td><td>Bonding runs to gas and water, access, making good</td></tr>
<tr><td>Older property, circuits unidentified, no records</td><td>Full day plus</td><td>Circuit identification, labelling, dead testing before reconnection</td></tr>
<tr><td>Board change plus remedials found on test</td><td>Second visit almost certain</td><td>Borrowed neutrals, low insulation resistance, undersized CPCs</td></tr>
<tr><td>Rented property with certification deadline</td><td>Day plus paperwork turnaround</td><td>Inspection, remedials, certificate issue to the landlord's timetable</td></tr>
</tbody>
</table>

<p>Two commercial points follow. First, the DNO cut-out seal is not yours to break, so any job needing the supply pulled has a third-party dependency in it and should never be sold as a fixed morning slot. Second, a genuine pre-works inspection converts every one of the rows above from a risk you carry to a variation the customer approves. Price the inspection, then price the board change from what the inspection found.</p>

<h2>Specification decisions that move the price and the CIS deduction base</h2>

<p>The current standard is <strong>BS 7671:2018+A4:2026</strong>, published in 2026. The previous version, BS 7671:2018+A2:2022+A3:2024, remains valid until <strong>15 October 2026</strong>, so during the transition period installers may work to either edition. Verify the position with your scheme provider before you commit a specification in writing, because a quote issued now may well be installed after the transition closes.</p>

<p>Three specification points carry most of the cost variance on a domestic board change:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>RCBO versus dual-RCD boards.</strong> A fully populated high-integrity or RCBO board costs meaningfully more in devices than a dual-RCD board, and quote data for 2026 puts supply-only cost for a ten-way RCBO unit well above the equivalent dual-RCD unit. The benefit is discrimination: one faulty circuit no longer takes half the house out. Present it as a priced option, not a default you absorb.</li>
<li><strong>Surge protection.</strong> Regulation 443.4 requires a risk assessment to determine whether protection against transient overvoltages is needed, and the older calculation method was removed by Amendment 2. Whichever way the assessment goes, it needs recording. An SPD adds device cost and, in a full board, sometimes a way.</li>
<li><strong>Arc fault detection devices.</strong> Under Regulation 421.1.7, AFDDs are required on single-phase AC final circuits supplying socket-outlets rated up to 32 A in higher risk residential buildings, HMOs, purpose-built student accommodation and care homes, and are recommended elsewhere with the decision documented. AFDD or combined AFDD-RCBO devices are a step change in unit cost, so an HMO board change is a different job commercially from the identical work in a family home.</li>
</ul>

<p>All of that device cost is <strong>materials</strong>, and materials are excluded from the CIS deduction base. Our guide to <a href="/blog/cis-basics/cis-deduction-rates-explained">CIS deduction rates and how they are applied</a> sets out the mechanics in full.</p>

<h2>Part P notification: the route, the cost and the allowable expenses</h2>

<p>In England and Wales, replacing a consumer unit is <strong>notifiable work under Part P of the Building Regulations</strong>. It is one of the small number of jobs, alongside new circuits and work in special locations, that cannot simply be certified and forgotten. There are three compliance routes:</p>

<ul>
<li>Self-certification as a member of a registered competent person scheme, such as NICEIC, NAPIT, ELECSA or STROMA, who then notify building control on your behalf</li>
<li>Certification by a registered third-party certifier</li>
<li>Notification to the local authority building control body, normally before work starts, with their fee attached</li>
</ul>

<p>Scotland runs a building warrant system rather than Part P, so confirm the position locally before quoting Scottish work. Whichever route applies, the customer should receive an Electrical Installation Certificate with the full schedule of test results, and, on the notified routes, a building regulations compliance certificate.</p>

<p>Your scheme membership, annual assessment fee, instrument calibration and per-notification charges are all business costs. They belong on the quote as recoverable overhead and on your tax return as <a href="/blog/expenses/allowable-expenses-cis-subcontractor">allowable expenses for a CIS subcontractor</a>, alongside mileage at the 2026/27 AMAP rate of 55p per mile for the first 10,000 business miles.</p>

<h2>Invoicing the job: splitting labour and materials for the CIS deduction</h2>

<p>Where a board change is carried out for a main contractor rather than direct for a householder, CIS applies. The deduction is <strong>20% for a registered subcontractor, 30% if unregistered, and 0% with gross payment status</strong>, and it is applied to the labour element only.</p>

<p>Consider a twelve-way RCBO board change on a contractor-run refurbishment, invoiced at £980 excluding VAT:</p>

<p>Invoice the same job as a single line of £980 and a contractor applying 20% across the total deducts £196, not £124. The extra £72 is not lost, but it sits with HMRC until you reclaim it, and on a run of board changes across a refurbishment contract that working capital gap becomes real. The detail is in our guide to <a href="/blog/cis-basics/cis-invoice-splitting-labour-materials">splitting labour and materials on CIS invoices</a>. To model the effect on a specific invoice, run the figures through the <a href="/calculators/cis-invoice-splitter">CIS invoice splitter</a>.</p>

<p>One further point for contractor work: if both parties are VAT and CIS registered and the customer is not the end user, the <a href="/blog/vat-and-mtd/vat-reverse-charge-construction">VAT domestic reverse charge</a> applies and you invoice without charging VAT. A householder or a landlord occupying or letting the property is an end user, so direct domestic work stays on normal VAT rules.</p>

<h2>Adjacent work a subcontractor can sell without overreaching</h2>

<p>A board change is the natural moment to raise the condition of everything downstream of it, which is exactly why it attracts the wrong kind of upselling. The defensible version is sequential rather than opportunistic.</p>

<p>Inspect first. A periodic inspection carried out and charged before the board change tells you what you are reconnecting, gives the customer a documented reason for any remedial work, and stops you absorbing pre-existing defects inside a fixed price. Our guide to <a href="/blog/cis-basics/eicr-certificates-pricing-guide">pricing EICR work</a> covers how to structure and charge that inspection.</p>

<p>Then quote the board change, the remedials and the options as separate priced lines. Bonding upgrades, SPD, AFDD where recommended rather than required, and additional ways for a future EV charger or solar diverter each stand on their own. Customers accept priced options far more readily than a single large number, and separate lines also keep the labour and materials split clean for CIS.</p>

<h2>Records that support the CIS split, Self Assessment and MTD</h2>

<p>Every board change should leave four documents behind: the certificate and test schedule, the notification confirmation, the purchase invoices for the board and devices, and your sales invoice with the labour and materials split shown. Those four are what evidence the deduction base if HMRC queries an invoice, and what make the year-end reconciliation arithmetic rather than archaeology.</p>

<p>From April 2026, sole traders and partnerships with gross income above £50,000 fall within <a href="/blog/vat-and-mtd/mtd-income-tax-cis">Making Tax Digital for Income Tax</a>. Gross income for that test means turnover before CIS deductions, not the net figure that reaches your bank, which catches a good number of subcontractors by surprise. Job-level record-keeping in compatible software is now the practical baseline.</p>

<p>Consumer unit replacement is a job where the price is set by what the existing installation forces you to do, not by the board on the van, so the inspection has to come before the fixed quote. Specify to the current standard, notify through the right Part P route, and split labour from materials on every invoice so the CIS deduction only ever touches the labour.
