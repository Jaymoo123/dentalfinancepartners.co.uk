- question: "Has the Making Tax Digital deadline been extended again in 2026?"
  answer: "No. The most recent deferral was announced on 19 December 2022 and moved mandatory MTD for Income Tax from April 2024 to April 2026. That date arrived and the £50,000 phase is live. The reason the question keeps coming back is that the programme slipped repeatedly between 2015 and 2022, so a lot of contractors reasonably expect another slip. HMRC guidance now carries all three phases with fixed dates and there is no announced review that would move them. The last change to the timetable, at Spring Statement 2025, added a phase rather than delaying one."
- question: "What are the current MTD for Income Tax dates and thresholds?"
  answer: "Qualifying income over £50,000 was mandated from 6 April 2026. Over £30,000 joins from 6 April 2027. Over £20,000 joins from 6 April 2028. Qualifying income is gross self-employment income plus gross property income, added together and measured before expenses. If your qualifying income is £20,000 or less you sit outside the announced scope, and no further phase below that level has been published."
- question: "Which tax year does HMRC use to decide whether I am in scope?"
  answer: "The Self Assessment return for the tax year two years before the phase begins. Mandation from April 2026 was tested on the 2024/25 return. Mandation from April 2027 is tested on the 2025/26 return, which is filed by 31 January 2027. Mandation from April 2028 is tested on the 2026/27 return. That lag is the part contractors miss: the figure that pulls you in was reported long before the obligation starts, and you cannot change it by trading differently now."
- question: "Are partnerships and LLPs in scope for MTD for Income Tax?"
  answer: "Not yet. HMRC guidance states that partnerships will need to use MTD for Income Tax in future and that the timeline will be set out at a later date. That is the one genuine open-ended deferral left in the programme. A contractor trading through a partnership or an LLP continues with the existing partnership return until HMRC publishes a date. Each partner should still check their own position, because personal sole-trade or rental income they hold outside the partnership is tested against the thresholds in their own name."
- question: "I run a PSC and let out one flat. Am I caught by MTD?"
  answer: "It depends only on the rent, because the company income is ignored. Salary and dividends are not qualifying income, so a director drawing £12,570 of salary and £60,000 of dividends has nil qualifying income from the company. If the flat produces £22,000 of gross rent, that figure alone is tested. It is under £30,000, so no obligation in April 2027, but it is over £20,000, so the April 2028 phase catches it. The test is on gross rent before mortgage interest, letting agent fees and repairs, which is why property owners are pulled in earlier than their profit suggests."
- question: "My side consultancy income has dropped. Does that get me out of MTD?"
  answer: "Not for the first year, and usually not quickly. Suppose your 2025/26 return showed £36,000 of gross sole-trade fees but you are only invoicing around £18,000 by 2027/28. You are still mandated from 6 April 2027 on the 2025/26 figure, because that is the year HMRC tested. Falling below a threshold does not release you automatically, and HMRC's exit route requires a sustained run of years below the level rather than a single quiet year. Assume you are in from your mandation date and treat any later release as a bonus."
- question: "What happened to the End of Period Statement?"
  answer: "It was removed as a separate submission. At Autumn Statement on 22 November 2023 the government announced that the EOPS requirement would be dropped, with its function folded into the final declaration. So the cycle is four quarterly updates through compatible software, then one final declaration after the tax year that confirms the figures, adds reliefs and other income, and finalises the tax. This was a genuine simplification, and it is worth knowing because a lot of older MTD explainers still describe a five-submission year that no longer exists."
- question: "Does MTD change my payment dates or my tax bill?"
  answer: "No. Quarterly updates are information submissions, not tax demands. The balancing payment and first payment on account remain due by 31 January after the tax year, with the second payment on account due by 31 July. MTD changes how figures reach HMRC, not the rates, allowances or amounts. A contractor in MTD budgets for exactly the same January and July dates as before, with the same payments on account."
- question: "Does MTD for Income Tax affect my IR35 position or my company?"
  answer: "No on both counts. IR35 and the off-payroll working rules decide whether an engagement is taxed like employment; MTD decides how a sole trader or landlord keeps records and reports. Company profits are reported on a Corporation Tax return, which is a separate regime and outside MTD for Income Tax. A contractor inside IR35 through a PSC still has no MTD obligation on salary and dividends. The only overlap is personal side income, which is tested in the normal way regardless of your IR35 status."
- question: "What should I do this tax year if I think a later phase will catch me?"
  answer: "Work out your qualifying income from the return HMRC will actually test, get your side-income bookkeeping into MTD-compatible software before the mandation date rather than after it, and decide now whether you or your accountant will file the quarterly updates. If you are close to a threshold, check whether any of the income you assumed was qualifying actually is, because misclassifying dividends or salary as qualifying income is the most common error. Do not build the plan around another delay."

<p>Making Tax Digital for Income Tax was first proposed in December 2015 with a start date of 2018. It actually began on <strong>6 April 2026</strong>, an eight-year gap produced by four separate announcements. That history, not any current policy, is why "MTD deadline extended" is still typed into search engines every month.</p>

<p>It has not been extended again. The last deferral was announced on <strong>19 December 2022</strong>, and every change since has either added a phase or simplified the mechanics rather than pushing dates back. This guide sets out which deferrals genuinely happened, what the timetable looks like now, the one piece that is still open-ended, and what a limited company contractor with side income should be doing about it this year.</p>

<h2>The deferral history behind the contractor rumour mill</h2>

<p>Understanding why contractors expect another delay makes it easier to see why one is unlikely. The programme slipped four times, and each slip was announced formally rather than drifting quietly.</p>

<table>
<thead>
<tr><th>Announcement</th><th>What it did</th><th>Start date after the change</th></tr>
</thead>
<tbody>
<tr><td>December 2015 roadmap</td><td>Proposed digital records and quarterly updates across Income Tax, VAT and Corporation Tax</td><td>From 2018</td></tr>
<tr><td>July 2017 ministerial statement</td><td>Pulled Income Tax out of the first wave, leaving VAT to go first</td><td>Income Tax not before April 2020</td></tr>
<tr><td>July 2020 announcement</td><td>Set a firm Income Tax start date for sole traders and landlords</td><td>April 2023</td></tr>
<tr><td>September 2021</td><td>Deferred by a further year, citing pandemic pressures</td><td>April 2024</td></tr>
<tr><td>19 December 2022</td><td>Deferred again and introduced phasing by income level</td><td>April 2026 for over £50,000</td></tr>
<tr><td>Spring Statement 2025</td><td>Added a third phase rather than delaying the existing ones</td><td>April 2028 for over £20,000</td></tr>
</tbody>
</table>

<p>Two things stand out. First, the deferrals stopped in December 2022, and the phasing introduced then is exactly what has since been delivered. Second, the most recent change went the other way: Spring Statement 2025 pulled <strong>more</strong> people into scope from April 2028 rather than granting anyone more time. A contractor reading the pattern as "it always gets delayed" is reading a period that ended more than three years ago.</p>

<h2>What is fixed now, and what is still genuinely deferred for contractors</h2>

<p>The live timetable has three phases, each tested against a specific past tax year:</p>

<table>
<thead>
<tr><th>Mandated from</th><th>Qualifying income over</th><th>Return HMRC tests</th><th>Filing deadline for that return</th></tr>
</thead>
<tbody>
<tr><td>6 April 2026</td><td>£50,000</td><td>2024/25</td><td>31 January 2026 (passed)</td></tr>
<tr><td>6 April 2027</td><td>£30,000</td><td>2025/26</td><td>31 January 2027</td></tr>
<tr><td>6 April 2028</td><td>£20,000</td><td>2026/27</td><td>31 January 2028</td></tr>
</tbody>
</table>

<p>Against that, one part of the programme really is still deferred with no date attached. HMRC's guidance says partnerships will need to use MTD for Income Tax in future and that the timeline will be set out later. A partnership start date of April 2025 was floated back in 2021 and then dropped. So a contractor trading through a general partnership or an <strong>LLP</strong> continues filing the existing partnership return, indefinitely, until HMRC publishes something. That is the single genuine "extension" still running, and it is far narrower than the rumour suggests.</p>

<p>The other simplification worth knowing is that the <strong>End of Period Statement was scrapped</strong> at Autumn Statement on 22 November 2023, with its function folded into the final declaration. The year is now four quarterly updates plus one final declaration, not five submissions plus a return. Older explainers that still describe an EOPS are out of date.</p>

<h2>The two-years-back test is what actually catches contractors</h2>

<p>The mechanic that surprises people is not the threshold, it is the lag. HMRC decides whether you are mandated by looking at the Self Assessment return for the tax year <strong>two years before</strong> the phase starts. Mandation from April 2027 turns on your 2025/26 figures, a return most contractors will not even file until January 2027.</p>

<p>Three consequences follow, and they all cut against the wait-and-see instinct:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>The deciding number is already history.</strong> If your 2025/26 gross side income was over £30,000, the April 2027 obligation is settled. Trading differently in 2026/27 changes nothing.</li>
<li><strong>A drop in income does not release you.</strong> Falling below the threshold in later years does not switch the obligation off automatically, and HMRC's exit route needs a sustained run of years below the level, not one quiet year.</li>
<li><strong>The test is gross, not profit.</strong> Qualifying income is gross sole-trade fees plus gross rents, added together before any expenses. A property with £26,000 of rent and a large mortgage may produce almost no taxable profit and still count as £26,000.</li>
</ul>

<p>Take a worked case. A PSC contractor draws a £12,570 salary and £65,000 of dividends, and separately runs a small training side business in their own name that billed £21,000 gross in 2025/26 and lets a flat producing £11,000 of gross rent. The company income is irrelevant. The qualifying income is £21,000 plus £11,000, which is £32,000, over the £30,000 line. That contractor is mandated from <strong>6 April 2027</strong>, despite believing throughout that MTD was a sole trader problem and nothing to do with them.</p>

<h2>What did not change for a PSC contractor</h2>

<p>The headline exclusion is unchanged and is worth restating, because it is the reason most readers of this site can stop worrying about the timetable entirely. A contractor operating through a <strong>personal service company</strong> and drawing salary and dividends generally has nil qualifying income, and company profits sit under Corporation Tax rather than Self Assessment. Our guide to <a href="/blog/mtd-and-compliance/mtd-income-tax-contractors-guide">MTD for Income Tax for contractors</a> works through the scope test in full, and the <a href="/blog/limited-company-tax/psc-limited-company-contractor-tax">PSC and limited company contractor tax</a> guide covers how the company layer is actually taxed.</p>

<p>Also unchanged:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Payment dates.</strong> The balancing payment and first payment on account are still due by 31 January, the second by 31 July. Quarterly updates are information, not invoices. The <a href="/blog/mtd-and-compliance/contractor-self-assessment-guide">contractor Self Assessment guide</a> sets out how those dates work.</li>
<li><strong>The amount of tax.</strong> MTD alters reporting, not rates, allowances or the personal allowance.</li>
<li><strong>Your IR35 position.</strong> MTD and the off-payroll working rules answer different questions and do not interact. Nothing in the MTD timetable changes how status is determined on an engagement, which our guide to <a href="/blog/ir35-status/what-is-ir35">what IR35 is</a> covers.</li>
</ul>

<h2>What a contractor should actually do this tax year</h2>

<p>If you have any income outside your company, four steps are worth taking in 2026/27 rather than later.</p>

<ol>
<li><strong>Calculate your qualifying income from the right year.</strong> For the April 2027 phase, add gross sole-trade fees and gross rents for <strong>2025/26</strong>, before expenses. That is the number that decides it. Do not include salary, dividends, savings interest or employment income, none of which count.</li>
<li><strong>Classify each income source once, properly.</strong> The most common error is treating dividends or a director's salary as qualifying income and panicking, or treating a genuine sole-trade sideline as company income and relaxing. Write the sources down and label each one.</li>
<li><strong>Get the side income into compatible software before the mandation date.</strong> A trade or property business that has always been reconstructed each January needs a running digital record. Starting the habit a year early costs little; starting it in the first mandated quarter is where people pick up penalty points.</li>
<li><strong>Decide who files.</strong> Four quarterly updates plus a final declaration is five deadlines a year rather than one. Agree now whether you or your accountant will submit them, and diarise the quarter ends.</li>
</ol>

<h2>Why planning around another delay is the expensive contractor mistake</h2>

<p>The wait-and-see strategy has an asymmetric payoff, and the asymmetry runs the wrong way. If you prepare and a delay arrives, you have lost nothing beyond having tidy records a year early, which is useful in itself. If you do not prepare and no delay arrives, you enter a mandated year with paper records, no software, no digital link and a quarterly deadline roughly a month after the first quarter end.</p>

<p>The evidence also points one way. April 2026 landed on schedule, the 2025 change added scope rather than removing it, and HMRC has published thresholds, tested years and guidance for all three phases. Betting against that on the strength of deferrals that stopped in 2022 is not a forecast, it is a habit.</p>
