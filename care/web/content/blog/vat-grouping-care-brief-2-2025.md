<p>Revenue and Customs Brief 2/2025, published on 24 April 2025, did not make VAT grouping unlawful in the care sector. It named one structure. If your group holds that structure, HMRC has said it will turn down new registrations where it judges refusal necessary, and that investigations into existing arrangements have already started. If your group holds an ordinary VAT group of commonly controlled companies, nothing in the brief touches it. The decision in front of most care groups is therefore not whether to react to the brief, it is which of those two things they actually have. That answer sets which of the two removal routes in section 43C applies, and the two do not carry the same effective date, so it is worth settling before the next return rather than in correspondence.</p>

<h2>What does the brief actually attack?</h2>
<p>One arrangement, and only that one. A regulated provider's welfare supplies are exempt, so its input VAT is lost; the structure answers that by placing an unregulated entity in the chain so the care is billed as taxable, with the group registration then taking the internal supply out of the picture and leaving the input tax recoverable. HMRC's position is that this is avoidance and that it will use its powers in full against it. The mechanism step by step, and the partial exemption arithmetic that made it attractive, are set out in our guide to <a href="/blog/vat-and-welfare-exemption/care-home-vat-exemption-edge-cases">the welfare exemption edge cases</a>. What matters here is the part the brief leaves to the legislation: what HMRC can do to a group, and from what date.</p>
<p>What the brief does not do is disturb the grouping rules. Section 43A of the Value Added Tax Act 1994 lets two or more UK bodies corporate group where one controls the others, or one person controls all of them, or two or more individuals carrying on a business in partnership control all of them. Since 1 November 2019 individuals and partnerships can be members in their own right. A family that owns three homes through three companies satisfies that test on the day it is written down, and grouping them changes the paperwork, not the exemption.</p>

<h2>Is ordinary grouping still worth having in a care group?</h2>
<p>Yes, and for reasons that have nothing to do with the brief. Section 43 treats the business of every member as carried on by the representative member, and any supply between members is disregarded. For a care group running a central services company that recharges management, payroll and maintenance to the operating homes, that disregard is the whole point. Outside a group, those recharges are standard-rated supplies to exempt operators and the VAT on them sticks at the receiving end. Inside a group, they leave the VAT system altogether, and one return replaces several. None of that requires an unregulated entity to be presented as making taxable care supplies to residents or commissioners.</p>
<p>The price is joint and several liability. Every member is liable for the VAT due from the representative member, which is the clause that turns a structural problem into a group-wide one rather than a subsidiary's problem.</p>

<h2>Which removal notice would you get?</h2>
<p>This is the part that separates the two positions in money, and it is the reason the structure question deserves a precise answer. Section 43C gives HMRC two routes out of a group and they do not carry the same date. The protection of the revenue notice sits in subsections (1) and (2); the ineligibility termination and its earlier effective date sit in subsections (3) and (4).</p>

<p>A structure built on an entity that comfortably satisfies the control test faces the first route. A structure that also stretched the control test, which several of the marketed arrangements did in order to keep the unregulated entity commercially at arm's length while still inside the group, is exposed to the second. The second is materially worse, because the correction reaches backwards rather than forwards.</p>

<h2>What does a provider inside one of these arrangements decide now?</h2>
<p>Three decisions, in this order.</p>
<ul>
  <li><strong>Quantify before you correspond.</strong> The figure that matters is the input tax recovered through the arrangement, period by period, since it started. Until that figure exists, nobody can say whether the answer is to unwind, to disclose, or to defend.</li>
  <li><strong>Test eligibility on its own terms.</strong> Work out whether every member met the section 43A control test throughout, separately from the avoidance question. That is what decides which removal route applies and therefore how far back the correction runs.</li>
  <li><strong>Separate the forward decision from the backward one.</strong> Leaving the group settles the treatment from here. Providers sometimes exit and treat the matter as closed. It is not closed, and an assessment arriving against a group that has already distributed its profits is a cash problem as well as a tax one.</li>
</ul>
<p>HMRC's brief asks providers to review their VAT accounting independently and have the position professionally reviewed, and offers a route in at CAGetHelpOutOfTaxAvoidance@hmrc.gov.uk using VAT grouping as the subject. Arriving there with the figures already established is a different conversation from arriving without them. A specialist reviews the structure and the returns together, because the two have to tell one story.</p>

<h2>What about a group that has never used one of these structures?</h2>
<p>Then the brief is a reason to document, not to restructure. If your group already recharges central costs between commonly controlled companies, the file should show the control test being met, the commercial purpose of the recharges, and the fact that no entity outside the regulated provider is being treated as making the care supplies. That file is short, and it is what answers a request for further information in one round instead of three.</p>
