<p>There are six money decisions behind a new domiciliary care agency, and the order they are made in matters more than any one of them. Your trading structure comes first because it is named on the CQC application. Opening capital and the financial viability statement come second because registration must be granted before you can lawfully raise a fee. Then the VAT position, then the pay model at £12.71 an hour from 1 April 2026, then your charge rate, then the reporting regime you will live with. Each decision narrows the one after it, which is why taking them out of order is expensive.</p>

<h2>Decision one: which trading structure do you register?</h2>

<p>Settle sole trader, partnership or limited company before you apply, because the applicant named on the CQC registration is the legal entity that will carry on the regulated activity. Moving into a company afterwards is a new registration, not an amendment.</p>

<p>The tax split is what makes the choice more than cosmetic. A company is taxed on its profits at 19% up to £50,000, and you are taxed only on what you draw out; a sole trader or partner is taxed on the whole profit as it arises, drawn or not. The bands above that, the dividend rates and the marginal relief between them are set out in <a href="/blog/business-structure-and-acquisition/care-structure-before-cqc">choosing your structure before you apply to CQC</a>.</p>

<p>The commercial side often decides it before the tax does. Local authority framework bids, insurers and lenders read a limited company balance sheet more readily than sole trader accounts, and limited liability matters in a sector where a single safeguarding claim can outlast the contract that caused it.</p>

<h2>Decision two: how much opening capital, and can you evidence it?</h2>

<p>Enough to carry the whole period before registration is granted, because trading in a regulated activity before that point is a criminal offence. Rent, your registered manager's salary, recruitment, insurance and systems all start while the revenue line is still zero, so the <a href="/blog/cqc-and-financial-compliance/cqc-registration-timeline-cash-burn-before-trading">cash you burn before you can trade</a> comes out of opening capital in full.</p>

<p>Then you have to evidence it. The application carries a financial viability statement on CQC's own template, and that is where the opening capital, the monthly burn and the contingency are tested. One that cannot show survival through the registration window stalls the application, which is why the <a href="/blog/cqc-and-financial-compliance/cqc-financial-viability-statement-walkthrough">walkthrough of the statement</a> is worth reading before you start filling it in.</p>

<p>Build the model monthly from lease signature to first fee received, add a contingency for a request for further information, and treat the registration duration as a variable input rather than a fixed date. A specialist reviews the assumptions before the pack is submitted.</p>

<h2>Decision three: what is your VAT position, and what does exemption cost you?</h2>

<p>Almost no domiciliary agency charges VAT on its fees, because once CQC has registered you your welfare services sit inside the exemption at Group 7 of Schedule 9 to the Value Added Tax Act 1994. So the live question is not whether to charge VAT. It is what the exemption costs you, because it behaves as a cost rather than a perk.</p>

<p>What it costs is the input tax. Nothing bought to deliver exempt care carries recoverable VAT, so the tax on premises, vehicles, software, equipment and professional fees stays in the cost base permanently, and every line of your model has to be built gross. The second consequence runs the other way: exempt fees are not taxable turnover, so they never push you over the £90,000 registration threshold however large the agency grows.</p>

<p>If you plan any taxable activity alongside the care, selling training to other providers for example, track those supplies separately from day one. Mixed providers run partial exemption calculations, and recovery is usually lost because exempt input tax fails the de minimis test, whose first limb is £625 a month.</p>

<h2>Decision four: how are carers engaged and paid?</h2>

<p>As employees, unless the arrangement genuinely fails every employment test, which in a rota-based domiciliary model it will not. Status is decided on substance: whether a carer can send a substitute, who chooses when and where each call happens, and who directs the work. HMRC's compliance attention in this sector lands on that point more than any other, and when an arrangement is unpicked the bill is retrospective rather than prospective.</p>

<p>Then price the hour honestly. The wage floor is £12.71 for workers aged 21 and over from 1 April 2026 and £10.85 for those aged 18 to 20, but the floor is not the cost. Employer National Insurance adds 15% above the £5,000 secondary threshold, offset for most new agencies by the Employment Allowance. Travel between one client and the next is working time for the minimum wage and has to be paid, mileage is reimbursed at the approved rate, and irregular-hours staff accrue holiday at 12.07% of the hours they work. The <a href="/services/start-a-domiciliary-care-agency">rules on travel time and sleep-in pay</a> are where the underpayments usually start.</p>

<h2>Decision five: what do you charge, and how is the rate built?</h2>

<p>Build the charge rate from a fully loaded cost per care hour, then add margin, rather than matching whatever local agencies advertise. The loaded cost includes the wage, employer National Insurance, holiday accrual, paid travel time and mileage, pensions, the non-chargeable hours your coordinators and registered manager work, and irrecoverable VAT. Our <a href="/calculators/true-cost-care-hour-calculator">true cost of a care hour calculator</a> adds them up.</p>

<p>The rate also depends on who pays. Local authority framework rates are negotiated against the fair cost of care framework in the Care Act statutory guidance, and a rate below your loaded cost does not become sustainable through volume. Model the funding mix you expect, not the one you would prefer.</p>

<h2>Decision six: which reporting regime will you be in?</h2>

<p>The last decision is really a consequence of the first. A company files annual accounts and a corporation tax return. A sole trader or partner falls into Making Tax Digital for Income Tax where combined self-employment and property income exceeds £50,000 from 6 April 2026, dropping to £30,000 from 6 April 2027 and £20,000 from 6 April 2028, which means quarterly updates from compatible software. Either way, payroll runs under real time information from your first employee.</p>

<p>Choose the bookkeeping system before the first month, and set it up so that fee income is analysed by funder from the start. Local authority, NHS and private income behave differently on cash collection and on contract risk, and a ledger that separates them is what makes the next framework bid and the next set of accounts straightforward, and your accountant prepares the year end from it.</p>

<p>England is the default position throughout. Decisions two and three change outside it, because each nation runs its own registration body and its own financial evidence, so check the regulator for the nation you will operate in before you build the model. Decisions one, four, five and six do not, because tax, VAT and the minimum wage are United Kingdom wide.</p>
