<p>Government-backed lending schemes have been part of the UK business-finance picture since the pandemic, and the current one is the <strong>Growth Guarantee Scheme</strong>, run by the British Business Bank. It is the direct successor to the Recovery Loan Scheme, which in turn followed the Coronavirus Business Interruption Loan Scheme (CBILS) and the Bounce Back Loan Scheme. The name has changed several times, but the core idea has not: the government gives the lender a partial guarantee, which makes the lender more willing to lend to smaller companies. The scheme sits within the wider set of government-backed options listed under <a href="https://www.gov.uk/business-finance-support">gov.uk business finance support</a>.</p>

<p>The scheme is widely misunderstood, and the misunderstanding is expensive. Many directors assume a government-backed loan is somehow underwritten for them, that if things go wrong the government picks up the tab. It does not work that way. This guide explains what the guarantee actually protects, who qualifies, what the scheme can fund, and how a Growth Guarantee facility compares with a standard commercial loan, so your company goes in with the right expectations.</p>

<h2>What the Growth Guarantee Scheme Is</h2>

<p>The Growth Guarantee Scheme is a UK government programme, administered by the <a href="https://www.british-business-bank.co.uk/growth-guarantee-scheme">British Business Bank</a>, that supports lending to smaller businesses. The British Business Bank does not lend money itself and does not take applications from companies. Instead, it accredits commercial lenders (banks, challenger banks, and specialist finance houses) and gives each of them a guarantee against a portion of the losses on the facilities they write under the scheme.</p>

<p>That guarantee currently covers <strong>70% of the outstanding balance</strong> the lender cannot recover if a borrower defaults. The lender carries the remaining 30% of the risk itself, which is deliberate: it keeps the lender's own money on the line so it still underwrites properly, rather than lending recklessly on the back of a state backstop.</p>

<p>The point of the scheme is not to hand out cheap money. It is to nudge lenders into saying yes to viable smaller companies they might otherwise decline, for example a business with a short trading history, a thinner asset base, or a sector the lender treats cautiously. The guarantee reduces the lender's downside, so the lending case gets over the line.</p>

<h2>How the Guarantee Actually Works (and What It Does Not Do)</h2>

<p>This is the section to read twice, because the guarantee protects the lender, not the borrower. Here is the reality for your company:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Your company is the borrower and repays in full.</strong> You draw the facility, you pay interest, you repay the capital, on exactly the same commercial terms as a non-scheme loan.</li>
<li><strong>The guarantee never reduces your debt.</strong> It does not cancel a balance, it does not act as insurance for you, and it does not mean the government steps in to pay your instalments if trading gets tough.</li>
<li><strong>The guarantee only bites on default and recovery failure.</strong> If your company defaults and the lender cannot recover the debt (including calling any security or guarantee), the government reimburses the lender for 70% of its remaining loss. That is a transaction between the lender and the government, and your company is still pursued for what it owes.</li>
<li><strong>A personal guarantee may still apply.</strong> The scheme does not remove personal guarantees. As a rule they are not taken on facilities up to £250,000, and above that they are at the lender's discretion. Your main home cannot be taken as security under the scheme, but the director exposure on a personal guarantee is otherwise real.</li>
</ul>

<p>So a "government-backed loan" is a normal loan your company must repay, wrapped in a guarantee that gives the lender comfort. That comfort can be genuinely valuable, it can turn a no into a yes, but it is not a softer or safer deal for the borrower.</p>

<h2>Is Your Company Eligible?</h2>

<p>Eligibility is set within the scheme rules and applied by each accredited lender, so the final call sits with the lender. The broad tests a smaller company needs to meet are:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>UK-based and trading.</strong> Your company must be carrying on business in the UK and generating the bulk of its income from trading activity, not investment.</li>
<li><strong>Turnover under £45 million.</strong> The scheme is for smaller businesses, with an annual group turnover ceiling of £45 million.</li>
<li><strong>A viable borrowing proposition.</strong> The lender must believe your company can afford the repayments. The guarantee is there to cover risk, not to prop up borrowing the lender thinks cannot be serviced.</li>
<li><strong>Not in one of the excluded categories.</strong> Certain sectors, and businesses in formal difficulty, are outside the scheme. Subsidy-control limits also apply, because the guarantee is a form of government subsidy.</li>
</ul>

<p>Meeting the headline criteria does not entitle your company to a facility. It makes your company eligible to be considered by an accredited lender, which then underwrites the deal on its own credit policy. A company that is a comfortable lend on ordinary terms may not even need the scheme, while a marginal case is exactly where the guarantee earns its keep.</p>

<h2>What the Scheme Can Fund</h2>

<p>One of the strengths of the Growth Guarantee Scheme is that it is not limited to term loans. Accredited lenders can apply the guarantee across several facility types, which means your company can use it for the borrowing that actually fits the problem:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Term loans</strong> for a defined, one-off cost, repaid over a fixed period.</li>
<li><strong>Overdrafts and revolving facilities</strong> for fluctuating or seasonal working-capital gaps. See our <a href="/blog/business-finance/small-business-loans">small business loans guide</a> for how these sit alongside term borrowing.</li>
<li><strong>Invoice finance</strong> for releasing cash tied up in your unpaid B2B invoices.</li>
<li><strong>Asset finance</strong> for buying plant, machinery, or vehicles, spreading the cost of the asset.</li>
</ul>

<p>Terms vary by product. Term loans and asset finance generally run up to six years, while overdrafts and invoice finance typically run up to three years. Match the term to the life of what you are funding, so your company is not still repaying long after the benefit has gone.</p>

<p>Picture a limited company that turns over £1.2 million and wins a large new contract, but needs £150,000 up front for stock and extra staff before the contract starts paying. Its bank is cautious because the company is only three years old, so it offers a Growth Guarantee Scheme term loan over five years.</p>

<tr><th>Element</th><th>What it means for the company</th></tr>
<tr><td>Loan amount</td><td>£150,000, drawn in full at the start.</td></tr>
<tr><td>Government guarantee</td><td>70% to the <strong>lender</strong>. The company sees no benefit on its own balance.</td></tr>

<p>The headline: a scheme-backed loan is not automatically cheaper. Its value is access. If your company would be approved comfortably on ordinary terms, the scheme may add nothing. If your company is a harder lend, the guarantee can be the difference between funding and no funding, and can sometimes improve the rate too. The only way to know is to compare a scheme offer against a conventional one for your specific circumstances.</p>

<h2>The Bottom Line</h2>

<p>The Growth Guarantee Scheme is a useful tool, but only if you understand what it is. It is a partial government guarantee to the lender that makes it easier for a viable smaller company to borrow. It is not free money, not insurance for the borrower, and not a way to escape repayment or a personal guarantee. Your company borrows, your company repays, in full, on commercial terms.</p>

<p>Used well, the scheme unlocks funding a cautious lender would otherwise refuse. Used on the wrong assumptions, it leads to nasty surprises. Compare a scheme-backed offer against a standard facility for your actual circumstances before you commit, and make sure you know exactly what your company (and you as a director) are signing up to.</p>
