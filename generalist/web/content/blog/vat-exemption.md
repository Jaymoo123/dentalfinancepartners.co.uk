<p>A VAT exempt supply carries no VAT on the invoice, but exemption is not the same thing as zero-rating and the difference is worth real money. Exemption blocks recovery of the VAT on the costs behind the sale, so that VAT stays in your accounts as a cost you never get back. Exempt turnover also sits outside the <strong>£90,000</strong> registration threshold. The exempt supplies are the sixteen groups listed in <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1994/23/schedule/9" rel="noopener noreferrer">Schedule 9 of the Value Added Tax Act 1994</a>.</p>

<p>That last point about recovery is where the money sits. Two businesses can both issue invoices showing £0 of VAT and end the year in completely different positions, because one of them gets the VAT on its rent, its equipment and its professional fees back and the other does not. This page explains what exemption is, lists the Schedule 9 groups, and works the partial exemption de minimis test in figures, because that test is the one small businesses most often apply wrongly.</p>

<p>Each specific exemption has its own page on this site. This is the parent, and it routes you down to the right child at the end.</p>

<h2>The rule: Schedule 9 of the VAT Act 1994</h2>

<p>VAT is charged on taxable supplies made in the UK by a taxable person in the course or furtherance of a business. Exemption works by lifting particular descriptions of supply out of that charge. The descriptions are set out in <strong>VATA 1994 Schedule 9</strong>, which is indexed in Part I and set out in full in Part II. There are <strong>sixteen groups</strong>.</p>

<p>Two consequences flow from the way the legislation is built, and both matter more than the list itself.</p>

<p>First, an exempt supply is <strong>not a taxable supply</strong>. That is not a technicality. The whole input tax recovery mechanism depends on costs being attributable to taxable supplies. Take the supply out of the taxable category and the attributable input tax has nothing to attach to, so it is not recoverable. A zero-rated supply, by contrast, remains a taxable supply. It is simply taxed at 0%. Recovery survives because the supply is still taxable.</p>

<p>Second, exemption attaches to <strong>supplies, not to businesses</strong>. There is no such thing in the legislation as an exempt business. A physiotherapy practice that also sells supports and braces over the counter is making exempt supplies and standard-rated supplies from the same premises on the same day. That is normal, and it is what puts a business into the partial exemption rules further down this page.</p>

<p>Schedule 9 now also carries a <strong>Part 3 (Exceptions)</strong>, inserted by Finance Act 2025, which removes the exemption from certain education supplies. That change is explained in full on our <a href="/blog/vat-and-making-tax-digital/vat-on-education">VAT on education</a> page, which owns that topic. It is noted here only so the structure of the Schedule makes sense: since that amendment, a supply is exempt if it is described in Part 2 <em>and</em> is not described in Part 3.</p>

<h2>VAT exemption for a business: the three consequences, in order</h2>

<p>When people search for VAT exemption business they are usually asking what it does to them commercially. Three things, and they are worth taking in this order.</p>

<h3>1. No output VAT on the exempt sale</h3>

<p>You do not add 20% to the invoice. To the customer, this looks identical to a zero-rated sale. On a £400 physiotherapy course of treatment the patient pays £400, not £480. For a business whose customers are consumers or other exempt bodies who could not have recovered the VAT anyway, this is a genuine competitive position: your price is the customer's cost.</p>

<h3>2. No input tax recovery on the attributable costs</h3>

<p>This is the one that surprises people. Every cost that is attributable to an exempt supply carries VAT you cannot recover. Rent where the landlord has opted to tax, equipment, software subscriptions, professional fees, marketing, vehicles, repairs. If the cost sits behind an exempt supply, the 20% on it is not a reclaimable asset in your accounts, it is part of the cost of the thing.</p>

<p>The practical effect is that an exempt business budgets in VAT-inclusive figures. A £2,000 piece of equipment is £2,400 to you, permanently. A standard-rated competitor prices the same item at £2,000 net. That gap is structural and it does not go away with better bookkeeping.</p>

<h3>3. Exempt turnover is outside the £90,000 registration test</h3>

<p>The registration test in Schedule 1 runs on <strong>taxable turnover</strong>: standard-rated, reduced-rated and zero-rated supplies. Exempt supplies are not taxable supplies, so they never count. A practice turning over £400,000 entirely from exempt medical care has no duty to register at all, and cannot register voluntarily, because there are no taxable supplies for recovery to attach to.</p>

<p>For a mixed business, the test runs only on the taxable slice. A sports club with £180,000 of exempt playing subscriptions and £52,000 of bar and function-room takings tests the £52,000 against £90,000, not the £232,000. The full mechanics of the rolling test are on our <a href="/blog/vat-and-making-tax-digital/vat-threshold-2025-26">VAT threshold</a> page, and the mirror position for a zero-rated seller, where registration is usually worth seeking rather than avoiding, is on <a href="/blog/vat-and-making-tax-digital/when-to-register-for-vat-zero-rated-goods">when to register for VAT if you sell zero-rated goods</a>.</p>

<h2>Outside the scope is a third thing again</h2>

<p>Exempt and zero-rated are two of three categories, not two of two. The third is <strong>outside the scope</strong>, and it means there is no supply for consideration at all, so VAT never engages.</p>

<p>The paradigm is a freely given donation. Money handed over with nothing supplied in return is not consideration for anything, so it is not a supply, so it is neither taxable nor exempt. It is simply outside the system. Grant funding with no strings, and genuine compensation payments, often land in the same place.</p>

<p>Outside the scope resembles exemption in one respect, which is that it does not support input tax recovery either. It differs in how it is counted and reported: an exempt supply is a supply that goes into the partial exemption calculation, while a non-business activity goes into a business and non-business apportionment first, before partial exemption is even reached. A club or a not-for-profit that mislabels donated income as exempt turnover, or exempt subscription income as a donation, distorts both calculations at once. That is the usual source of error in the sector.</p>

<h2>Partial exemption: the de minimis limit, and the two 50% tests that are not the same test</h2>

<p>Most businesses that make exempt supplies also make some taxable ones. Once registered, such a business is <strong>partly exempt</strong>, and its input tax has to be attributed in three buckets.</p>

<ul>
  <li><strong>Directly attributable to taxable supplies</strong>: recoverable in full. The bar stock at the sports club, the braces the physiotherapist resells.</li>
  <li><strong>Directly attributable to exempt supplies</strong>: not recoverable. The treatment-room equipment, the hearse.</li>
  <li><strong>Residual</strong>: costs that support both. Rent, heat and light, accountancy, the phone system. Recoverable in the taxable proportion.</li>
</ul>

<h3>The standard method</h3>

<p>The standard method apportions the residual pot using a value-based fraction. In HMRC's own words in <a href="https://www.gov.uk/guidance/partial-exemption-vat-notice-706" rel="noopener noreferrer">VAT Notice 706</a>:</p>

<blockquote><p>"Value of taxable supplies in the period (excluding VAT) / total value of supplies in the period (excluding VAT) x 100 = recoverable percentage"</p></blockquote>

<p>The resulting percentage is rounded up. So a business with £52,000 of taxable supplies and £180,000 of exempt supplies in the period has taxable supplies of £52,000 out of £232,000, which is 22.41%, rounded up to <strong>23%</strong>. Twenty-three per cent of the residual input tax is recoverable, on top of everything directly attributable to taxable supplies.</p>

<h3>The de minimis limit: both limbs, and they are cumulative</h3>

<p>There is then a relief which lets a business with only a little exempt input tax recover the lot. This is the <strong>de minimis limit</strong>, in <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/1995/2518/regulation/106" rel="noopener noreferrer">regulation 106 of the VAT Regulations 1995</a>. Exempt input tax is recoverable in full where it:</p>

<ol>
  <li>does <strong>not exceed £625 per month on average</strong>, which is <strong>£1,875 a VAT quarter</strong> and <strong>£7,500 a year</strong>; <strong>and</strong></li>
  <li>does <strong>not exceed one half of all input tax</strong> for the period concerned.</li>
</ol>

<p>Regulation 106 puts it this way: relevant input tax which "does not amount to more than £625 per month on average, and ... does not exceed one half of all his input tax for the period concerned" is treated as attributable to taxable supplies.</p>

<p>Both limbs. Cumulative. Fail either one and the exempt input tax is not recoverable, no matter how comfortably the other limb passes.</p>

<h3>The two simplified tests, and the different 50%</h3>

<p>HMRC also operates two simplified de minimis tests, at Notice 706 paragraph 11.7, so that a business can establish it is de minimis without doing the full attribution exercise first.</p>

<ul>
  <li><strong>Simplified test one</strong>: total input tax is no more than £625 per month on average, <strong>and</strong> the value of exempt supplies is no more than 50% of the value of all supplies.</li>
  <li><strong>Simplified test two</strong>: total input tax <em>less</em> input tax directly attributable to taxable supplies is no more than £625 per month on average, <strong>and</strong> the value of exempt supplies is no more than 50% of the value of all supplies.</li>
</ul>

<p><strong>The 50% in the simplified tests is a different 50%.</strong></p>

<p>Set the two side by side and the difference is unmissable.</p>

<p>One 50% is about <strong>input tax</strong>, which is VAT on what you bought. The other is about <strong>supplies</strong>, which is the value of what you sold. They are different numbers measuring different things, and a business can easily pass one and fail the other. Reading one as the other is the misapplication this section exists to correct: the business checks that exempt sales are under half of total sales, concludes it is de minimis, and recovers exempt input tax it was never entitled to.</p>

<p>Note also that the three tests are <strong>alternatives</strong>. Notice 706 paragraph 11.8 is explicit that a business is de minimis if it passes any one of them, whether that is one of the two simplified tests or the full regulation 106 test. They are not hurdles to clear in sequence.</p>

<p>One further point which belongs here rather than in the worked examples: the de minimis tests are applied per return period and then <strong>again over the partial exemption longer period</strong>, as part of the annual adjustment. A business can be de minimis in three quarters out of four and fail on the annual figures, in which case the earlier recovery is adjusted back. There is also a standard method override which can apply where the standard method gives a result that does not fairly reflect the use of the costs. The mechanics of both are set out in Notice 706, and if either is in play the calculation needs checking against the notice rather than against a summary. A dedicated de minimis calculator is planned for this site separately.</p>

<h2>Worked examples: the de minimis test, quarter by quarter</h2>

<h3>Example one: a funeral director who passes both limbs</h3>

<p>A funeral director makes exempt supplies of funerals under Group 8 and standard-rated supplies of memorial masonry and flowers. In the quarter to 30 June, total input tax is <strong>£2,400</strong>, of which <strong>£900</strong> is attributable to the exempt funeral supplies.</p>

<ul>
  <li><strong>Limb (a), the money limit.</strong> The quarterly equivalent of £625 a month is <strong>£1,875</strong>. Exempt input tax of £900 is below £1,875, so limb (a) passes.</li>
  <li><strong>Limb (b), the half of all input tax limit.</strong> Half of total input tax of £2,400 is <strong>£1,200</strong>. Exempt input tax of £900 is below £1,200, so limb (b) passes.</li>
</ul>

<p>Both limbs pass, so the business is de minimis for the quarter and the <strong>£900 of exempt input tax is recoverable in full</strong>. The VAT return claims the whole £2,400.</p>

<h3>Example two: the same business, one limb fails</h3>

<p>In the quarter to 30 September the same funeral director has a quiet period for masonry work. Total input tax is again <strong>£2,400</strong>, but <strong>£1,400</strong> of it is attributable to exempt supplies.</p>

<ul>
  <li><strong>Limb (a).</strong> £1,400 is still below the quarterly £1,875, so limb (a) passes. Nothing has gone wrong yet on the money limit.</li>
  <li><strong>Limb (b).</strong> Half of £2,400 is £1,200. Exempt input tax of £1,400 is <strong>more than</strong> £1,200, so limb (b) fails.</li>
</ul>

<p>One limb failing is enough. The business is <strong>not de minimis</strong> for this quarter, and the <strong>£1,400 is not recoverable</strong>. Only the £1,000 attributable to taxable supplies is claimed.</p>

<p>Compare the two quarters. The total input tax is identical at £2,400 both times. The exempt input tax moved by £500 and stayed comfortably under the £1,875 money limit throughout. Yet the recoverable figure fell from £2,400 to £1,000, a swing of £1,400, entirely on the second limb. A business watching only the £625 monthly average would have claimed the full £2,400 in both quarters and carried an assessable error into the second one.</p>

<h3>Example three: why the wrong 50% gives the wrong answer</h3>

<p>Take that September quarter again and add the sales figures. Exempt funeral turnover is <strong>£58,000</strong> and taxable masonry and floristry turnover is <strong>£62,000</strong>, so total supplies are £120,000 and exempt supplies are 48.3% of them, comfortably under 50%.</p>

<p>A business that reaches for the supplies-based 50% and stops there concludes it is de minimis and recovers all £2,400. But the supplies-based 50% belongs to the simplified tests, and both of those also require total input tax (or total input tax less the directly attributable taxable element) to be no more than £625 a month on average, which is £1,875 a quarter. Total input tax here is £2,400, so <strong>neither simplified test is passed</strong> either. The correct answer remains the one in example two: £1,400 is blocked. The supplies percentage never mattered, because the test that applied was the one measured on input tax.</p>

<h2>What people get wrong</h2>

<ul>
  <li><strong>Treating the de minimis limit as a single 50% test.</strong> This is the error at the top of the list for a reason. There are two 50% tests: exempt input tax against total input tax in regulation 106, and value of exempt supplies against value of all supplies in the two simplified tests. Checking the wrong one produces a confident, well-documented and incorrect recovery claim.</li>
  <li><strong>Applying the £625 as a quarterly figure.</strong> It is a monthly average. On a quarterly return the figure to compare against is <strong>£1,875</strong>, and on an annual basis it is £7,500. Testing £625 against a quarter's exempt input tax blocks recovery that was properly due.</li>
  <li><strong>Saying "we are VAT exempt" when you mean "we are not VAT registered".</strong> These are unrelated situations. A business under the threshold making standard-rated supplies is a taxable business that has not yet had to register, and it will have to once it crosses £90,000. A business making exempt supplies never crosses the test on that turnover at all. The two get conflated in conversation constantly, and they lead to opposite answers on registration.</li>
  <li><strong>Assuming exempt is better than standard-rated.</strong> It is almost always worse than zero-rating, and where a business sells mainly to VAT-registered customers it can be worse than standard rating too. A standard-rated supplier's business customers reclaim the VAT charged, so the 20% costs them nothing, while the supplier recovers all its own input tax. The exempt supplier charges nothing but absorbs the VAT on every cost. Never treat exemption as a benefit without pricing in the blocked recovery.</li>
  <li><strong>Forgetting the annual adjustment.</strong> The de minimis tests are applied per return period and again over the longer period. Quarterly passes do not settle the year.</li>
  <li><strong>Assuming exempt turnover counts toward £90,000.</strong> It does not, and for many readers this is the most valuable line on the page. The mirror error is assuming zero-rated turnover does not count. It does. Taxable turnover includes zero-rated supplies and excludes exempt ones.</li>
  <li><strong>Deciding exemption at business level.</strong> Exemption attaches to supplies. A single business can, and often does, make exempt, zero-rated and standard-rated supplies in the same week.</li>
</ul>
