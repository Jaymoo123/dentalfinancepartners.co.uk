- question: Do bookkeepers legally have to be registered in the UK?
  answer: "There is no register you must join to call yourself a bookkeeper, but there is a supervision requirement you cannot avoid. - question: Does a bookkeeper need a qualification?
  answer: No. Bookkeeper is not a protected title in the UK, and neither is accountant. Anyone can print either word on a business card with no exam, no insurance and no professional body behind them. The restricted roles are narrower than most people assume. Statutory audit work requires registration as a statutory auditor, insolvency work requires a licensed insolvency practitioner, and the chartered designations are limited to members of the chartered bodies. Routine bookkeeping sits outside all of that.
- question: How do I check whether a bookkeeper is supervised?
  answer: Ask which route they use, then verify it. If they say HMRC, ask for the registration number and check it against the Supervised Business Register that HMRC publishes as a downloadable spreadsheet. It shows the registration number, business and trading name, the first part of the postcode, the date supervision began and the sectors covered. If they say a professional body, ask for the membership number and the practising certificate, then confirm it with that body directly. HMRC also warns that a newly registered business can take time to appear, so a recent registration may not show yet.
- question: My bookkeeper is AAT qualified but does not appear on the HMRC register. Is something wrong?
  answer: Probably not. The HMRC register only lists businesses HMRC supervises directly. A bookkeeper supervised through a professional body such as AAT, ACCA, the Institute of Certified Bookkeepers or the Institute of Accountants and Bookkeepers will never appear on it, because their supervision runs through the body rather than HMRC. Absence from the register only becomes a red flag once you have asked which route they use and they cannot evidence either one. Ask for the membership number and check it with the body.
- question: I found out mid-year that my bookkeeper has no AML supervision at all. What should I do?
  answer: Treat it as an immediate change of supplier rather than a wait-and-see item, and separate the two problems. The supervision breach is theirs. The state of your records is yours. Get a full export of the accounting file, the bank feed history and any VAT or payroll submissions made in your name, then have a supervised bookkeeper or accountant review the period already filed. If an incorrect return has gone in, an unprompted disclosure to HMRC attracts a substantially lower penalty than one HMRC has to prompt, so speed matters more than tidiness here.
- question: If my bookkeeper makes a mistake, does HMRC chase them or me?
  answer: You. The return is legally yours, and penalties for inaccuracy or late filing are assessed against the taxpayer, not the agent. HMRC guidance states that relying on another person to do something is not a reasonable excuse unless you took reasonable care to avoid the failure. In practice that means explaining what was required, setting deadlines, and monitoring that the work was actually done. There is a real distinction in the case law between an agent who simply failed to press submit and a professional who gave you considered advice you had no reason to doubt, but neither removes your name from the return.
- question: Does supervision mean my bookkeeper is insured?
  answer: Not automatically. Professional indemnity insurance is a condition of membership at most of the accountancy and bookkeeping bodies, so it usually travels with professional body supervision. Direct HMRC registration carries no such requirement. If your bookkeeper is HMRC supervised, ask separately for a copy of the current PI certificate and check the limit of indemnity against the size of your business. A £50,000 limit is thin cover for a company running six figure VAT through its books.
- question: Do I still need supervision checks if the bookkeeper works inside my own business?
  answer: No. The supervision requirement applies to businesses providing accountancy services to clients. An employee keeping your books on your payroll is not providing a service to a third party, so no AML registration is needed for that role. The moment that person starts taking on outside clients, even a couple of friends' sole trader accounts on evenings, the requirement bites on them.

<p>There are 22 professional bodies named in Schedule 1 of the Money Laundering Regulations 2017, and any bookkeeper who does not belong to one of them must instead pay HMRC £300 to apply plus £400 for every premises they work from. That is the actual legal requirement sitting behind the question. It has nothing to do with exams.</p>

<p>The confusion is understandable, because two separate things get bundled into the word "registered". One is a compulsory anti-money laundering supervision requirement that applies to every bookkeeper trading with clients, qualified or not. The other is voluntary membership of a professional body, which is a quality signal rather than a licence. A bookkeeper can be fully legal and completely unqualified. A bookkeeper can also be highly qualified and, if they have let their supervision lapse, trading unlawfully.</p>

<p>This guide separates the two, gives you the three checks worth running before you hand over a year of records, and closes on the part most people get wrong: who HMRC actually penalises when the numbers are off.</p>

<h2>The legal requirement: AML supervision</h2>

<p>Under the Money Laundering, Terrorist Financing and Transfer of Funds (Information on the Payer) Regulations 2017, businesses providing accountancy services must be supervised for anti-money laundering purposes. Bookkeeping falls squarely inside that definition. So does payroll processing for clients, VAT return preparation, and tax return work.</p>

<p>There are exactly two lawful routes. The bookkeeper either belongs to one of the professional bodies listed in Schedule 1 of the regulations, which supervises them as part of membership, or they register with HMRC directly as an accountancy service provider. HMRC's own guidance is direct about the consequence of doing neither: a business must not trade before registering, and trading while unregistered is a criminal offence.</p>

<p>The Schedule 1 list runs to 22 bodies covering both accountancy and legal services. The ones you are most likely to meet on a bookkeeper's website are AAT, ACCA, ICAEW, CIMA, IFA, the Institute of Certified Bookkeepers, and the Institute of Accountants and Bookkeepers. That last name is new. It replaced the International Association of Bookkeepers in Schedule 1 on 30 June 2026 under SI 2026/621, so older articles and older membership logos still show the previous name. It is a rename, not a new body.</p>

<p>A one person bookkeeping business working from home therefore faces £740 to register and £400 a year after that. Professional body membership is often comparable once you add subscription, continuing professional development and the practising certificate, which is why plenty of competent bookkeepers pick the body route and get supervision, a complaints procedure and an insurance requirement in the same package.</p>

<h2>The optional part: qualifications</h2>

<p>Nothing in UK law reserves the word bookkeeper. Nothing reserves the word accountant either. Both can be used by anyone, with no exam passed and no body behind them.</p>

<p>The genuinely restricted roles are narrow. Statutory audit requires registration as a statutory auditor. Insolvency work requires a licensed insolvency practitioner. The chartered designations are limited to members of the chartered institutes, so "chartered accountant" means something specific in a way that "accountant" does not. Everything a bookkeeper does day to day sits outside those reserved areas.</p>

<p>That does not make qualifications decorative. A bookkeeper holding an ICB or AAT practising certificate has passed assessments, submits to a complaints process, has to carry professional indemnity insurance and can be disciplined by their body. Someone with no letters after their name may be equally capable, but if the work goes wrong your only route is the courts. The practical question is not "are they qualified" in the abstract, it is "what happens to me if they get this wrong".</p>

<h2>Three checks before you hire</h2>

<p>Run these in order. The whole sequence takes about fifteen minutes.</p>

<p><strong>1. Ask which supervision route they use.</strong> The answer should be immediate and specific. "HMRC, registration number XML..." or "ICB, membership number..." A vague answer about being "fully compliant" is the answer, and it is not a good one.</p>

<p><strong>2. Verify it independently.</strong> For HMRC supervision, download the Supervised Business Register from GOV.UK and search it. It lists registration number, business and trading name, the first part of the postcode, the date supervision began and the sectors registered. For professional body supervision, contact the body with the membership number. Two cautions apply. HMRC notes that newly registered businesses can take time to appear on the register, and that appearing on it is confirmation of registration, not an endorsement to trade with anyone. Also, and this trips up a lot of business owners, a bookkeeper supervised by a professional body will never show on the HMRC register at all. Absence is not evidence until you have asked the first question.</p>

<p><strong>3. Match the engagement to the work.</strong> Ask for a letter of engagement setting out what they will do, what stays with you, deadlines and fees. Ask for the PI insurance certificate and check the limit of indemnity against your turnover. Ask which software they work in, because from 6 April 2026 sole traders and landlords with qualifying income above £50,000 fall inside <a href="/blog/vat-and-making-tax-digital">Making Tax Digital for Income Tax</a> and quarterly updates cannot be produced from a shoebox and a spreadsheet emailed each January.</p>

<h2>Where the liability actually sits</h2>

<p>Here is the part that costs people money. Your VAT return, your self assessment, your company accounts and your <a href="/blog/payroll-and-paye">RTI payroll submissions</a> are legally yours. Appointing an agent to prepare and file them does not transfer that. HMRC assesses inaccuracy and late filing penalties against the taxpayer.</p>

<p>The myth is that a bookkeeper's mistake automatically becomes a reasonable excuse. It does not. HMRC's guidance sets out the rule plainly: where a person relies on any other person to do anything, that is not a reasonable excuse unless the person took reasonable care to avoid the relevant failure. Reasonable care in this context is behavioural and evidenced. Did you explain what was needed? Did you set a deadline? Did you check the work had actually been done and the return actually filed? A business owner who can produce a chain of emails chasing a bookkeeper who went silent is in a very different position from one who handed over a carrier bag in April and thought no more about it.</p>

<p>The tribunal in Lithgow drew a distinction worth understanding. An agent acting as an administrator, the person who simply had to press submit, gives you little room to argue you were not in default. A professional giving genuine advisory advice is different: acting on properly given professional advice, where you had no reason to think it wrong or heavily caveated, usually supports the conclusion that you were not careless. Both routes still leave the penalty notice addressed to you in the first instance.</p>

<p>The practical consequence is that verifying supervision is not a compliance box exercise. It is the cheapest available filter on who is holding records you remain personally answerable for.</p>

<h2>Red flags worth walking away from</h2>

<ul>
<li>No supervision route named, or a claim that supervision is "not needed for bookkeeping"</li>
<li>Refusal to provide a registration or membership number for you to check</li>
<li>No letter of engagement, or one that says nothing about who is responsible for filing deadlines</li>
<li>No professional indemnity insurance, or a limit far below your turnover</li>
<li>Offering to file work that needs a different credential, such as signing off an audit</li>
<li>Holding your accounting data in a system you have no login to and cannot export</li>
</ul>

<p>That last one matters more than it sounds. If your records live in a subscription in someone else's name, changing bookkeeper stops being a decision and becomes a negotiation.</p>

<h2>Getting the split right</h2>

<p>For most businesses, the sensible arrangement is a supervised bookkeeper handling the volume work and an accountant handling the statutory filings and the planning. The bookkeeper keeps the ledger accurate at a lower hourly rate. The accountant works from clean data, which is where the fee savings actually come from. <p>To answer the question directly: your bookkeeper does not need a qualification, but they absolutely need AML supervision, and you can verify that in a quarter of an hour. Do the check before you hand over the records, not after HMRC asks a question you cannot answer.</p>
