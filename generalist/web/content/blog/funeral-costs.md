<p>Funeral costs carry two different VAT treatments on the same invoice, and the split catches out both the businesses that raise those invoices and the people who read them. The funeral itself is <strong>exempt</strong> from VAT: the disposal of the remains of the dead, and the making of arrangements for it, carry no VAT at all under <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1994/23/schedule/9" rel="noopener noreferrer">VATA 1994 Schedule 9 Group 8</a>. The flowers, the headstone and the newspaper announcement are standard rated at <strong>20%</strong>. Because the main supply is exempt, the business also cannot recover all of the VAT on its own costs.</p>

<p>This page is written for the funeral business, not for a bereaved family comparing prices. It answers the VAT question a funeral director, a cemetery operator or a crematorium operator has to answer every time they raise an invoice: which lines carry VAT, which do not, and what the exemption costs the business in unrecovered input tax. It does not publish funeral prices and it does not compare providers.</p>

<h2>The rule: Schedule 9 Group 8, burial and cremation</h2>

<p>Exempt supplies are listed in <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1994/23/schedule/9" rel="noopener noreferrer">Schedule 9 of the Value Added Tax Act 1994</a>, which is indexed in Part I and set out in Part II. There are sixteen groups. <strong>Group 8 is Burial and cremation</strong>, and its first two items are the whole of the funeral exemption:</p>

<ul>
  <li><strong>Item 1:</strong> the disposal of the remains of the dead.</li>
  <li><strong>Item 2:</strong> the making of arrangements for, or in connection with, the disposal of the remains of the dead.</li>
</ul>

<p>Read those two items together and the shape of the exemption becomes clear. Item 1 covers the physical act: the burial, the cremation. Item 2 covers the professional work of organising it, which is what a funeral director is actually paid for. HMRC's published guidance on the treatment is in <a href="https://www.gov.uk/guidance/burial-cremation-and-commemoration-of-the-dead-notice-70132" rel="noopener noreferrer">VAT Notice 701/32, burial, cremation and commemoration of the dead</a>.</p>

<p>Note what Group 8 does not say. It does not exempt everything a funeral business sells, it does not exempt everything that appears on a funeral invoice, and it does not exempt everything connected with a death. It exempts disposal, and arranging disposal. Everything else on the bill has to earn its exemption by being incidental and closely related to that, or it is taxed in the ordinary way.</p>

<h2>Who can make the exempt supply</h2>

<p>The exemption attaches to the supply, not to a licence or a register. Anyone making a supply that falls within items 1 or 2 makes an exempt supply. In practice that means:</p>

<ul>
  <li><strong>Undertakers and funeral directors</strong>, for the funeral and for arranging it.</li>
  <li><strong>Cemetery operators</strong>, for burial and for interment fees.</li>
  <li><strong>Crematorium operators</strong>, for cremation.</li>
</ul>

<p>The point that saves the most argument in practice is this one: <strong>the supply stays exempt whether it is billed to the bereaved family or to another funeral professional</strong>. A funeral director who carries out a funeral on behalf of another funeral director, or who provides the disposal element into another firm's package, is still making an exempt supply and still charges no VAT on it. There is no rule that says exemption is lost the moment the customer is a business rather than a consumer, and there is no requirement for the customer to be the person who commissioned the funeral.</p>

<p>That is worth stating plainly because the instinct in most of VAT runs the other way. Business-to-business supplies are usually the ones that carry tax. Here they do not, and a funeral director who has been charging 20% on exempt work billed to a trade customer has been charging VAT that was never due.</p>

<h2>The boundary: exempt against standard rated inside one funeral bill</h2>

<p>A typical funeral package is not one supply. It is an exempt supply with standard-rated goods and services bolted onto it, and the invoice has to reflect that. The table below sets out the boundary using the things the same businesses actually sell.</p>

<p>The dividing line, and the sentence worth committing to memory, is <strong>disposal against commemoration</strong>. What gets the deceased buried or cremated is exempt. What remembers them afterwards is standard rated. Once the test is put that way, most of the difficult lines answer themselves: a hearse moves the deceased, so it is on the exempt side; a memorial bench remembers them, so it carries VAT.</p>

<h3>Reading the table in practice</h3>

<p>Three cases account for most of the arguments, and the disposal-against-commemoration test settles all three.</p>

<p><strong>Flowers the family asks the funeral director to arrange.</strong> The family never speaks to the florist, the flowers appear on the funeral director's invoice, and the whole thing feels like part of the funeral. It is still a standard-rated supply of flowers by the funeral director, because the identity of the person who arranges a supply does not change what the supply is. The director is making two supplies here, not one.</p>

<p><strong>A headstone ordered through the funeral director.</strong> Same answer, same reason. The mason charges the director, the director charges the family, and both supplies are standard rated. The memorial is commemoration, and commemoration never enters the exemption no matter how many hands the invoice passes through.</p>

<p><strong>The hearse.</strong> This is the case that goes the other way and it is worth seeing why. Transport is not usually exempt, and a vehicle is plainly not the disposal of remains. It is on the exempt side only because it is incidental to and closely related to the disposal: it exists to get the deceased to the grave or the crematorium, and nobody buys it for its own sake. That is a narrow, purposive test, and it is the reason the coffin and the bearers come with it.</p>

<h2>Goods that follow the exemption because they are incidental</h2>

<p>The coffin is the case that shows why the exemption is not purely about services. A coffin is plainly goods, not a service, and nothing in items 1 and 2 mentions goods at all. It still follows the exempt treatment, because it is <strong>incidental to and closely related to</strong> the exempt supply of disposing of the remains. You cannot carry out a burial or a cremation without it, and the family is not shopping for a coffin as a thing in its own right. It is part of the funeral.</p>

<p>The same logic puts <strong>bearers</strong> and <strong>the transport of the deceased</strong> on the exempt side. Both exist only to get the deceased to the place of disposal. Neither is bought for its own sake.</p>

<p>Do not stretch this further than it goes. "Incidental and closely related" is a test, not a wildcard, and it does not reach the commemorative goods in the right-hand column of the table above. Flowers are supplied at the same time, on the same invoice, arranged by the same business, and they are still standard rated, because they are not incidental to disposing of the remains. They are their own thing. The fact that something appears on a funeral invoice tells you nothing at all about its VAT treatment.</p>

<h2>Apportioning a funeral package</h2>

<p>Because a package carries both treatments, it <strong>normally needs apportioning</strong>. That is the operative compliance point for a funeral business and it is where the practical work sits.</p>

<p>The easiest version of the problem is where the package is priced line by line: £X for the funeral, £Y for flowers, £Z for the announcement. Then there is nothing to apportion, because the consideration for each element is already identified. The harder version is a single inclusive price covering an exempt funeral and standard-rated extras, where the business has to split one figure between two treatments.</p>

<p>Where a split is needed, it must be made on a <strong>fair and supportable basis</strong>, and the business needs to be able to show how it was arrived at if HMRC asks. There is no single prescribed formula that applies to every funeral business, and any calculation presented as the official method should be treated with suspicion. What matters is that the basis is reasonable, that it is applied consistently, and that the working is retained with the records. Costs-based and market-value-based approaches are both used in practice; the question in any enquiry is whether the split produces a sensible answer, not whether it matches a particular template.</p>

<p>Two practical habits are worth building in. First, <strong>price the standard-rated elements separately on the invoice wherever you can</strong>, because a stated price is far easier to defend than a derived one. Second, <strong>state whether your quoted prices are VAT inclusive or VAT exclusive</strong> for the standard-rated lines, because on an invoice that mostly carries no VAT at all, a family reading the total will not assume VAT is buried in it.</p>

<h2>What exemption actually costs the business</h2>

<p>Exemption sounds like relief. For the business making the supply it is a cost, and it is the most commercially important thing on this page.</p>

<p>An exempt supply has three consequences:</p>

<ol>
  <li><strong>No output VAT</strong> is charged on the sale.</li>
  <li><strong>No input tax recovery</strong> on the costs attributable to that sale. The VAT on those costs is not reclaimed; it stays in the cost base.</li>
  <li><strong>The turnover sits outside the £90,000 registration test.</strong> The test in <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1994/23/schedule/1" rel="noopener noreferrer">VATA 1994 Schedule 1</a> runs on taxable turnover, which means standard-rated, reduced-rated and zero-rated supplies. Exempt funeral income is simply not counted.</li>
</ol>

<p>Point 2 is the one that shows up in the accounts. A hearse, a fleet vehicle, the rent and fitting-out of a chapel of rest, stationery, professional fees: all of it carries VAT, and to the extent those costs are attributable to exempt funeral supplies, none of that VAT comes back. On a £60,000 vehicle bought for exempt funeral work, £10,000 of VAT is absorbed rather than reclaimed.</p>

<p>Point 3 has a consequence people find counterintuitive. A funeral business can be well past £90,000 of total income while its <em>taxable</em> turnover, the flowers, the memorials and the announcements, sits below the threshold. Registration is tested on that taxable slice alone. Some funeral businesses register voluntarily anyway, precisely to recover the input tax attributable to the standard-rated side, and that decision turns on the size of that side and the VAT sitting behind it.</p>

<h3>Partial exemption, in two sentences</h3>

<p>A funeral business making both exempt funeral supplies and standard-rated commemorative supplies is <strong>partly exempt</strong>, so its input tax splits three ways: directly attributable to taxable supplies and recoverable in full, directly attributable to exempt supplies and not recoverable, and residual, which is apportioned. Where the exempt input tax is small enough it can still be recovered in full under the <strong>de minimis limit</strong>, which is broadly £625 per month on average (£1,875 a quarter, £7,500 a year) together with a 50% test, and there are two simplified tests that use a different 50% comparison which businesses routinely confuse with the first.</p>

<p>Both the mechanics and the trap are set out in full on the parent page: <a href="/blog/vat-and-making-tax-digital/vat-exemption">VAT exemption, Schedule 9, partial exemption and the de minimis limit</a>. Work through it before attempting the calculation, because conflating the two 50% tests is the most common error in this area and it produces a recovery figure that looks right and is not.</p>

<h2>Worked example one: one invoice, two treatments</h2>

<p>A funeral director raises a single invoice covering a <strong>£2,900</strong> funeral, <strong>£180</strong> of floral tributes and a <strong>£95</strong> newspaper announcement. Assume the flowers and the announcement are priced VAT exclusive, which is an assumption that has to be stated because it changes the arithmetic.</p>

<p>The £2,900 carries no VAT. The £275 of commemorative and ancillary goods carries <strong>£55</strong> of VAT. One invoice, two treatments, and the VAT column is not blank even though the largest line on the bill is exempt.</p>

<p>Change one thing and the answer changes. If the £180 and the £95 had been quoted to the family as VAT-inclusive prices, the VAT inside them would be £275 x 1/6 = <strong>£45.83</strong>, and the total would stay at £3,175. Same goods, same treatment, different arithmetic, which is why the inclusive-or-exclusive question has to be settled at the point of quoting rather than at the point of invoicing.</p>

<h2>Worked example two: the same business across a quarter</h2>

<p>Now step back from the invoice to the VAT return. Over a quarter, the same funeral director takes <strong>£58,000</strong> of exempt funeral income and <strong>£12,000</strong> of standard-rated income from flowers, memorials and announcements. Roughly 83% of turnover is exempt.</p>

<p>The input tax follows that shape. VAT on the hearse, on the chapel-of-rest rent, on the funeral stationery and on professional fees relating to the funeral side is <strong>attributable to exempt supplies and is not recoverable</strong>. VAT on the flower stock and on memorial masonry bought in for resale is attributable to taxable supplies and is recoverable in full. VAT on the things that serve both, the office, the accounting software, the phone system, is <strong>residual</strong> and is apportioned, with only the taxable proportion recovered.</p>

<p>The practical effect is that most of this business's input VAT does not come back. It sits in the cost base, which means it has to be recovered through pricing instead, and a funeral business that budgets as though its purchase VAT is reclaimable will be short by the amount of that unrecovered tax every quarter.</p>

<p>Contrast a <strong>stonemason</strong>. Every supply a stonemason makes, headstones, memorials, plaques, lettering, is standard rated. There is no exempt slice, no partial exemption, no residual apportionment, and every pound of input VAT on stone, tools, workshop rent and vehicles is recovered in full. Two businesses working on adjacent parts of the same event, and completely different VAT positions. That difference is worth understanding before a funeral director decides to bring memorial masonry in-house, or a mason decides to start offering funeral services.</p>

<h2>Registering, invoicing and keeping the records straight</h2>

<p>Three practical consequences follow from everything above, and they are the things a funeral business actually has to do.</p>

<h3>Work out which turnover is being tested</h3>

<p>Because exempt funeral income is excluded from the registration test, the first job is to separate the two streams in the books rather than watching a single total. A business tracking one turnover figure against £90,000 is measuring the wrong thing in both directions: it may believe it must register when its taxable slice is nowhere near the threshold, or it may miss the point at which the flowers, memorials and announcements alone cross it. The test is run on the taxable slice, month by month, on a rolling twelve-month basis in the ordinary way.</p>

<p>If the taxable slice is comfortably below the threshold, registration becomes a commercial decision rather than a legal one. Voluntary registration lets the business recover the input tax attributable to that taxable side, and the question is simply whether the VAT recoverable on flower stock, masonry and the related overheads is worth the compliance and the 20% now charged on those sales. Where customers are bereaved families rather than VAT-registered businesses, that 20% is a real price increase and not a wash, which pushes the arithmetic the other way.</p>

<h3>Build the split into the invoice, not into a year-end adjustment</h3>

<p>A funeral invoice that shows the exempt and standard-rated elements on separate lines, each priced, does three jobs at once: it charges the right VAT, it makes the apportionment self-evident, and it leaves a record that answers an HMRC question without reconstruction. An invoice that shows a single inclusive figure does none of those and forces a split to be derived later, from memory, under pressure. The cheapest time to solve this problem is when the quote is written.</p>

<h3>Attribute costs as they come in</h3>

<p>Partial exemption is an attribution exercise, and attribution is easy at the point of purchase and hard at the end of a quarter. Flower stock is taxable. Memorial masonry bought in for resale is taxable. The hearse, the chapel of rest and the funeral stationery are exempt. The office, the software and the phones are residual. Coding those categories into the purchase ledger as invoices arrive turns the quarterly calculation into a report rather than a research project, and it is also what makes the de minimis tests quick to run rather than something the business skips and assumes it fails.</p>

<h2>Funeral costs and inheritance tax</h2>

<p>People searching for funeral costs are often asking a different question entirely: whether the cost of the funeral can be taken off the estate for inheritance tax. It is a fair question and it has nothing to do with the VAT treatment above.</p>

<p>At the level of principle, <strong>reasonable funeral expenses are deductible in computing the value of an estate for inheritance tax</strong>. What is reasonable, what is included, how it interacts with the rest of the estate and how it is claimed all depend on the particular estate and are outside the scope of this page. We are not going to publish a figure, a cap or a rule of thumb here, because the answer that matters is the one that applies to the actual estate in front of the executor, and a number lifted from a general page is exactly how executors get this wrong.</p>

<p>For the current official position, start with <a href="https://www.gov.uk/inheritance-tax" rel="noopener noreferrer">GOV.UK's inheritance tax guidance</a>, and see also <a href="https://www.gov.uk/valuing-estate-of-someone-who-died" rel="noopener noreferrer">valuing the estate of someone who has died</a>. For the planning side, our page on <a href="/blog/limited-company-tax/trust-accountant-uk-inheritance-tax-planning">trusts and inheritance tax planning</a> covers the surrounding ground. Where an estate is anything other than straightforward, take advice on it specifically rather than relying on general guidance.</p>

<p>One neutral note on <strong>pre-paid funeral plans</strong>, because they come up in the same searches: funeral plan providers and intermediaries are regulated by the Financial Conduct Authority, and the FCA sets out the consumer position at <a href="https://www.fca.org.uk/consumers/funeral-plans" rel="noopener noreferrer">fca.org.uk</a>. We do not advise on funeral plans and this page makes no comparison between them.</p>

<h2>What people get wrong</h2>

<h3>Treating the whole package as exempt because the funeral is exempt</h3>

<p>This is the big one, and it is an error that compounds. A business that treats flowers, memorials and announcements as exempt has under-declared output VAT on every one of those sales, and has probably also under-recovered its input tax on the same goods, because it has attributed them to the exempt side. Both halves of the mistake have to be unwound. The commemorative goods are standard rated and always were.</p>

<h3>Assuming exempt means better off</h3>

<p>Exemption is not a relief in the business's favour. It removes output tax and it removes recovery, and for a business with substantial VAT-bearing costs, the lost recovery is the larger number. This is the whole difference between exemption and zero-rating, and it is why a zero-rated business is in a structurally better VAT position than an exempt one.</p>

<h3>Charging VAT when billing another funeral professional</h3>

<p>The exemption attaches to the supply. A funeral director's exempt service billed on to another funeral professional is still exempt. Charging 20% on it because the customer is a business is wrong, and the customer, being largely exempt themselves, will not be able to recover the VAT they should never have been charged.</p>

<h3>Confusing exempt with zero rated</h3>

<p>Both show £0 in the VAT column, and on the face of an invoice they look identical. They are not. A zero-rated supply is a taxable supply charged at 0%, and the seller recovers the VAT on the costs behind it. An exempt supply is not a taxable supply at all, and that VAT is lost. The distinction also decides whether the turnover counts toward the £90,000 registration threshold: zero-rated turnover counts, exempt turnover does not. Our page on <a href="/blog/vat-and-making-tax-digital/zero-rated-vat">zero-rated VAT</a> sets out the difference in full.</p>

<h3>Treating the IHT deduction and the VAT treatment as the same question</h3>

<p>They are unrelated. Whether VAT was charged on a line of a funeral invoice has no bearing on whether that cost is deductible against the estate, and the deductibility of funeral expenses has no bearing on how the funeral director accounts for VAT. Two different taxes, two different tests, two different people asking.</p>

<h3>Assuming there is a published apportionment formula</h3>

<p>There is not one that applies universally. The requirement is a fair and supportable basis, consistently applied and documented. A formula copied from a trade forum and presented to HMRC as the official method is not a defence; the working behind your own split is.</p>
