<p>A membership subscription is exempt from VAT only in two situations: where a non-profit making club supplies it to someone who is actually taking part in sport, or where it is a subscription to a trade union, professional or other public interest body. Everything else, including commercial gym memberships, social memberships at sports clubs and most discount or networking subscriptions, is standard-rated at 20%.</p>

<p>That sentence resolves most of the question, but it hides the part that costs clubs money. The exemption is not a reward for being a sports club. It is a test of the body making the supply and of what the member actually receives, and a club that gets it right on the subscription can still lose more than it saves on the input tax it can no longer recover.</p>

<p>This guide sets out the rule and its statutory home, the four cumulative conditions a club has to meet, the boundary between an exempt and a standard-rated subscription with examples on both sides, the apportionment a mixed club must carry out, the partial exemption consequence that follows, and the separate exemption for professional body and trade union subscriptions.</p>

<h2>The rule: VATA 1994 Schedule 9 Group 10</h2>

<p>VAT exemptions are listed in <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1994/23/schedule/9" rel="noopener noreferrer">Schedule 9 of the Value Added Tax Act 1994</a>. Group 10 of that Schedule is named "Sport, sports competitions and physical education". It exempts certain sporting services supplied by an <strong>eligible body</strong> to individuals taking part in sport or physical education, together with entry to certain competitions in sport or physical recreation.</p>

<p>Two conditions do all the work, and both have to hold at the same time:</p>

<ol>
<li><strong>The supplier must be an eligible body.</strong> That is a question about the constitution and the destination of the surplus, not about the sport. HMRC's conditions are set out below.</li>
<li><strong>The individual must be taking part in the sport or physical education.</strong> That is a question about what the member receives, not about the club's overall character.</li>
</ol>

<p>Miss either one and the supply is standard-rated. A commercial gym fails the first. A social member at a genuine amateur club fails the second. HMRC's published practice on the whole group is in <a href="https://www.gov.uk/guidance/sport-supplies-that-are-vat-exempt-notice-70145" rel="noopener noreferrer">VAT Notice 701/45, Sport supplies that are VAT exempt</a>, which is the document a treasurer should be working from.</p>

<p>One point of citation discipline. This page cites <strong>the group</strong>, not individual item numbers within it, because the item-level wording of Group 10 has not been read verbatim against the statute. If you need the exact statutory words for a file note or a ruling request, read them on legislation.gov.uk directly rather than taking them second hand from any guide, this one included.</p>

<h2>The four cumulative eligible-body conditions</h2>

<p>This is the test almost every club assumes it passes. Notice 701/45 sets out four conditions, and <strong>all four must hold</strong>. Passing three is not a partial exemption; it is no exemption.</p>

<ol>
<li><strong>The body must be non-profit making.</strong> That means it is precluded from distributing any profit to a person with a financial interest in it. Note what this does not mean. It is not a statement that the club never makes a surplus. A club can run a healthy surplus for years and remain non-profit making, because the test is about where the surplus can go, not whether one exists.</li>
<li><strong>The constitution must prevent distribution of profit.</strong> The club's governing document must contain a clause preventing the distribution of profit, or restricting any distribution to another non-profit making body or to members on winding up or dissolution. This is a documentary condition. If the constitution is silent, the condition is not met, whatever the club's actual practice has been.</li>
<li><strong>Any surplus from playing activities must actually be applied</strong> to the maintenance or improvement of the club's related facilities. This is a behavioural condition sitting on top of the documentary one. A clause in the rules is not enough if the money in fact goes somewhere else.</li>
<li><strong>The body must not be subject to commercial influence</strong> and must not be part of a wider commercial undertaking. A club that has been restructured so that a connected commercial operator takes value out of it can fail here even though the first three limbs look clean.</li>
</ol>

<p>Read together, conditions one to three describe a body whose constitution locks the money inside the sport and which then behaves that way. Condition four stops the structure being hollowed out from the outside. A commercially owned leisure operator fails at limb one and never reaches the rest.</p>

<h2>What the exemption covers beyond the subscription</h2>

<p>Where the two conditions hold, the exemption is wider than the word "subscription" suggests. It reaches the services closely linked with and essential to the sport being supplied to the person taking part, including:</p>

<ul>
<li>Playing, competing, refereeing, umpiring, judging, coaching and training</li>
<li>Use of changing rooms, showers, playing equipment and equipment storage</li>
<li>Match fees charged for the use of playing facilities</li>
<li>Mooring, hangarage and workshop facilities, which is where sailing, gliding and motorsport clubs sit</li>
</ul>

<p>What it does not reach is everything the club sells that is not the sport. Bar takings, catering, function hire, shop and equipment sales and sponsorship are separate commercial supplies and follow their own treatment. Detailed positions on a club's bar and catering income belong to <a href="https://www.gov.uk/guidance/sport-supplies-that-are-vat-exempt-notice-70145" rel="noopener noreferrer">HMRC's own guidance</a>, which is the right source for them, and any alcohol duty question is a separate excise matter covered on <a href="https://www.gov.uk/topic/business-tax/alcohol-duties" rel="noopener noreferrer">GOV.UK</a>.</p>

<h2>What "non-profit making" actually requires</h2>

<p>Almost every argument about a club's VAT position ends up here, so it is worth slowing down on it. "Non-profit making" is a description of the club's <strong>constitution and the destination of its money</strong>. It is not a description of its trading result.</p>

<p>Three practical consequences follow, and each of them surprises somebody:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>A surplus does not break the exemption.</strong> A club can take more in subscriptions, match fees and competition entries than it spends, year after year, and remain non-profit making, provided that surplus is locked in by the constitution and actually applied to the club's sporting facilities. Treasurers occasionally suppress a surplus in the belief that showing one would cost the exemption. It would not, and the suppression creates a different problem in the accounts.</li>
<li><strong>A deficit does not create the exemption.</strong> A commercial operator losing money is still a commercial operator. There is no limb of the test that a struggling proprietary gym can pass.</li>
<li><strong>The document has to say it.</strong> A club run in an entirely non-commercial spirit for fifty years, whose rules happen to be silent on distribution, does not meet the second condition. This is the cheapest failure to fix and the most common one to find: read the rules before you assert the exemption, and if the clause is missing, get it put in properly and date the change.</li>
</ul>

<p>The fourth limb, commercial influence, is the one that has moved most in practice. It is aimed at arrangements where a club's form is amateur but its substance funnels value to a connected commercial party, for example through a linked management company, a tied lease or a related-party supply priced to extract the surplus. A committee that is asked to restructure the club's operations around an outside operator should treat the VAT position as one of the questions on the table, not as something to check afterwards.</p>

<p>Where the club's status is genuinely uncertain, the answer is not a house view. It is HMRC's, in writing, on the club's own constitution and facts.</p>

<p>Read down the two columns and the pattern is clear. The deciding question is never the word printed on the invoice. It is <strong>who supplies it</strong> and <strong>whether the member is actually playing</strong>. The same sport, at the same price, on the same evening, changes treatment when the supplier changes.</p>

<h2>Social and non-playing membership: standard-rated, and why</h2>

<p>This is the single most commonly misapplied point in the whole area, and the error is always in the same direction. A treasurer reasons that the club is an eligible body, that the club's subscriptions are therefore exempt, and that all subscriptions are subscriptions. That reasoning drops the second condition.</p>

<p>The exemption is for services supplied to an individual <strong>taking part</strong> in sport. A social member who has no right to play is not taking part. What that member buys is access to a clubhouse, a bar and a social calendar, which is a standard-rated supply of services. The same applies to non-playing categories that clubs invent for their own reasons: honorary social members, country members with no playing rights, associate members who only attend fixtures as spectators.</p>

<p>The consequence is that a club with both classes of member is making two different kinds of supply out of one membership scheme, and has to account for them separately.</p>

<h2>The apportionment, and what it drags in</h2>

<p>A club with playing and social members must split its subscription income between the exempt and the standard-rated element. There is no single prescribed formula for that split. HMRC expects a method that is <strong>fair and supportable on the club's own facts</strong>, based on what each class of member actually receives, and the club should be able to produce its workings on request. Do not copy a percentage from another club's accounts; its membership structure is not yours.</p>

<p>The split is the easy part. The consequence is not. Once a club has exempt income, it is <strong>partly exempt</strong> for input tax purposes, and the rules on recovery apply to everything it spends:</p>

<ul>
<li>Input tax directly attributable to <strong>taxable</strong> supplies (the bar stock, the function catering) is recoverable in full.</li>
<li>Input tax directly attributable to <strong>exempt</strong> supplies (the pitch maintenance, the nets, the playing equipment) is <strong>not recoverable at all</strong>.</li>
<li><strong>Residual</strong> input tax on costs serving both (insurance, accountancy, utilities, the clubhouse roof) is apportioned, recoverable only in the taxable proportion.</li>
</ul>

<p>There is relief for small amounts. Exempt input tax can still be recovered in full where it falls within the partial exemption <strong>de minimis limit</strong>, which requires exempt input tax of no more than £625 a month on average (£1,875 a quarter, £7,500 a year) and no more than half of all input tax in the period. Two simplified tests offer alternative routes to the same outcome, and they use a different 50% comparison: the value of exempt supplies against the value of all supplies. Those two 50% tests are not the same test and conflating them is the usual source of error. The full arithmetic, including the simplified tests and the annual adjustment, is set out in our guide to <a href="/blog/vat-and-making-tax-digital/vat-exemption">VAT exemption and partial exemption</a>, which is the parent page for this one, and in <a href="https://www.gov.uk/guidance/partial-exemption-vat-notice-706" rel="noopener noreferrer">VAT Notice 706</a>.</p>

<p>The commercial point a club committee needs to hear before it celebrates an exemption: <strong>exemption blocks recovery</strong>. For a club planning a clubhouse rebuild, a new all-weather surface or a floodlight installation, the irrecoverable VAT on that project can dwarf several years of VAT saved on subscriptions. Work out the recovery position before the capital decision, not after the invoice arrives.</p>

<h2>Schedule 9 Group 9: trade unions and professional bodies</h2>

<p>The other half of the membership question has nothing to do with sport. Group 9 of Schedule 9 is named "Subscriptions to trade unions, professional and other public interest bodies", and it exempts subscriptions to bodies of that character. It is the reason a chartered institute's annual practising subscription does not carry VAT while a commercial trade directory's does.</p>

<p>The qualitative boundary is the useful thing to hold on to. The exemption is aimed at bodies whose purpose is the members' profession, their representation as workers, or an object in the public interest. It is not a general relief for "any organisation a business pays to join". A subscription that is in substance the purchase of a commercial service, business leads, discounted supplies, advertising or a publication, sits outside it, whatever the body calls itself.</p>

<p>The item-level conditions for Group 9 are detailed and are not reproduced here, because publishing a condition list we have not verified line by line would be worse than useless to a body deciding how to invoice its members. Check the specific body's position against <a href="https://www.gov.uk/guidance/clubs-and-associations-vat-responsibilities-notice-7015" rel="noopener noreferrer">HMRC's guidance on clubs and associations</a> and the Group 9 material on <a href="https://www.gov.uk/topic/business-tax/vat" rel="noopener noreferrer">GOV.UK</a>, or get the position confirmed in writing.</p>

<p>One practical wrinkle that catches professional bodies: a subscription often buys a bundle. Representation and regulation on one side, a journal, an events programme, a members' insurance arrangement or a discount scheme on the other. Where the package contains genuinely separate elements, the subscription has to be <strong>apportioned</strong> between the exempt and the taxable parts in the same way a mixed sports club apportions, and the same partial exemption consequences follow on the body's own costs. This is the most common issue in practice, and it is a bigger source of error than the sports exemption because more bodies are affected by it.</p>

<h2>Worked example one: a cricket club with two membership classes</h2>

<p>A members' cricket club is an eligible body: it is non-profit making, its constitution restricts any distribution on dissolution to another non-profit making body, its surplus goes into the square and the nets, and no commercial operator has any interest in it. It is VAT-registered because its bar turnover took it over the <a href="/blog/vat-and-making-tax-digital/vat-threshold-2025-26">£90,000 registration threshold</a>.</p>

<p>Its subscriptions for the season:</p>

<ul>
<li>180 playing members at £120 = <strong>£21,600</strong></li>
<li>60 social members at £45 = <strong>£2,700</strong></li>
</ul>

<p>The £21,600 of playing subscriptions is <strong>exempt</strong>. No VAT is due on it, and it does not count toward the registration threshold.</p>

<p>The £2,700 of social subscriptions is <strong>standard-rated</strong>. Assume, as most clubs do, that the £45 is the price the member pays and is therefore VAT-inclusive. The VAT is then £2,700 at the VAT fraction of 1/6, which is <strong>£450</strong>, leaving £2,250 of net income. If instead the club intends £45 to be a net price, it has to add £9 and charge £54, and the member notices. Stating that assumption explicitly is the difference between a £450 surprise and a priced decision.</p>

<p>Now the part the committee did not budget for. The club has £21,600 of exempt income sitting alongside its taxable bar and social income, so it is partly exempt. The VAT on directly attributable playing costs, the groundsman's supplies, the covers, the club's share of pitch machinery, is <strong>not recoverable</strong>. The VAT on shared costs is recoverable only in the taxable proportion. On a normal year's running costs that irrecoverable slice can easily exceed the £450 of output VAT the club accounts for on the social subscriptions, and on the year the club re-roofs the pavilion it will be several multiples of it.</p>

<h2>Worked example two: the same activity, run for profit</h2>

<p>A commercially owned gym across town charges £45 a month for a membership giving access to the same kind of facilities the cricket club's playing members use, plus a joining fee.</p>

<p>There is no exemption at any point. The operator fails the eligible-body test at the first limb, because it exists to distribute profit to its owners. Every membership, every joining fee, every personal training session and every day pass is standard-rated at <strong>20%</strong>. On £45 VAT-inclusive, £7.50 goes to HMRC each month.</p>

<p>But the operator makes no exempt supplies at all, so <strong>every penny of its input tax is recoverable</strong>: the fit-out, the equipment finance, the rent where the landlord has opted to tax, the utilities, the marketing. On a £400,000 fit-out that is roughly £66,667 of VAT recovered that the cricket club, on an equivalent project, would simply lose.</p>

<p>Same activity, opposite VAT treatment, and the commercially "worse" treatment is often the financially better one. That is the trade-off no club constitution mentions.</p>

<h2>The questions this page deliberately does not answer</h2>

<p>Some membership questions do not have a single answer that can be safely written down for every club, and a guide that invents one is doing damage rather than saving you a phone call. Three sit in that category.</p>

<p><strong>Joining fees and life memberships.</strong> A joining fee is not automatically the same supply as the annual subscription, and a life membership raises a further question about when the supply is treated as made. The treatment turns on the club's own terms: what the payment buys, whether it is refundable, and what rights it confers. Do not assume the subscription treatment carries across, and do not assume it does not. Check the current position on <a href="https://www.gov.uk/guidance/clubs-and-associations-vat-responsibilities-notice-7015" rel="noopener noreferrer">HMRC's clubs and associations guidance</a> against your own rules.</p>

<p><strong>Affiliation fees paid up to a governing body.</strong> Many clubs collect a county or national governing body levy inside the subscription and pass it on. Whether that element is the club's own supply or a disbursement, and how it is treated, depends on the terms on which it is collected and remitted. It is a question worth asking specifically rather than folding into the subscription split by default.</p>

<p><strong>Bar takings and functions.</strong> These are separate commercial supplies, not part of the sporting exemption, and their detailed treatment, including any excise questions on alcohol, belongs to HMRC's own guidance rather than to an accountancy guide on membership fees.</p>

<p>Each of these is a live question at a real club. Treating them as settled because a page on the internet sounded confident is how a club ends up with an assessment covering four years of returns.</p>

<h2>What a committee should actually do this quarter</h2>

<p>The exemption is not a filing position you adopt once. It is a set of facts that has to keep being true, and the evidence for it lives in documents most clubs do not keep tidily. A short list of what to have on file:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>The current constitution</strong>, with the non-distribution clause visible, and a record of when any amendment was adopted.</li>
<li><strong>A written note of each membership class</strong> and what it entitles the member to do. This is the evidence for the split between exempt and standard-rated subscriptions, and without it the split is an assertion.</li>
<li><strong>The apportionment working</strong>, with the reasoning for the method, reviewed when the membership structure changes rather than rolled forward indefinitely.</li>
<li><strong>The partial exemption calculation</strong> for each return period, and the annual adjustment, showing which input tax was treated as directly attributable and which as residual.</li>
<li><strong>A recovery appraisal ahead of any capital project</strong>, done before the contract is signed. On a clubhouse, a surface or a floodlight scheme the irrecoverable VAT is a real line in the funding gap, and funders will ask whether it has been allowed for.</li>
</ul>

<p>England is the default position throughout this page, and the VAT treatment is the same across the United Kingdom, because VAT is not a devolved tax. What can differ by nation is the regulatory and grant environment a club operates in, which sometimes shapes the structure and therefore, indirectly, the eligible-body analysis.</p>

<h2>What people get wrong</h2>

<ul>
<li><strong>Assuming "sport" is the test.</strong> It is not. The eligible body is the test. The activity being unambiguously sport gets you nowhere if the supplier is a commercial operator.</li>
<li><strong>Assuming the non-distribution clause alone is enough.</strong> It is one of four cumulative limbs. The surplus also has to actually go back into the facilities, and the club must be free of commercial influence.</li>
<li><strong>Reading "non-profit making" as "does not make a surplus".</strong> It means the constitution and the destination of the money, not the bottom line in a given year. A profitable club can be non-profit making; a loss-making commercial gym cannot.</li>
<li><strong>Treating a social membership as exempt because the club is exempt.</strong> The exemption follows the member's participation, not the club's letterhead.</li>
<li><strong>Missing that partial exemption blocks input tax.</strong> This is usually a bigger number than the VAT saved on the social subscriptions, and it is invisible until someone totals the irrecoverable column.</li>
<li><strong>Assuming any trade or professional subscription is exempt.</strong> Group 9 is narrower than "any body a business joins", and a bundled subscription may need apportioning even where the body itself qualifies.</li>
<li><strong>Copying another club's apportionment percentage.</strong> The split has to be supportable on your own membership facts. An inherited percentage is the first thing an officer will ask you to justify.</li>
</ul>
