## Asset type + play

Answer-first page on the one motoring rule where the answer is a number rather than a yes or no. The wedge is the **scope** of the 50% block, which is where businesses lose money without noticing: the block bites on everything paid under the leasing agreement, including optional services bundled into the rental and an excess mileage charge that forms part of the leasing supply, but **maintenance supplied and invoiced as a separate supply escapes it entirely**. The practical consequence is that the wording on the lease invoice decides the recovery, so the invoice is worth reading before signing.

Second wedge: the 50% is a private-use proxy, not an apportionment. It does not move because the car is used 90% for business, and it does not go away if the business keeps a mileage log.

## Required structure (H2 skeleton)

1. **First 60 words, the answer.** Where a business leases a car that is available for any private use, only 50% of the VAT on the lease charge can be recovered. The other 50% is blocked to cover private use, and it does not change with the business mileage. Maintenance supplied and invoiced separately from the lease is outside the block and its VAT is recoverable in full.

## BOUNDARY TABLE (mandatory, exact columns)

Columns: **"Blocked, 50% of the VAT is lost"** against **"Recoverable input tax"**. Four trade-anchored row pairs minimum:

| Blocked, 50% of the VAT is lost | Recoverable input tax |
|---|---|
| A letting agent's monthly rental on a leased car the negotiator takes home: 50% blocked | The maintenance package on that same car, supplied and identified separately on the tax invoice: recoverable in full |
| A salon's bundled "all-in" lease where servicing is not separately identified: the whole charge is caught at 50% | The same salon's separate invoice from an independent garage for a service: recoverable in full |
| An excess mileage charge forming part of the leasing supply on a courier firm's car | An excess mileage charge that forms part of a separate supply of maintenance |
| An early-termination charge when a cafe hands a lease car back: caught by the block | A driving school's tuition car leased for driving instruction: a qualifying use, so the 50% block does not bite |
| A van leased by a joiner with a payload of one tonne or more: not a car, so no block at all and the VAT is fully recoverable (this row belongs on the recoverable side; state it plainly) | |

Keep the last row as a full-width note under the table if a pair reads awkwardly.

## Worked examples (two, real figures)

1. A three-year contract hire at £360 a month plus VAT of £72. Show £36 recovered and £36 blocked each month, and the annual figure, and state that the split does not change if the car does 95% business mileage.
2. The same lease where maintenance of £45 plus £9 VAT is invoiced as a separate supply: £36 of the rental VAT recovered plus the full £9 on maintenance. Then the same package sold as one bundled charge, where the whole VAT amount is caught at 50%. The two versions of the same deal are the point of the example.

## HP GAPS (do NOT invent, omit or link gov.uk)

1. **Do NOT quote a specific statutory sub-article number for the 50% restriction.** Stage 1B B §4.3 records it as unconfirmed. Cite Notice 700/64 and art. 7 generally, and nothing more precise.
2. **Hire purchase, lease purchase and personal contract purchase VAT treatment** is not locked in HP and was not verified. Do not set out a rule for them. One hedged sentence that the treatment depends on whether the agreement is a supply of goods or a supply of services, with a link to Notice 700/64, is the ceiling.
3. **Self-drive hire day limits** (broadly under 30 consecutive days and under 90 days in 12 months) appear in Stage 1B B §4.1 from art. 7(2E)/(2F) but are summarised as "broadly". If you state them, attribute them to the Order and hedge with "broadly"; do not present them as a clean test.
4. **Short-term car hire.** The commonly cited 50%-versus-full-recovery treatment for hire of ten days or less was not verified. Omit it.
5. No fee figures, no named experts.
