**Boundary table (MANDATORY). Two columns: "Exempt (no VAT, no recovery)" against "Zero-rated (0% VAT, recovery allowed)".** Minimum four trade-anchored row pairs, and no education row:

| Exempt: no VAT charged, no input tax recovered | Zero-rated: 0% charged, input tax still recovered |
|---|---|
| A funeral director's disposal of remains and the arrangements for it | A baker's loaf of bread sold cold over the counter |
| An undertaker's crematorium fee | A printer's run of 5,000 leaflets |
| A physiotherapist's treatment of a patient (registered practitioner, health purpose) | A children's clothing shop's school-age coats within the measurement limits |
| A sports club's playing membership subscription | A coach operator's 52-seat day trip |
| A landlord's rent on an unopted commercial unit | A bookshop's paperbacks and its ebook versions |

Put the payoff sentence directly under the table: both columns show £0 of VAT on the invoice, and only the right-hand column gets the VAT on its costs back. That is why zero-rating is worth more than exemption.

**The de minimis section. Write it exactly this way, it is the reason the page exists.**

State the full test first: exempt input tax is recoverable in full where it (a) does not exceed **£625 per month on average**, that is **£1,875 a quarter** and **£7,500 a year**, **AND** (b) does not exceed **one half of all input tax for the period**. Both limbs, cumulative. That is regulation 106.

Then state the two simplified tests, and label the trap in a `<strong>` sentence of its own: **the 50% in the simplified tests is a different 50%.** Simplified test one: total input tax no more than £625 a month on average AND the **value of exempt supplies** no more than 50% of the **value of all supplies**. Simplified test two: total input tax less input tax directly attributable to taxable supplies no more than £625 a month on average, and the same exempt-supplies-value limb. Say plainly that regulation 106 compares **exempt input tax against total input tax**, while the simplified tests compare **the value of exempt supplies against the value of all supplies**, and that reading one as the other is the misapplication this section exists to correct. Then note the three tests are alternatives: pass any one and you are de minimis.

**Worked examples (two, named to a trade, figures derived only from the £625 / 50% anchors):**
- A funeral director with £2,400 of total input tax in a quarter, of which £900 is attributable to exempt supplies. £900 is above £1,875? No, it is below. Is it above half of £2,400? No, £1,200 is half. Both limbs pass, so the £900 is recoverable. Show both limbs being tested, in that order.
- The same business the following quarter with £2,400 total input tax and £1,400 exempt input tax. Below £1,875, so limb (a) passes, but £1,400 is more than half of £2,400, so limb (b) fails and the £1,400 is not recoverable. One limb failing is enough. This is the example that teaches the rule.

**What people get wrong:**
- Calling an exempt business "VAT exempt" when they mean not registered. Those are different situations.
- Assuming exempt means better than standard-rated. It is usually worse than zero-rated and sometimes worse than standard-rated, because of the blocked recovery.
- Treating the de minimis limit as a single 50% test.
- Applying the £625 as a per-quarter figure. It is a monthly average, £1,875 a quarter.
- Forgetting the annual adjustment: the tests are applied per period and again over the longer period.
- Assuming exempt turnover counts toward £90,000. It does not, and that is often the most valuable thing on this page for a reader.
