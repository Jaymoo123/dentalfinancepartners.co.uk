# Selling a probate property: the order of operations, and the one decision to make before you list

## Statutory anchor
- Primary: **Administration of Estates Act 1925 s.1(1)**: real estate to which the deceased was entitled for an interest not ceasing on death "shall on his death ... devolve ... on the personal representative of the deceased, in like manner as before the commencement of this Act chattels real devolved on the personal representative"; **s.1(3)** makes the personal representatives the representative of the deceased in regard to that real estate. WebFetch-VERIFIED at https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1925/23/section/1 on 2026-08-21. This is the answer to "who owns the house now": the personal representatives do, from the moment of death, not the beneficiaries.
- Primary: **Administration of Estates Act 1925 s.2** ("Application to real estate of law affecting chattels real"). **s.2(1)** applies the law on probate, letters of administration and administration of personal estate to personal representatives dealing with real estate, giving them the same powers of disposition. **s.2(2)** is the operative conveyancing rule: where there is more than one personal representative, **a conveyance of real estate, or a contract for a conveyance, requires the concurrence of all of them or an order of the court**, except that where probate has been granted to one or some of the executors named, that executor or those executors may convey without a court order, and the conveyance is as effective as if all had concurred. WebFetch-VERIFIED at https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1925/23/section/2 on 2026-08-21 (revised text, amendments from 1 July 1995). This is the answer to "who signs", and it is the reason a missing co-executor stops a sale dead.
- Primary (procedural, per the dispatch instruction to frame procedurally where statute does not carry the point): **gov.uk "Applying for probate"**, VERIFIED 2026-08-21, two sentences quoted verbatim on the page: **"You should not make any financial plans or put property on the market until you've got probate"** and **"You'll normally have to start paying Inheritance Tax before probate is granted."** There is no single statutory section that says "you need a grant before you can sell", because the constraint is practical and registrational rather than a prohibition in the 1925 Act. The page states the position procedurally, on gov.uk's own words, and does **not** invent a statutory bar.
- Primary (tax): **TCGA 1992 s.38(1)(c) + s.38(2)**: exhaustive list of deductible incidental costs of disposal, naming surveyor, valuer, auctioneer, accountant, agent and legal adviser fees, costs of transfer or conveyance including SDLT, and (per CG15250) advertising to find a buyer and the cost of any valuation or apportionment required for the CGT computation. VERIFIED at https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1992/12/section/38 on 2026-08-21.
- Supporting: **HMRC Statement of Practice SP2/04**, the agreed scale of allowable incidental expenditure for personal representatives' **cost of establishing title**, for deaths after 5 April 2004 (superseding SP8/94 and SP7/81). Existence and coverage VERIFIED via **CG30560** at https://www.gov.uk/hmrc-internal-manuals/capital-gains-manual/cg30560 on 2026-08-21; the manual page confirms the agreement with the professional bodies but does **not** reproduce the scale, and the gov.uk Statement of Practice page returned 404 at seed time, so **the scale bands and percentages are a gating Stage 2 verification item and must not be written from memory**. **FA 2019 Sch 2 para 3(1)(b)**: 60-day return for disposals of UK land, "60th" substituted for "30th" by FA 2022 s.23(2); VERIFIED at https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2019/1/schedule/2 on 2026-08-21. Personal representatives are within it (HP §39, HMRC CG30540).

## CGT hook (mandatory, the wave's organising rule)
A probate sale is usually a small-gain disposal and a large-cost one, which is exactly why the costs matter more here than anywhere else in this wave. The estate's base cost is the death value, so the only gain is post-death movement, and against that thin gain the personal representatives can set the full **TCGA 1992 s.38(1)(c)** list: agent's commission, conveyancing, advertising to find a buyer, and the valuation obtained for the tax computation. Personal representatives also have something no ordinary vendor has, an agreed scale under **HMRC SP2/04** for the cost of establishing title, which converts part of the administration expense into allowable expenditure. The costs of selling therefore routinely turn a nominal probate gain into no gain at all, and where they do not, the estate's annual exempt amount and the 24% personal-representative rate decide the bill, with the **60-day return under FA 2019 Sch 2 para 3(1)(b)** running from completion where tax is due. The mechanics behind every one of those sentences already exist on the site and this page links them rather than repeating them: `/blog/capital-gains-tax/cgt-on-inherited-property-uk-probate-base-cost` for the base cost, `/blog/capital-gains-tax/cgt-inherited-rental-property-calculation-uk` for the computation, `/blog/capital-gains-tax/cgt-payment-deadlines-property-sales-2026` for the deadline, and `/blog/capital-gains-tax/capital-gains-tax-property-complete-guide-uk` as the pillar.

What is left, and what nothing on the site covers, is the sequence an executor actually has to run: valuation, grant, marketing, offer, exchange, completion, distribution, and where each of those can be brought forward and where it cannot. The cannibalisation check returned a top Jaccard of 0.12, and the freeze-adjacency rule is what does the real differentiation work here, not the token score.

The accountant's angle is the house differentiator. Consumer coverage of probate sales comes from solicitors and cash-buying firms, and it splits into two unhelpful halves: legal pages about obtaining the grant, and marketing pages offering to buy the house quickly. Neither prices the sale. This page does three things none of them do. First, it states the conveyancing capacity rule from **AEA 1925 s.2(2)** in plain terms, because "all the executors have to sign, unless probate was granted to fewer of the named executors" is the single most common reason a probate sale collapses and almost nobody writes it. Second, it quotes gov.uk's own line about not putting property on the market before the grant and then explains honestly what agents do in practice and what the risk of that is, which is a more useful answer than either repeating the guidance or ignoring it. Third, and this is the decision the whole page is built around: **sell as personal representatives, or assent the property to the beneficiaries and let them sell?** That fork changes who gets an annual exempt amount, which rate applies, who files, and who bears the costs, and it has to be decided **before** the property is listed because it changes whose name is on the contract. Framing it as a pre-listing operational decision rather than a tax computation is the lane, and the computation itself is linked out. Fourth, the Inheritance Tax cash-flow trap is a selling problem, not a tax problem: gov.uk says the tax normally starts being paid before the grant, and the grant is what unlocks the house that would have paid the tax.
