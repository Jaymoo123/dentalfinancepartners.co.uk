<p>The single biggest myth in dental practice buying is that you need a large cash deposit before a lender will talk to you. For a qualifying dentist, that is usually wrong. Specialist lenders routinely advance up to <strong>100% of the goodwill and equipment</strong> value of a practice, and occasionally more once a working-capital tranche is added, meaning a first-time buyer can complete without putting down a deposit against those elements at all. This is not a loophole or a hard-sell product. It reflects the way lenders assess dentistry: a resilient, contract-backed, professionally regulated activity with a very low default record.</p>

<p>This guide answers the question buyers actually ask, "can I really buy with no deposit?", head on. We explain why 100% lending exists, exactly what it covers and what it does not, the criteria you have to clear, the security and personal guarantee that stand in place of a deposit, how much you can borrow against a practice's earnings, and what to do when a lender comes back at 90% instead of 100%. The tax and structuring of the deal (how goodwill is relieved, how the purchase is apportioned) is covered in our sibling guides and summarised here with a link, because this page stays firmly in the lending lane.</p>

<h2>Can you really buy a dental practice with no deposit?</h2>

<p>Yes, in the common case. When people say "100% finance" for a dental practice, they mean the lender advances the full value of the <strong>goodwill and the equipment</strong>, which between them make up the bulk of most practice prices. Because those two elements are fully funded, the buyer does not need a cash deposit against them. That is genuinely different from a general business acquisition, where a lender would typically want 20% to 40% of the price in cash before lending the rest.</p>

<p>Two clarifications matter from the outset. First, this is <strong>business-purpose commercial lending</strong> to a trading dental business, not a residential or regulated mortgage, and the no-deposit feature has nothing to do with personal home lending. Second, "100%" refers to the goodwill and equipment. If you are also buying the <strong>freehold premises</strong>, those are funded through a separate commercial mortgage that is almost never advanced at the full property value, so a freehold purchase does still involve some equity. Our <a href="/blog/practice-finance/dental-practice-commercial-mortgage">dental commercial mortgage guide</a> covers the freehold side; this page is about the goodwill-and-equipment acquisition loan.</p>

<h2>The professional category: why lenders back dentists</h2>

<p>Banks and specialist lenders operate a <strong>professional-category</strong> approach to a small group of regulated occupations, dentists, doctors, vets and a few others, that they consider materially lower risk than the general market. For dentistry, several features drive that view:</p>

<ul>
  <li><strong>Regulated, insurable operators.</strong> Every practising dentist is registered with the <a href="https://www.gdc-uk.org">General Dental Council</a> and carries professional indemnity, so the lender is backing a vetted, accountable individual whose earning capacity is portable.</li>
  <li><strong>Resilient, often contract-backed income.</strong> A large share of UK dental income flows from NHS General Dental Services or Personal Dental Services contracts administered by the <a href="https://www.nhsbsa.nhs.uk">NHS Business Services Authority</a>, giving a predictable, government-underwritten revenue base that private-sector trading businesses rarely match.</li>
  <li><strong>Very low sector default rates.</strong> Practices are sticky (patients rebook, NHS contracts renew) and the historic loss experience on dental lending is small, which is what lets a lender waive the deposit it would demand elsewhere.</li>
  <li><strong>A regulated operating framework.</strong> Every provider must be registered with the <a href="https://www.cqc.org.uk">Care Quality Commission</a>, which gives lenders confidence in the standard and continuity of the operation they are financing.</li>
</ul>

<p>In short, the deposit a general borrower puts down exists to give the lender a loss cushion. For dentistry, the professional category, the regulated operator and the resilient income together provide that comfort instead, so the cash deposit against goodwill and equipment can fall away.</p>

<p>The <strong>goodwill and equipment loan</strong> is the core 100% product. Because some lenders add a working-capital tranche for the first trading months on top, you will occasionally hear of lending "above 100%", which simply means the facility exceeds the goodwill-plus-equipment figure to include an opening cash buffer. The tax treatment of these elements (<a href="https://www.gov.uk/hmrc-internal-manuals/corporate-intangibles-research-and-development-manual/cird44000">goodwill amortisation relief</a> at 6.5% a year on qualifying post-April-2019 purchases, and capital allowances on the equipment) is a structuring matter we keep to a sentence here and cover in the <a href="/blog/buying-a-practice/dental-practice-goodwill-buying-selling">goodwill buying and selling guide</a>. <h2>The criteria: what makes you eligible</h2>

<p>A no-deposit loan is not automatic. Lenders assess the <strong>buyer</strong> and the <strong>practice</strong> together, and both have to hold up.</p>

<h3>On the buyer</h3>
<ul>
  <li><strong>GDC registration</strong> and, usually, at least two years of post-qualification clinical experience. You do not need prior ownership experience, associates are the typical borrowers here.</li>
  <li><strong>Clinical hours and capacity</strong> to run or clinically oversee the practice you are buying.</li>
  <li><strong>A clean credit history.</strong> County court judgments, defaults or a thin file will weaken the case and can trigger a deposit requirement.</li>
  <li><strong>A credible plan</strong> for the practice under your ownership, particularly if you are changing the NHS and private mix.</li>
</ul>

<h3>On the practice</h3>
<ul>
  <li><strong>Verifiable, stable accounts.</strong> Lenders want to see consistent income and a believable goodwill valuation, not a spike in the year before sale.</li>
  <li><strong>A defensible income mix.</strong> NHS contract income (checked against the <a href="https://www.nhsbsa.nhs.uk">NHS BSA</a> position) is viewed favourably for its predictability; a heavily private book is fine but assessed on its retention record.</li>
  <li><strong>Earnings that comfortably cover the loan</strong>, which is the affordability test covered below.</li>
</ul>

<p>Where the buyer profile is thin or the practice's numbers are volatile, the lender does not necessarily refuse. It usually just moves the deposit dial: 100% becomes 90%, or a cash contribution is asked for. The <a href="/dental-guides/practice-purchase-financial-due-diligence">financial due-diligence guide</a> sets out exactly which numbers a lender (and your accountant) will interrogate before an offer is confirmed.</p>

<h2>Security and personal guarantees when there is no deposit</h2>

<p>If you are not putting cash in, the lender needs its security elsewhere. Expect two things:</p>

<ul>
  <li><strong>A charge over the business assets.</strong> A debenture or fixed and floating charge gives the lender a claim over the goodwill, equipment and receivables it has funded.</li>
  <li><strong>A personal guarantee.</strong> As the borrowing director (for a company) or proprietor (for a sole trader), you personally guarantee the debt, so you are liable if the practice cannot repay. Lenders sometimes cap the guarantee at a proportion of the loan, and commonly require life or critical-illness cover to sit alongside it.</li>
</ul>

<p>The personal guarantee is the honest trade-off for a no-deposit loan: the risk the deposit would have carried is transferred to you personally rather than sitting as lost equity. It is standard across professional-category lending and not a red flag in itself. What matters is understanding the extent of the guarantee and, where possible, negotiating a cap. The interest on the borrowing, because it is wholly for the trade, is generally deductible against practice profits under the ordinary <a href="https://www.gov.uk/hmrc-internal-manuals/business-income-manual/bim45650">HMRC rules on interest deductibility</a>, a point your accountant will confirm and one we keep brief here.</p>

<h2>Affordability: how much you can actually borrow</h2>

<p>The headline valuation is not the ceiling. The real limit is <strong>affordability</strong>, and lenders size the loan against the practice's <strong>adjusted EBITDA</strong>: its earnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation, adjusted to deduct a market-rate replacement salary for the owner's clinical work and to strip out one-off or non-trading items. The loan repayments then have to be covered by that adjusted figure with a margin, commonly a <strong>debt-service cover of around 1.25 to 1.5 times</strong>.</p>

    <li><strong>Repayments:</strong> at an illustrative rate the annual loan cost is around £42,000, so adjusted EBITDA covers debt service about <strong>1.4 times</strong>, inside the lender's comfort band.</li>
  </ul>
  <p>This is the profile lenders approve at 100%: a clinically credible associate, a stable practice, and earnings that cover the debt with headroom. Change any one of those (a weaker income record, a keener price relative to earnings) and the cover ratio, not the asking price, is what forces the loan down.</p>

<h2>When 100% is not offered, and how to bridge the gap</h2>

<p>Not every deal lands at 100%. A lender may come back at 90%, or ask for a cash contribution, when the buyer profile is thin, the practice's income is volatile, the price looks high against earnings, or the goodwill valuation is hard to defend. A shortfall does not kill the purchase. The usual routes to bridge it are:</p>

<ul>
  <li><strong>Put in the difference as cash</strong>, if you have the reserve, treating it as equity in your own business.</li>
  <li><strong>Approach a second specialist lender.</strong> Appetite varies, and a bank that offers 90% is rarely the whole market. Another lender may be at 100% for your buyer type this quarter.</li>
  <li><strong>Renegotiate the price.</strong> If the shortfall reflects a stretched valuation, the sensible fix is often a lower price, not more debt.</li>
  <li><strong>Use a vendor deferment.</strong> The seller leaves part of the price outstanding, repaid from future profits, effectively covering the gap the lender will not.</li>
</ul>

<p>Because lender appetite shifts and each specialist prices dentistry differently, a single 90% offer tells you very little about what the market as a whole will do. This is precisely where a broker who places dental deals regularly earns their fee: knowing which lenders are currently at 100% for a first-time associate, a single-site owner, or a group buyer, and packaging the affordability case to get there.</p>
