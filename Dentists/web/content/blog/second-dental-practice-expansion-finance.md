<p>Buying your first dental practice is a first-time-buyer conversation. You are proving you can run a practice at all, and lenders back you largely on the professional category and the target practice's own numbers. Buying your <strong>second</strong> practice is a different conversation entirely. You are no longer an unknown quantity; you are a proven operator with an existing, profitable business that a lender can see, value and, crucially, lend against. The questions change from "can this person run a practice?" to "can this person run two, and does the combined business comfortably carry the combined debt?"</p>

<p>This guide covers how practice number two, and the wider move into a multi-site group, is actually funded. It stays strictly in the lending lane: releasing equity from practice one, standalone acquisition facilities, how lenders assess a two-site borrower, how borrowing sits across a group structure, and how the acquisition itself is funded across goodwill, premises and equipment. The tax and structuring side, group companies, inter-company loans, profit extraction, is summarised and cross-linked, not re-argued here. For the journey as a whole, this page sits under our pillar guide on <a href="/blog/buying-a-practice/how-to-buy-a-dental-practice">how to buy a dental practice</a>.</p>

<h2>Why practice number two is a different funding conversation</h2>

<p>Three things change the moment you own a practice that works. First, you have a <strong>track record</strong>: a set of accounts showing you can generate and sustain profit, which is the single strongest thing a lender can see. Second, you have an <strong>asset with equity in it</strong>, and that equity can be released to help fund the next purchase, something a first-time buyer simply does not have. Third, and this is the new risk lenders start weighing, you have a <strong>capacity question</strong>: running two sites is not twice as easy as running one, and a lender wants to know that practice two will not quietly damage practice one by pulling your attention away.</p>

<p>So the second-practice borrower is stronger on <strong>covenant</strong> and tested harder on <strong>management</strong>. An owner who has already stepped back from full-time chairside work in practice one, delegated day-to-day management, and can show the first site runs without them present, is in a far better position than one whose first practice depends entirely on them being there five days a week. The lending decision starts to look less like a professional-category advance and more like ordinary commercial lending to a growing business, judged on combined performance and the strength of the operator behind it.</p>

<h2>Using the equity in practice one</h2>

<p>The defining feature of second-practice funding is that you have an existing asset to work with. As practice one has grown in value and its acquisition debt has reduced, <strong>equity</strong> has accumulated in it, the gap between what the practice is worth and what you still owe. That equity can be put to work in two broad ways.</p>

<p>The first is to <strong>refinance practice one</strong> and release a lump of equity as cash, which then becomes your contribution towards practice two. Refinancing can also improve the rate or term on the original loan at the same time, so it does double duty. The important lending point, and it has a tax dimension, is that the released money must be used <strong>for the trade</strong> (here, buying another practice) for the interest on it to remain deductible; releasing equity to spend personally breaks that link. We cover the mechanics and the equity-release trap in detail in our guide to <a href="/blog/practice-finance/refinancing-a-dental-practice-loan">refinancing a dental practice loan</a>, and the interest-deductibility rule follows <a href="https://www.gov.uk/hmrc-internal-manuals/business-income-manual/bim45650">HMRC's guidance on the deductibility of interest</a> (the tax follows the use of the money, not the lender).</p>

<p>The second is to leave practice one largely as it is and take a <strong>standalone acquisition facility</strong> for practice two, secured against the second practice itself, with the equity in practice one simply strengthening your overall covenant rather than being cashed out. In reality, most second-practice deals blend the two: some equity released from practice one to reduce the cash you have to find, and a separate acquisition facility for the balance, structured so that the <strong>combined debt-service position</strong> across both sites stays comfortable. Leaning too hard on released equity can over-gear practice one; leaning entirely on a standalone loan can mean finding a larger deposit than you need to. Structuring the two together is where a broker earns their place.</p>

<h2>How lenders assess a two-site borrower</h2>

<p>When you buy practice two, the lender stops looking at a single practice in isolation and starts assessing the <strong>combined business</strong>. Several things come into focus that never mattered on a first purchase.</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>The combined covenant.</strong> The adjusted, normalised earnings of both practices are added together, and the lender tests whether the combined earnings comfortably cover the combined debt. This is measured through <strong>debt-service cover</strong>, the ratio of adjusted earnings to loan repayments, and it is the real ceiling on how much a group can borrow, far more than any headline lending multiple.</li>
<li><strong>Management capacity.</strong> The lender wants evidence that you can oversee two sites. A practice manager in place, associates carrying the clinical load, clear reporting, these all tell a lender that practice two will be managed, not just owned. A borrower who is still the busiest clinician in practice one has a capacity problem the lender can see.</li>
<li><strong>Track record.</strong> Two to three years of accounts for practice one, showing stable or growing profit, does the heavy lifting on credit. This is why the second acquisition is often easier to fund than the first, despite being larger.</li>
<li><strong>Security across the sites.</strong> Goodwill, equipment and any freehold in either practice can potentially be taken as security. A group with a freehold in the mix can usually support more borrowing than one that is entirely leasehold, because there is a tangible asset behind part of the debt.</li>
<li><strong>NHS-backed income.</strong> Where either practice holds an NHS contract, that contracted income is treated as a resilient part of the covenant. Each contract stays with its own practice and does not move automatically on a purchase, so the second acquisition involves the usual novation and provider steps; confirm the position with the <a href="https://www.nhsbsa.nhs.uk/">NHS Business Services Authority</a> and the commissioner.</li>
</ul>

<p>The net effect is that an established, profitable operator is usually a <strong>better credit risk</strong> on the second purchase than they were on the first, provided the capacity question is answered. The lender is backing a proven business, not a hopeful individual.</p>

<h2>A worked two-site funding stack</h2>

<p>The following figures are illustrative, to show how the pieces fit, not a quote or a prediction of terms. Assume an owner of a single practice, <strong>practice one</strong>, now worth around <strong>£600,000</strong> with only £180,000 of the original acquisition loan still outstanding. They want to buy <strong>practice two</strong>, a leasehold practice priced at <strong>£450,000</strong> (goodwill and equipment).</p>

<table>
<tr><th>Step</th><th>Action</th><th>Figure</th></tr>
<tr><td>Practice one value</td><td>Current market value</td><td>£600,000</td></tr>
<tr><td>Debt on practice one</td><td>Remaining acquisition loan</td><td>£180,000</td></tr>
<tr><td>Equity released</td><td>Refinance practice one, release equity for the trade</td><td>£120,000</td></tr>
<tr><td>Practice two price</td><td>Goodwill and equipment (leasehold)</td><td>£450,000</td></tr>
<tr><td>Acquisition facility</td><td>New loan secured on practice two</td><td>£330,000</td></tr>
<tr><td>Total funding for practice two</td><td>Released equity plus acquisition facility</td><td>£450,000</td></tr>
</table>

<p>Here the owner refinances practice one to release <strong>£120,000</strong> of equity (the new practice-one loan rises from £180,000 to £300,000, still comfortably within its £600,000 value), and combines that with a <strong>£330,000</strong> acquisition facility secured on practice two. Practice two is funded in full without the owner finding fresh cash from savings, because the equity in the first practice does the work a cash deposit would have done on a first purchase.</p>

<p>The lender's real test is the <strong>combined debt-service position</strong>: total group debt of £630,000 (£300,000 on practice one plus £330,000 on practice two) against the combined adjusted earnings of both sites. If the two practices together produce adjusted earnings that cover the combined repayments with sensible headroom (lenders typically look for cover comfortably above 1.25 times), the deal works. If the numbers are tight, the answer is usually to release less equity, extend a term, or bring a freehold into the security rather than to abandon the purchase. And because the released equity is used for the trade, the interest on it stays deductible, unlike equity drawn out for personal use.</p>

<h2>Group structure and where the borrowing sits</h2>

<p>Once you own more than one practice, <strong>structure</strong> becomes a live question, and it is primarily a tax and legal decision rather than a lending one. Some owners hold both practices in the <strong>same limited company</strong>. Others set up a <strong>separate company</strong> for practice two. Many, as the group grows, put a <strong>holding company (holdco)</strong> over one or more <strong>trading companies (opcos)</strong>, each running a practice. Each approach has different consequences for tax, liability, borrowing and a future sale, and the right choice depends on your circumstances and plans.</p>

<p>The lending consequence is simply that the structure determines <strong>where the debt and the security sit</strong>. Lenders can generally lend into the holdco or into the individual opcos, and can take security across the group, but they want a clean, well-documented structure so it is obvious which company owns what and which entity is borrowing. Getting the structure right before you complete matters, because unwinding or re-papering it afterwards is expensive and can trigger tax charges. We keep the structuring detail to our dedicated tax guides: <a href="/blog/practice-accounting/inter-company-loans-dividends-dental-groups-uk">inter-company loans and dividends in dental groups</a> covers how money moves between group companies, and <a href="/dental-guides/practice-profit-extraction-partnership-vs-ltd">profit extraction, partnership versus limited company</a> covers the ownership vehicle. Whichever structure you choose, register it correctly at <a href="https://www.gov.uk/government/organisations/companies-house">Companies House</a>, and remember that each practice still needs its own provider registration with the <a href="https://www.cqc.org.uk/">Care Quality Commission</a>.</p>

<h2>Funding the acquisition across goodwill, premises and equipment</h2>

<p>The second purchase is funded across the same three components as the first, but a group borrower usually has more options on each.</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Goodwill.</strong> The intangible value of the practice, typically the largest slice of the price, is funded on the main acquisition facility. Purchased goodwill can attract amortisation relief where it qualifies (the tax detail sits in our goodwill and structuring guides, not here); the <a href="https://www.gov.uk/hmrc-internal-manuals/corporate-intangibles-research-and-development-manual">HMRC Corporate Intangibles manual</a> is the source for the relief rules. As a lending matter, goodwill is funded on a medium-term loan secured against the practice.</li>
<li><strong>Premises.</strong> If practice two is <strong>freehold</strong>, the surgery building is usually funded by a separate <strong>commercial mortgage</strong> on a longer term (commonly fifteen to twenty years), because the security and the sensible repayment period differ from goodwill lending. Our guide to the <a href="/blog/practice-finance/dental-practice-commercial-mortgage">dental practice commercial mortgage</a> covers this product. If practice two is leasehold, this component does not arise.</li>
<li><strong>Equipment.</strong> Chairs, imaging and surgery equipment already in practice two are generally bundled into the acquisition; new or upgraded equipment can be funded separately by asset finance so it does not consume acquisition headroom.</li>
</ul>

<p>An experienced owner buying practice two may also qualify for full <strong>professional-category</strong> funding of the goodwill and equipment, sometimes more readily than a first-time buyer, because the track record supports it. Whether full funding is offered depends on the combined covenant and the security; our page on <a href="/blog/practice-finance/100-percent-dental-practice-finance">100 percent dental practice finance</a> sets out the criteria. As always, affordability across both sites, not the availability of a headline product, decides how much is actually advanced.</p>

<h2>Cross-charging and inter-company funding, in brief</h2>

<p>As a group operates, money moves between the sites: shared central costs, a loan from one company to another, dividends up to a holding company. These <strong>inter-company</strong> flows carry tax consequences and have to be documented and, where relevant, priced at arm's length. This is structuring, not lending, so we do not re-cover it here beyond the lending-relevant point: lenders want to see a <strong>clean, documented flow of money</strong> between the group's companies, which is exactly what sound tax structuring already produces. Loose or informal transfers between your practices make a lender nervous and can create unexpected tax charges. The full treatment is in our guide to <a href="/blog/practice-accounting/inter-company-loans-dividends-dental-groups-uk">inter-company loans and dividends in dental groups</a>, and it is a conversation to have with your accountant before the group takes shape.</p>

<h2>The multi-site growth path, and where a broker and an accountant fit</h2>

<p>Practice two is rarely the end of the story. Owners who make the second acquisition work often go on to a third and beyond, and each step compounds the same logic: a stronger combined covenant supports more borrowing, released equity from the maturing sites funds the next purchase, and the group structure that was set up early pays off as it scales. The constraint that governs the whole path is <strong>management capacity</strong>, not funding. Lenders will keep advancing against a growing, profitable group; what limits sensible growth is whether the operator has built the team and the systems to run more sites without the quality, or the numbers, slipping.</p>

<p>Two advisers matter at each step, and they do different jobs. A <strong>dental commercial-finance broker</strong> structures the money: how much to release from the existing sites, how to split funding across goodwill, premises and equipment, which lenders will take a group covenant, and how to keep the combined debt-service position comfortable. A <strong>specialist dental accountant</strong> structures everything around the money: the group companies, the inter-company flows, the profit extraction, the tax on each acquisition, and the exit plan. Bringing both in early, before you make an offer on practice two, is what turns an ambitious plan into a fundable one.</p>
