<article>

<p>If your pub or licensed club has gaming machines that pay cash prizes, you have a specific tax compliance obligation that operates differently from VAT or payroll: you must register for Machine Games Duty with HMRC <strong>before those machines are made available to play</strong>. Not when you first take income. Before the machines go live. The duty is then charged on net takings at one of three rates, 5%, 20% or 25%, and which rate you pay depends on what a single play costs on the machine.</p>

<p>This page explains what MGD is, which machines trigger it, how the three machine types and their rates work, who is legally responsible, how to register, and how the returns cycle fits alongside your other pub tax commitments. The rates and the type tests below come from <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2012/14/schedule/24">Finance Act 2012 Schedule 24</a> and from HMRC's rates page at <a href="https://www.gov.uk/machine-game-duty/how-much-you-pay">gov.uk/machine-game-duty/how-much-you-pay</a>, with the registration rules at <a href="https://www.gov.uk/guidance/machine-games-duty">gov.uk/guidance/machine-games-duty</a>.</p>

<h2>You must register for MGD before a cash-prize machine takes a penny</h2>

<p>The rule is simple and the sequence matters: registration comes first, operation comes second. <a href="https://www.gov.uk/guidance/machine-games-duty">HMRC's guidance</a> is clear that gaming machines requiring Machine Games Duty must be registered before they are made available to play. An operator who switches on machines and registers later is already non-compliant from the first play.</p>

<p>This matters because HMRC treats MGD as a self-assessment regime. You are expected to know the rule applies, register before playing, and file returns on time. There is no reminder and no grace period built into the framework. Pub operators applying for a premises licence or expanding into gaming machines for the first time should treat MGD registration as a pre-launch item, sitting alongside their licence application, not as an afterthought.</p>

<p>The core test is whether the machine offers cash prizes. If a play on the machine can result in a cash payout, or a prize that can be exchanged for cash, MGD registration is required. The table below sets out the main scenarios.</p>

<p>The boundary that matters is the one set by the exemption: machines where prizes are worth less than the cost of a single play fall outside MGD. This is a precise threshold, not a general "low-value prizes" category. If there is any mechanism that allows a player to receive cash, even as one option among several non-cash outcomes, the machine is likely caught. When in doubt, register and take specific advice rather than assuming exemption.</p>

<h2>The three machine types, and the rate each one pays</h2>

<p>Once a machine is inside the duty, the next question is which rate it pays. There are three, and they run on net takings: <strong>5% for a type 1 machine, 20% for a type 2 machine and 25% for all other machines</strong>. The dividing line is the cost of a play, not the takings of the site and not the size of the estate. A single machine in a village local and a bank of machines in a city-centre bar are rated by exactly the same test.</p>

<h3>Type 1, the lower rate, 5%</h3>

<p>A machine is type 1 where, in the words of <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2012/14/schedule/24">Finance Act 2012 Schedule 24 paragraph 5(2)</a>, "the highest charge payable for playing a dutiable machine game on the machine does not exceed 20p, and the maximum amount of cash that can be won from playing a dutiable machine game on the machine does not exceed £10".</p>

<p>Both limbs must be satisfied. A 10p-a-play machine with a £25 cash jackpot is not type 1: the cost limb passes but the prize limb fails. A 50p-a-play machine with a £5 top prize is not type 1 either. Only a machine that is cheap to play <strong>and</strong> capped at a modest cash prize qualifies for the 5% rate. The limbs are tested against the highest charge and the maximum win available on the machine, not against a typical play or an average payout, so the dearest game on the cabinet and the biggest prize it can pay are what count.</p>

<h3>Type 2, the standard rate, 20%</h3>

<p>A machine is type 2 where, under <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2012/14/schedule/24">Schedule 24 paragraph 5(3)</a>, "it is not a type 1 machine, and it can be demonstrated that the highest charge payable for playing a dutiable machine game on the machine does not exceed £5". Type 2 pays the standard rate of 20%.</p>

<p>Two points follow and both are routinely missed. First, type 2 is defined negatively: a machine only reaches type 2 by failing type 1. That is why a 10p-a-play machine with a £50 jackpot lands on 20% rather than 5%, even though it is one of the cheapest machines on the floor. Second, the burden is on the operator: the cost of play test must be something that "can be demonstrated", so your records need to show the highest charge payable on every game the cabinet offers.</p>

<h3>All other machines, the higher rate, 25%</h3>

<p>Everything that is neither type 1 nor type 2 pays the higher rate of 25%. In practice that means a machine where the highest charge payable for a play exceeds £5. GOV.UK's rates table labels this group "all other machine types", and it is the residual category the statute leaves behind after the two defined types.</p>

<p>You will see this group called "type 3" in trade press and supplier paperwork. It is useful shorthand and it is not a statutory label. <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2012/14/schedule/24">Schedule 24</a> defines type 1 and type 2 only and reaches the rest as a residual, so a contract referencing "type 3 machines as defined in Schedule 24" is citing a definition that does not exist in those words. The rate is real; the label is informal.</p>

It is a presentational simplification of the fact that a machine paying £10 or less at 20p or less would already be type 1. The statutory type 2 test in paragraph 5(3) is about the cost of play only, plus failing type 1. For a borderline machine, follow the statute rather than the table.</p>

<h2>What happens when one machine offers more than one type of game</h2>

<p>This is the single most expensive thing an operator can get wrong, and almost nothing written about MGD leads on it. Where a machine offers more than one type of game, the highest applicable rate applies to <strong>all</strong> takings from that machine. HMRC states it without qualification: <a href="https://www.gov.uk/machine-game-duty/how-much-you-pay">"If your machine has more than one type of game, you pay the rate for the highest rated game on all takings from the machine."</a></p>

<p>The rate is a property of the cabinet, not of the individual game. So a machine that has run happily at 5% for two years, offering nothing but a 20p game with a £10 top prize, ceases to be a type 1 machine the moment a £1 game is enabled on it. From that point the whole machine is type 2 and every penny of its net takings is charged at 20%, including the takings from the original 20p game that would on its own still qualify for 5%.</p>

<p>That mechanic turns a routine software update into a tax event. Suppliers push game packs remotely, and a pack that adds a higher-stake title re-rates the machine without anyone at the pub touching it. The operator carries the liability and the operator is the one HMRC asks. The practical control is a written instruction that no game may be enabled on any cabinet without your prior agreement, plus a machine-by-machine record of the highest charge payable and the maximum cash prize after every visit or update. The same logic runs the other way: if you want a cabinet on the 5% rate, it must offer nothing but games inside both type 1 limbs.</p>

<h2>What MGD is charged on: net takings</h2>

<p>MGD is charged on net takings from dutiable machine games, not on the gross cash that goes into the machine. Net takings are the amounts paid in to play dutiable machine games less the value of prizes paid out, taken across the accounting period. The rate is then applied to that net figure.</p>

<p>The arithmetic is straightforward once the inputs are right:</p>

<ul>
  <li><strong>Cash in.</strong> Everything staked on dutiable machine games during the period, taken from the machine's meters rather than from the cash bag, because the bag has been emptied and refilled and may include float.</li>
  <li><strong>Prizes out.</strong> Cash and cash-equivalent prizes paid out on those games during the same period, again from the meters and from the supplier's collection records.</li>
  <li><strong>Net takings.</strong> Cash in less prizes out. This is the dutiable amount.</li>
  <li><strong>Duty.</strong> Net takings multiplied by 5%, 20% or 25%, depending on the type of the machine the takings came from.</li>
</ul>

<p>Because the rate attaches to the machine, net takings have to be attributed machine by machine before the rate is applied. An operator with a 5% cabinet and a 20% cabinet in the same bar cannot net them into one figure and apply an average.</p>

<p>The fourth pair is the one that costs money. Nothing about the social club cabinet looks different from the pub's type 1 fruit machine, and the 20p game on it is identical. The £2 game is what moves every penny of that cabinet's net takings from 5% to 20%.</p>

<h2>Worked examples</h2>

<p>All three examples run over a single accounting period and use net takings, meaning cash in less prizes paid out, read from the machine meters and reconciled to the supplier's collection records.</p>

<h3>Example 1: a pub with one type 1 fruit machine</h3>

<p>A village pub runs a single fruit machine at 20p a play with a £10 maximum cash prize. Over the period the meters show £3,600 played in and £2,400 paid out in prizes.</p>

<ul>
  <li>Cash in: £3,600</li>
  <li>Prizes out: £2,400</li>
  <li>Net takings: £3,600 less £2,400 = £1,200</li>
  <li>Machine type: type 1, because the highest charge payable is 20p (not exceeding 20p) and the maximum cash win is £10 (not exceeding £10). Both limbs of <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2012/14/schedule/24">Schedule 24 paragraph 5(2)</a> are satisfied.</li>
  <li>Duty: £1,200 at 5% = <strong>£60</strong></li>
</ul>

<h3>Example 2: the same pub's second machine, a type 2</h3>

<p>The same pub adds a second, separate cabinet at £1 a play with a £100 cash jackpot. Over the period that machine takes £8,000 in and pays £5,500 out.</p>

<ul>
  <li>Cash in: £8,000</li>
  <li>Prizes out: £5,500</li>
  <li>Net takings: £8,000 less £5,500 = £2,500</li>
  <li>Machine type: type 2. It fails type 1 on both limbs (£1 exceeds 20p, £100 exceeds £10), and the highest charge payable of £1 does not exceed £5, which is the paragraph 5(3) test.</li>
  <li>Duty: £2,500 at 20% = <strong>£500</strong></li>
</ul>

<p>Note that the two machines are accounted for separately and then added together on the return. The pub's duty for the period is £60 plus £500, which is £560. There is no blended rate and no netting of one machine's takings against the other's.</p>

<h3>Example 3: the trap, adding one game to a type 1 cabinet</h3>

<p>At the supplier's suggestion the pub agrees to have a £2-a-play game enabled on the first cabinet, the 20p fruit machine, to widen its appeal. Nothing else changes. The 20p game still runs, the £10 prize cap on it is unchanged, and net takings on the cabinet are the same £1,200 as before.</p>

<ul>
  <li>The cabinet now offers a game whose charge payable is £2, so the highest charge payable on the machine is £2, which exceeds 20p. The machine is no longer type 1.</li>
  <li>£2 does not exceed £5, so the machine is type 2 and the standard rate of 20% applies.</li>
  <li>Because the machine has more than one type of game, the rate for the highest rated game applies to <strong>all</strong> takings from the machine, not just to the £2 game's share.</li>
  <li>Duty: £1,200 at 20% = <strong>£240</strong>, against £60 before. The same cabinet, the same net takings, four times the duty.</li>
</ul>

<p>This is why the multi-game rule belongs in your machine contract. A game pack that adds £15 a week of takings and quadruples the duty on the cabinet is a bad trade, and it is one operators usually make without being shown the numbers.</p>

<h2>What people get wrong</h2>

<ul>
  <li><strong>"One game on the machine is cheap, so the machine is type 1."</strong> The test looks at the highest charge payable on the machine and the maximum cash that can be won on it. If any game on the cabinet breaches either type 1 limb, the machine is not type 1, and the highest applicable rate applies to all of that machine's takings.</li>
  <li><strong>"The prize value decides whether I pay 20%."</strong> It does not. The statutory type 2 test in paragraph 5(3) is about the cost of play, not exceeding £5, plus failing type 1. The prize column on the GOV.UK rates table is a presentational simplification, and the statute governs a borderline case.</li>
  <li><strong>"I pay duty on the cash in the machine."</strong> You pay on net takings, meaning cash in less prizes paid out. Paying on gross would overstate the duty substantially on a machine with a normal payout percentage.</li>
  <li><strong>"Type 3 is a statutory category."</strong> It is not. Schedule 24 defines type 1 and type 2, and everything else is reached as the residual higher-rate group at 25%. Use the label if it is convenient, but do not cite it as a statutory definition.</li>
  <li><strong>"I can register once the machine is earning."</strong> Registration must be in place before the machine is made available to play. That is the point on which operators are most often found non-compliant, and it is not curable after the fact.</li>
  <li><strong>"Free-replay quiz machines are caught."</strong> Where the prize is less than the cost to play, the machine is not a dutiable machine game and no registration is required. A quiz machine that pays a cash jackpot is a different matter entirely.</li>
  <li><strong>"The supplier handles the duty, it comes out of the split."</strong> The commercial split is a private contract. The duty obligation sits with the licence holder, and HMRC will pursue the licence holder for it.</li>
  <li><strong>"MGD and the gaming machine permit are the same regime."</strong> They are not. MGD is a tax administered by HMRC; the permit or licence allowing you to site gaming machines at all runs under the gambling regime and is administered separately. Holding one does not discharge the other.</li>
</ul>

<h2>Who is liable: the operator holding the qualifying licence</h2>

<p>Machine Games Duty falls on the operator holding the qualifying gambling or alcohol licence. Not the machine supplier. Not the leasing company. Not the engineer who installed the cabinet.</p>

<p>This matters because many pubs operate gaming machines under profit-share or commission arrangements with an external supplier, who may handle installation, maintenance, and cash collection. Those commercial arrangements are private contracts and are irrelevant to HMRC. From HMRC's perspective, the compliance obligation sits with the person or business that holds the premises licence or the qualifying gambling authorisation under the Gambling Act 2005.</p>

<p>If you hold the licence and machines are on your premises, the MGD obligation is yours. Any contract with a machine supplier should address who bears the duty commercially, but that contract cannot transfer the legal compliance responsibility. Given the multi-game rule above, that contract should also say who decides what games run on the cabinet, because in a profit-share arrangement the party choosing the games is frequently not the party paying the duty.</p>

<h2>How to register for MGD</h2>

<p>Registration is completed through HMRC's online services. You will need your business details and the reference for your qualifying licence. The full process is set out at <a href="https://www.gov.uk/guidance/machine-games-duty">gov.uk/guidance/machine-games-duty</a>.</p>

<p>Operators applying for a new premises licence should plan to complete MGD registration as part of the pre-opening checklist, not as a separate task after the pub opens. The timing constraint is absolute: the machines must not go live before registration is complete.</p>

<p>If you are taking over an existing pub that already has gaming machines in situ, check whether the previous operator's MGD registration transfers or whether a new registration is required in your name. A change of licence holder typically requires fresh registration. While you are doing that, record the highest charge payable and the maximum cash prize on every cabinet you are inheriting. The outgoing operator's rate may have been right for the settings on the day they registered and wrong for the settings you are taking on.</p>

<h2>MGD returns: the reporting cycle and what you report</h2>

<p>Once registered, operators must file MGD returns and pay the duty on machine takings. The mechanism works as follows: the operator reports the net takings from qualifying machines, applies the duty rate that attaches to the machine those takings came from, and pays the resulting amount to HMRC.</p>

<p>The return filing frequency and the accounting periods that go with it are set by HMRC and are published at <a href="https://www.gov.uk/guidance/machine-games-duty">HMRC's Machine Games Duty guidance</a>. The rates themselves are on the separate rates page at <a href="https://www.gov.uk/machine-game-duty/how-much-you-pay">gov.uk/machine-game-duty/how-much-you-pay</a>, and are the 5%, 20% and 25% figures set out above.</p>

<p>What you need to record for each return period:</p>

<ul>
  <li>Total cash in (money played into the machines), by machine</li>
  <li>Total cash out (prizes paid out), by machine</li>
  <li>Net takings (the difference, which is the dutiable amount), by machine</li>
  <li>The type of each machine, and the highest charge payable and maximum cash prize that put it in that type</li>
  <li>Any change to the games available on a machine during the period, and the date it took effect</li>
  <li>Machine-level records if HMRC requires them for a compliance check</li>
</ul>

<p>The practical implication: pub operators need their machine supplier or till system to provide accurate takings data for every return period, split by machine. If you rely on a supplier to collect cash from the machines, agree in writing what data they will provide and when, so you can file on time. Add the game configuration to that list. A takings report that does not tell you what was playable on the cabinet leaves you unable to evidence the rate you applied.</p>

<h2>Record-keeping and how MGD sits alongside your other pub taxes</h2>

<p>MGD returns add a standing compliance obligation alongside the duties, filings and declarations already running for a licensed trade business. Specifically:</p>

<ul>
  <li><strong>VAT:</strong> machine income may or may not be exempt depending on the machine type. Cash-prize gaming machines are generally exempt from VAT, which means the income does not increase your VAT-taxable turnover but you also cannot recover input VAT on machine-related costs. If you are close to the <a href="https://www.gov.uk/vat-registration/when-to-register">£90,000 VAT registration threshold</a>, check whether machine income counts toward that threshold before assuming it does not push you over.</li>
  <li><strong>Excise duties:</strong> MGD is one of several duties a licensed operator meets. The other big one is <a href="/blog/licensed-trade/alcohol-duty">alcohol duty</a>, which works on a completely different basis (strength and volume of the product, rather than a percentage of takings) and uprates on its own calendar. If you also serve cask or keg products, your <a href="/blog/licensed-trade/draught-relief-explained">draught relief position</a> runs as a separate obligation alongside MGD.</li>
  <li><strong>AWRS due diligence:</strong> buying alcohol from unapproved wholesalers is a separate risk area. See our guide to <a href="/blog/licensed-trade/awrs-checks">AWRS checks for pub operators</a>.</li>
  <li><strong>Corporation tax or income tax:</strong> machine net takings form part of trading profit and are taxed accordingly through your annual return. The MGD paid is a cost of that income.</li>
  <li><strong>Revenue mix and margin:</strong> machine income behaves nothing like wet or dry sales, because the duty comes off the top at a rate you do not control. Treat it as its own line when you look at <a href="/blog/hospitality-accounts/gross-profit-menu-pricing">gross profit across the revenue mix</a>, rather than folding it into other income.</li>
</ul>

<p>HMRC can request records going back several years in a compliance check. Keep machine-level data, supplier statements, game configuration records and copies of all MGD returns for at least the standard six-year retention window. For MGD purposes that means contemporaneous records, not reconstructed from memory.</p>

<p>If your pub is also approaching the threshold for Making Tax Digital for Income Tax (which applies to sole-trader operators above £50,000 from 6 April 2026 under <a href="https://www.gov.uk/guidance/sign-up-your-client-for-making-tax-digital-for-income-tax">HP 24</a>), machine income forms part of the turnover that triggers MTD obligations. More on that in our <a href="/blog/making-tax-digital/mtd-itsa-hospitality-sole-traders">MTD for sole-trader operators guide</a>.</p>

<h2>Getting your gaming-machine compliance handled</h2>

<p>MGD registration and returns are a recurring commitment, not a one-off. The risk is not that the rules are complicated (they are not), but that the filing cycle gets missed because it sits outside the familiar VAT and payroll rhythms, and that a cabinet quietly changes type until a compliance check finds three years of takings charged at the wrong rate.</p>

<p>Across more than one site the data-collection challenge grows: machine-level records, per-machine rate determinations, supplier reconciliation, and separate return cycles for each registered business. That work sits alongside payroll for bar and kitchen staff, alcohol duty management, and the <a href="/services/hospitality-vat">VAT scheme decisions</a> that apply to a wet-led business.</p>

<p>If you are opening a new pub, taking over an existing site with machines already in place, or expanding from one machine to several, the time to address MGD is before the machines go live, not after HMRC writes to you. See our <a href="/for/pubs-and-bars">pub and bar accounting hub</a> for the full picture of what licensed-trade compliance looks like across tax, duties and payroll.</p>
