<h2>VAT on food: the short answer</h2>

<p><strong>Most food for human consumption is zero-rated. It becomes standard-rated at 20% in only three situations: it is catering, meaning it is consumed on the premises; it is hot food taken away; or it falls into one of the standing excepted categories such as confectionery, crisps and savoury snacks, soft drinks and alcohol.</strong> Everything else on this page is the detail of those three exceptions. The rules come from <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1994/23/schedule/8/2026-04-06">VATA 1994 Schedule 8 Group 1</a>, explained by <a href="https://www.gov.uk/guidance/catering-takeaway-food-and-vat-notice-7091">VAT Notice 709/1</a> and <a href="https://www.gov.uk/guidance/food-products-and-vat-notice-70114">VAT Notice 701/14</a>.</p>

<p>The practical consequence for most food businesses is that a mixed menu will carry both rates, and your till or EPOS system needs to apply them correctly at line level. Getting this wrong is one of the most common VAT errors HMRC identifies in hospitality inspections. It is equally a retail problem: a bakery, a butcher, a forecourt shop and a supermarket deli all sit on the same boundary as a takeaway does.</p>

<h2>The rule in law: Schedule 8 Group 1 and the catering exclusion</h2>

<p><strong>Food of a kind used for human consumption is zero-rated by <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1994/23/schedule/8/2026-04-06">VATA 1994 Schedule 8 Group 1</a>. Two things pull a supply back out of zero-rating. The excepted items list removes named categories (ice cream, confectionery, alcoholic and other beverages, savoury snacks, pet food) from relief entirely. Note 3 removes catering, which it defines as any supply of food for consumption on the premises on which it is supplied, and any supply of hot food for consumption off those premises.</strong> Where a supply is standard-rated, the rate itself is 20% under <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1994/23/section/2">VATA 1994 section 2(1)</a>.</p>

<p>Three further Notes do the work that causes most of the arguments:</p>

<ul>
  <li><strong>Note 3A defines "premises".</strong> Premises include any area set aside for the consumption of food by that supplier's customers. HMRC puts it slightly wider in <a href="https://www.gov.uk/guidance/catering-takeaway-food-and-vat-notice-7091">Notice 709/1</a>: the areas occupied by the food retailer, or any area set aside for the consumption of food by the food retailer's customers, whether or not that area may also be used by the customers of other food retailers. Shared seating is caught.</li>
  <li><strong>Note 3C defines "hot".</strong> Food is hot where it is above ambient air temperature. That is a physical test, not a marketing test.</li>
  <li><strong>Note 3B sets the five conditions.</strong> Hot food taken away is standard-rated where it has been heated for the purpose of enabling it to be consumed hot, heated to order, kept hot after being heated, provided in packaging that retains heat or is specifically designed for hot food, or advertised or marketed in a way that indicates it is supplied hot.</li>
</ul>

<p>Citing the statute rather than only the notice matters when you are defending a position, because it is the wording of Schedule 8 that a tribunal applies.</p>

<h3>The ambient gateway, which almost every summary drops</h3>

<p><strong>The five conditions are not free-standing. Note 3B only engages once the food is hot within the meaning of Note 3C, which is to say above ambient air temperature at the time it is provided to the customer. Only then do the five conditions bite.</strong> Writing "any food marketed as hot is standard-rated" without the ambient gateway is simply wrong, and it is the single most repeated error in online summaries of this area.</p>

<p>The effect runs in the operator's favour more often than people expect. A product carrying the word "hot" on the label but sold cold from an ambient shelf has not passed the gateway, so the marketing condition never comes into play. Conversely, once the food is genuinely above ambient when handed over, a single condition is enough.</p>

<p>Note also that the old single hot-food test is obsolete. Finance Act 2012 inserted Notes 3A to 3C and replaced it, so sources still quoting a single test are out of date.</p>

<h2>VAT on hot food: the five hot-food tests</h2>

<p><strong>Food is "hot" for VAT purposes if it is above ambient air temperature when provided to the customer and it meets any one of five conditions set out in Note 3B and restated in <a href="https://www.gov.uk/guidance/catering-takeaway-food-and-vat-notice-7091">VAT Notice 709/1</a>. Meeting a single condition is enough to make the supply standard-rated. The five are: the food was intentionally heated for consumption; it was heated to order; it is kept hot after cooking (for example in a heated cabinet or bain-marie); it is supplied in heat-retentive packaging; or it is advertised or marketed as a hot supply.</strong> If the food fails all five, it may be zero-rated (subject to the excepted items and the eat-in rule).</p>

<h3>The freshly baked exception</h3>

<p>Freshly baked goods that are incidentally warm at the point of sale, but that satisfy none of the five tests, may remain zero-rated. A loaf of bread still warm from the oven but not intentionally heated for consumption, not held in a heat-retentive environment and not marketed as hot can be sold at zero rate. The moment any test is met, that protection falls away. This distinction is particularly important for bakery cafes and artisan bread stalls.</p>

<h3>Why this matters in practice</h3>

<p>The five-test structure means the VAT rate can depend on your process, not just your product. Two businesses selling the same item can have different VAT liabilities if one holds it in a heated cabinet and the other does not. Your procedures, your packaging choices and your menu wording all have VAT consequences. See our <a href="/services/hospitality-vat">VAT returns and schemes service</a> for a review of how your processes map to these tests.</p>

<h2>Eat-in vs takeaway: anything consumed on the premises is standard-rated</h2>

<p><strong>Under <a href="https://www.gov.uk/guidance/catering-takeaway-food-and-vat-notice-7091">VAT Notice 709/1</a>, anything consumed on the supplier's premises is catering and standard-rated at 20%, regardless of whether the food is hot or cold. This includes food courts, areas with designated seating and any space you make available for customers to eat. A business selling cold sandwiches with a seating area must charge standard rate on eat-in sales, even though the identical sandwich in a bag to take away is zero-rated.</strong></p>

<p>The on-premises rule operates independently of the hot-food tests. A cold item that fails all five tests and would otherwise be zero-rated becomes standard-rated the moment a customer eats it at a table or counter you provide. This is why the eat-in/takeaway split at the point of sale is a mandatory operational step, not just a courtesy question to the customer.</p>

<h3>What counts as "on the premises"?</h3>

<p>HMRC's position covers more than just dedicated restaurant seating. Shared food-court tables in a shopping centre, outdoor seating you control, picnic benches outside your premises, and standing ledges you provide all count. The key question is whether you are making the space available for consumption. If a customer eats while standing at your counter, that is also on-premises.</p>

<h3>Splitting the rate correctly</h3>

<p>For businesses with both eat-in and takeaway sales, HMRC requires a genuine split. The method must reflect actual sales, not an estimate. Till-level prompts ("eat in or take away?") are the standard approach. Businesses operating both channels need EPOS configuration that captures the eat-in/takeaway decision per transaction and applies the correct rate automatically. A business that applies zero rate across all sales because it "mainly does takeaway" is taking a material compliance risk.</p>

<h2>VAT on cold food: zero-rated by default, with two ways to lose it</h2>

<p><strong>Cold food for human consumption, sold to be taken away, is zero-rated. There are exactly two ways a cold supply loses that treatment. It is eaten on the premises, which makes it catering under Note 3(a). Or it is one of the excepted items, which never had zero-rating in the first place. Temperature alone never turns a cold supply into a standard-rated one.</strong></p>

<p>Put another way, VAT on takeaway food is a question about the five hot-food conditions and the excepted items, and nothing else. That sounds obvious until you look at how often the reasoning is reversed on the shop floor. Staff are trained to think in terms of hot and cold, so a cold item eaten at a table gets rung through at zero rate because "it wasn't hot". The rate was never about the temperature in that transaction. It was about the table.</p>

<h2>VAT on supermarket food UK-wide, and other retail counters</h2>

<p><strong>A supermarket's ordinary grocery shop is almost entirely zero-rated, because groceries are food of a kind used for human consumption and the customer takes them away. VAT at 20% appears in three places on the same receipt: the excepted items (confectionery, crisps and savoury snacks, soft drinks, alcohol and ice cream), anything from a hot counter that passes the ambient gateway and meets a Note 3B condition, and anything the customer eats at seating the store sets aside.</strong></p>

<p>The third of those is the one almost everyone gets wrong, and it is worth stating plainly. A supermarket's cold meal-deal sandwich taken away is zero-rated. The same sandwich eaten at the store's own seating, or at shared food-court seating the supplier sets aside, is standard-rated by Note 3(a). <strong>Cold food eaten in is standard-rated because of the premises test, not because of temperature.</strong> No hot-food rule is involved in that outcome at all, which is why looking for one produces the wrong answer.</p>

<p>The same structure applies to every retail counter that has quietly acquired seating: a forecourt shop with two stools by the window, a delicatessen with a shelf and a bar stool, a bakery with a bench outside its own door. Note 3A is about area set aside for consumption by that supplier's customers, so the retailer's intention in putting the furniture there is what matters, not how often it is used.</p>

<p>Shared seating deserves a specific warning. Food-court operators often assume that seating owned by the landlord and used by everyone cannot be their premises. <a href="https://www.gov.uk/guidance/catering-takeaway-food-and-vat-notice-7091">Notice 709/1</a> closes that door: the area counts whether or not it may also be used by the customers of other food retailers. If your unit fronts onto a food court, configure your till to ask.</p>

<h2>Is there VAT on food in restaurants?</h2>

<p><strong>Yes, on all of it. UK VAT on restaurant food is 20% across the menu, because every dish served for consumption at the table is catering under Note 3(a). The starter, the main, the side, the dessert and the coffee all carry the same rate.</strong> There is no cold-dish exception, no bread-basket exception and no children's-menu exception.</p>

<p>Restaurants that also run a collection or delivery arm are the ones with a genuine decision to make. Once the food leaves the premises, the on-premises rule stops applying and the hot-food tests take over. A hot main course collected at the counter is still standard-rated, because it is hot food for consumption off the premises. A cold dessert or a cold salad sold to take away can be zero-rated if it meets none of the five conditions and is not an excepted item. Most restaurant takeaway menus are overwhelmingly hot, which is why the practical answer is usually "20% either way", but the analysis still has to be done rather than assumed.</p>

<h2>Is there VAT on pub food?</h2>

<p><strong>Yes, on everything eaten at the pub. A Sunday roast, a cold ploughman's, a bowl of chips and a packet of crisps served to a table are all standard-rated at 20%, the first three as catering and the crisps as an excepted item. Alcohol is standard-rated in every context, whether it is drunk at the bar, taken into the garden or sold in a sealed bottle to take home.</strong></p>

<p>A pub selling genuine takeaway food is in the same position as a takeaway. Cold food taken off the premises can be zero-rated if it meets none of the five hot-food conditions. In practice the wet-led side of the business is standard-rated in full, so the zero-rated fraction of a pub's turnover is usually small and usually confined to cold food collected to go.</p>

These are fixed categories and there is no exception based on size, packaging or whether they are sold as part of a meal deal.</p>

<h3>Plain biscuits vs chocolate-covered biscuits</h3>

<p>Plain biscuits are zero-rated. Chocolate-covered biscuits fall into the confectionery carve-out and are standard-rated. This distinction trips up a number of cafe and coffee-shop operators selling individually wrapped biscuits alongside hot drinks. If your packaging identifies the product as chocolate-covered, it is standard-rated regardless of how small the portion is.</p>

<p>The borderline cases in this area (cake against biscuit, the chocolate-covered borderlines, flavoured milks) turn on the characteristics of the individual product and have been litigated repeatedly. We do not publish a verdict on a named product from memory. Check the item against <a href="https://www.gov.uk/guidance/food-products-and-vat-notice-70114">Notice 701/14</a>, run it through the <a href="/calculators/food-drink-vat-rate-checker">food and drink VAT checker</a>, and take advice before you change the rate on a material line.</p>

<h3>Coffee: the most common query</h3>

<p>Hot drinks heated to order meet the second hot-food test and are standard-rated. This applies to espresso, filter coffee, hot chocolate, tea and any other hot beverage whether or not they contain dairy. Iced coffee and cold-brew products are more complex: plain cold-brew coffee with no added sugar or flavouring may be zero-rated, but many iced coffee preparations include syrups, flavourings or sweeteners that push them into the soft-drinks carve-out. If you sell iced coffee with flavouring or added sugar, treat it as standard-rated unless you have confirmed otherwise. Do not state a specific VAT treatment for a named branded product; use the checker tool.</p>

<h3>Hot pastries and sausage rolls</h3>

<p>A sausage roll or pasty is standard-rated if it is above ambient when handed over and meets any of the five conditions. The clearest case is a product sold directly from an oven or heated display cabinet: it has been intentionally heated for consumption and is being kept hot, so two conditions are met. The same product cooled to ambient temperature and held in a standard (unheated) display case may be zero-rated if it is also not marketed as hot and not supplied in heat-retentive packaging. The key operational question is what your process looks like at the point of sale. For businesses operating both a heated cabinet and an ambient display, the process needs to be consistent and documented, because HMRC can ask for evidence.</p>

<h2>Delivery and third-party apps: the underlying supply still decides the rate</h2>

<p><strong>Delivering food, or selling through a third-party aggregator such as Deliveroo, Uber Eats or Just Eat, does not change the VAT rate that applies to the food. The rate follows the underlying supply. Hot food delivered by a rider is still standard-rated at 20%. Cold food that would otherwise be zero-rated remains zero-rated. <a href="https://www.gov.uk/guidance/catering-takeaway-food-and-vat-notice-7091">VAT Notice 709/1</a> sets the rules on the food; the delivery channel is not a separate factor in the VAT liability of the food itself.</strong></p>

<p>Operators using aggregator platforms should note that the platform's own commission or service fee is a separate supply by the platform to you, typically standard-rated. Your food supplies to the end customer carry the rate determined by the hot/cold and carve-out rules. These are two distinct supplies with distinct VAT treatments.</p>

<h3>Packaging for delivery</h3>

<p>Heat-retentive packaging designed to keep food hot during delivery is one of the five hot-food tests. If you use insulated bags or containers specifically intended to retain heat, the food in them is standard-rated on that basis alone, even if you might have argued it had cooled slightly by the point of handover. The test is whether the packaging is heat-retentive by design, not whether the food is measurably hot when it arrives.</p>

<h3>Marketplace vs agent models</h3>

<p>Some aggregators operate as your agent (you make the supply to the customer and the app collects payment on your behalf). Others operate as principal (the app makes the supply to the customer and buys from you). The VAT treatment of your supplies can differ depending on the contractual model. If you are unsure whether your platform acts as agent or principal, review the platform's terms or speak to your accountant. This affects both your output VAT and how the platform's commission is treated. See our <a href="/services/hospitality-vat">VAT returns and schemes service</a> for guidance on this in the context of your accounts.</p>

<h2>What people get wrong</h2>

<p>These are the positions HMRC actually challenges on inspection. Each one is a reasoning error rather than an arithmetic one, which is why they survive for years inside otherwise well-run businesses.</p>

<ul>
  <li><strong>"Cold food eaten in is zero-rated because it is not hot."</strong> No. Cold food eaten in is standard-rated by the premises test in Note 3(a). Temperature never enters it. This is the single most expensive error on the list, because it usually applies to a whole sales channel rather than one product.</li>
  <li><strong>"Any food marketed as hot is standard-rated."</strong> Only if it is above ambient air temperature when provided to the customer. The five conditions do not bite until that gateway is passed, so a product labelled "hot and spicy" and sold from a chilled shelf is not caught by the marketing condition.</li>
  <li><strong>"Shared food-court seating is not my premises."</strong> The premises test is statutory, not just HMRC practice, and it captures any area set aside for the consumption of food by that supplier's customers, whether or not other retailers' customers use it too.</li>
  <li><strong>"Freshly baked bread is hot food."</strong> It is incidentally warm. If it meets none of the five conditions it stays zero-rated, and warmth from the oven is not by itself heating for the purpose of being consumed hot.</li>
  <li><strong>"Cold means zero-rated."</strong> Not for the excepted categories. Confectionery, crisps and savoury snacks, soft drinks, alcohol and ice cream are standard-rated cold and taken away, and no packaging or meal-deal structure changes that.</li>
  <li><strong>"Delivery changes the rate."</strong> It does not. The rate follows the underlying supply, on your own moped or through an aggregator.</li>
</ul>

<p>One further point on mixed supplies. Where a single price covers items at different rates (a meal deal pairing a zero-rated sandwich with a standard-rated drink and crisps), the consideration has to be apportioned on a fair and reasonable basis. The mechanics are fact-specific, so treat the apportionment method as a point to agree rather than to assume.</p>

<h2>What this means for your till and VAT return</h2>

<p>The rules above create a practical obligation: your point-of-sale system must capture rate correctly at line level and record the eat-in/takeaway decision per transaction. A VAT return for a mixed-supply business with eat-in seating needs to reflect the actual split of standard-rated and zero-rated sales, not an approximation.</p>

<p>Common problems identified in HMRC inspections of food businesses include: applying zero rate to hot food because it is "takeaway"; applying zero rate to soft drinks because they are cold; failing to prompt for the eat-in/takeaway decision consistently; and using a till that pools all sales at one rate.</p>

<p>If you are approaching the <a href="https://www.gov.uk/vat-registration/when-to-register">£90,000 rolling 12-month taxable turnover threshold</a>, note that both your standard-rated and your zero-rated food sales count toward that figure (zero-rated sales are taxable supplies, just at the zero rate). A business with predominantly cold, zero-rated food can still cross the registration threshold and must monitor monthly, not at year-end.</p>

<h2>When to get help</h2>

<p>Food VAT is one of the areas where the gap between the apparent rule and the correct rule is widest. "Hot food is taxed" is a simplification that gets businesses into trouble; the reality is a multi-dimensional test that depends on your process, your packaging, your seating and your menu wording simultaneously.</p>

<p>The circumstances where specialist input is worth seeking include: you are approaching the £90,000 threshold and have a mixed-rate menu; you have recently changed your packaging or how you hold food between cooking and service; you have added seating to a site that previously sold only to take away; you are setting up a new delivery operation or joining an aggregator platform; you have been selected for a VAT inspection; or you want to check whether the Flat Rate Scheme could simplify your accounting (catering businesses use a 12.5% sector rate under <a href="https://www.gov.uk/hmrc-internal-manuals/vat-flat-rate-scheme/frs7300">FRS 7300</a>, pubs 6.5% and hotels and accommodation 10.5%, though whether FRS saves you money depends on your actual rate mix).</p>
