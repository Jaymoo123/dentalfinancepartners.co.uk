Semi-Commercial Mortgages: Mixed-Use Property Finance in the UK

A semi-commercial property, the shop with a flat above, the office with living accommodation, the pub with a manager's maisonette, is one of the most common assets in UK property investment and one of the most misunderstood to finance. It is not a residential mortgage, it is not a standard buy-to-let, and it is not a pure commercial mortgage either. It sits in the middle, financed as a specialist mixed-use commercial loan, valued as two things at once, and governed by a regulatory test that decides whether the loan is even regulated in the first place. This guide sets out how a semi-commercial mortgage works, where the boundaries sit, and the three tax quirks that make mixed-use property genuinely different. This is educational information, not a financial promotion. Semi-commercial mortgages taken for a business or investment purpose are unregulated business lending, but their promotion is restricted under section 21 of the Financial Services and Markets Act 2000. We do not arrange finance, compare live rates, or invite you into a credit agreement. Where a mixed-use property is occupied as your own home, a different, regulated set of rules applies and you should speak to an FCA-authorised mortgage adviser. What we do here is explain the mechanics and, crucially, the tax treatment that sits alongside them. What counts as semi-commercial or mixed-use property A property is semi-commercial when it combines a commercial and a residential use within a single building or title. The textbook case is a ground-floor retail unit with one or more self-contained flats on the upper floors. The same category captures offices with living accommodation, guest houses, shops with a maisonette, and pubs or restaurants with residential quarters above.
