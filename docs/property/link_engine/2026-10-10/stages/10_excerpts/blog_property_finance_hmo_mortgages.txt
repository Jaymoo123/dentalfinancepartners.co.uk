HMO Mortgages: How Valuation, Lenders and ICR Actually Work

An HMO mortgage is the finance behind a house in multiple occupation, a property let room by room to three or more tenants who form more than one household and share a kitchen or bathroom. The reason it is a specialist product, priced and underwritten differently from a standard buy-to-let, is simple arithmetic: the same four walls let by the room can produce two or three times the rent of a single family tenancy, and for larger HMOs that income can be turned directly into the valuation. This page is about the finance mechanics, how HMO lenders value the property, what licensing and planning they check, the experience they expect and how the interest coverage ratio behaves on HMO rent. The HMO tax treatment (expense ratios, Section 24, incorporation) lives on our property tax guides, and we cross-link it rather than repeat it. Throughout, the framing is a business one. HMO buy-to-let held for investment is unregulated business lending under the Regulated Activities Order 2001, and everything below assumes a landlord buying or refinancing to let, not an owner-occupier. The one important exception, a live-in-landlord HMO where you or a family member occupy a room, is a regulated mortgage contract, and we flag where that line sits. Why an HMO mortgage is a specialist product Three features push HMO lending out of the mainstream buy-to-let pool and into a smaller group of specialist and challenger lenders. First, the income is higher and more granular: five or six tenancies rather than one, often with bills included, which lenders read as both higher gross yield and higher management intensity.
