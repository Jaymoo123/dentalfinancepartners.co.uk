## Gap/Angle
- This topic currently lives almost entirely on insurer/broker sites (selling the *policy*) rather than probate-specialist sites (helping someone understand the *estate administration* consequence) — a wills-probate-site page can differentiate by leading with the **executor/estate impact** angle: a life insurance payout NOT in trust becomes part of the estate, delays access to funds until probate is granted, and inflates the estate for IHT purposes; a payout IN trust bypasses probate entirely and goes straight to named beneficiaries. That estate-administration framing (vs. "should I buy this product") is the whitespace.
- Several results conflate "life insurance in trust avoids IHT" with the separate mechanic of the 7-year PET/taper relief rule for premium gifts — worth clearly separating: (1) the trust structure keeps the *payout* outside the estate, and (2) *premiums* paid into the policy may themselves be gifts that need to fall within annual exemptions or the normal-expenditure-out-of-income exemption to avoid their own IHT exposure. No competitor result clearly separates these two mechanics.
- No result gives a plain checklist of what happens practically for executors when a deceased person did (or didn't) write their policy in trust — i.e. why executors should check for a trust deed early, since it changes who gets paid, how fast, and whether the sum is included in the IHT400 estate valuation.
- This is core regulated financial-product-structuring territory. The brief must instruct the writer to explain the mechanism factually only (what "written in trust" means, why it keeps the payout outside the estate, why premiums need separate exemption treatment, why trustees then control the policy) and explicitly flag that **deciding whether/how to write a specific policy in trust, and choosing a trust type, is regulated financial advice** — no product or provider recommendations, no "you should write your policy in trust" directive language.
