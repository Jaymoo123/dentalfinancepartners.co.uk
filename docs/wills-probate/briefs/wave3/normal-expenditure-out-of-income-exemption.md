## Gap/Angle
- Every strong result is adviser/accountant-facing technical content (Canada Life, M&G, Royal London, Aberdeen Adviser) — there is no plain-English, executor/family-facing explainer aimed at people trying to understand this exemption either while planning their own gifting or, more relevantly for this site's audience, executors assessing whether a deceased person's regular gifts can be claimed as exempt on the IHT403.
- The **executor's-eye-view** is a genuine and underused angle: this exemption is claimed *after death*, on the IHT403 account, by the executor piecing together the deceased's gifting pattern and income — not by the giver during their lifetime. Most competitor content is written from the living donor's planning perspective, not the executor's evidential/administrative perspective (what records to look for, bank statements, standing orders, what HMRC will accept as proof of "pattern" and "income vs capital").
- No result clearly and simply distinguishes this exemption from the flat £3,000 annual exemption and the 7-year PET rule — readers commonly conflate all "lifetime gifting" IHT rules into one; a clean side-by-side distinction (unlimited but conditional, vs capped but unconditional, vs time-limited PETs) is a strong structural differentiator.
- This is a nuanced, evidence-dependent exemption. The brief must instruct the writer to state the three conditions factually and precisely (regular pattern/normal expenditure, paid from income not capital, does not reduce the giver's standard of living), cite gov.uk/HMRC Inheritance Tax Manual (IHTM06100–IHTM14231 series, s.21 IHTA 1984) as the authority, and explicitly flag that **whether specific historic gifts actually qualify is a fact-specific determination HMRC assesses on the evidence at the time of a claim** — this is not something the page should attempt to pre-judge for a reader's own situation, and complex or high-value gifting patterns should be reviewed with a tax adviser or the solicitor handling the IHT403.
