<p>You can make a doctors tax rebate claim yourself, free, through HMRC, and keep all of it. A repayment agent makes the same claim on the same forms and keeps a share. HMRC's own wording is that taxpayers who claim directly through the free service "will receive 100% of the money owed". The amounts in play are modest and personal: the uniform and laundry flat rate is £185 a year for ambulance staff on active service, £125 for nurses, healthcare assistants, therapists and radiographers, £125 for porters, ward clerks and hospital domestics, and £80 for laboratory, pharmacy and uniformed ancillary staff. Those are deductions, not payments, so £125 is worth £25 at basic rate and £50 at higher rate.</p>

<h2>Should you claim yourself or use a repayment agent?</h2>

<p>Claim yourself. The decision only looks close because the agent quotes a headline refund and you compare it with the effort of a form you have never filled in. Set the two side by side instead.</p>

<h2>How much is a doctors tax rebate actually worth?</h2>

<p>Multiply each deduction by your marginal rate, the percentage of tax you pay on your next pound of income. There is no single NHS uniform allowance figure to quote, because the flat rate follows the occupational group rather than the employer, which is why published figures for it disagree so widely. Take your own group's rate from the list above. If you are in the nursing group, which HMRC defines to include nurses and midwives of all grades, auxiliaries, students, dental nurses, nursing assistants and healthcare assistants or workers, you can add £12 a year for shoes and £6 for stockings, tights or socks where a prescribed style or colour is obligatory at your workplace, giving a maximum of £143. The additions turn on the obligation, not on who bought the items.</p>

<p>Someone in the £125 group paying basic rate saves £25 a year. Claimed for the current year and the 4 previous tax years, that is about £125 in total, of which £100 arrives as a repayment. At the higher rate the same deduction saves £50 a year. Those are the numbers a percentage fee is applied to.</p>

<h2>What can you claim that an agent usually will not?</h2>

<p>The parts of your claim that need evidence, which is where most of the money sits for a doctor. Your GMC registration fee is deductible under ITEPA 2003 s.343, and an annual subscription to a body on HMRC's approved List 3 under s.344, where it is relevant to your work. Medical indemnity you pay yourself is deductible, though for NHS general practice clinical work in England the Clinical Negligence Scheme for General Practice has provided state indemnity at no subscription since 1 April 2019, so a paid subscription now largely covers private and non clinical matters. Business mileage between sites is 55p a mile for the first 10,000 miles in 2026/27 and 25p after that, while home to your first site of the day is ordinary commuting and is not claimable.</p>

<p>The common thread is that none of these are flat rates, so each needs a record. An agent working at volume tends to submit the one line that needs no paperwork and leave the rest, and the rest is usually worth more. The <a href="/blog/medical-professional-expenses-what-is-claimable">guide to medical professional expenses</a> sets out the full list, and <a href="/blog/nurse-tax-relief-professional-subscriptions">professional subscriptions</a> covers the bodies and the restrictions on some of them.</p>

<h2>When can you not claim the uniform flat rate?</h2>

<p>Where the cost has not fallen on you. Nothing is due if your employer reimburses the cost, and nothing is due for laundering if your employer provides a free laundering service that you choose not to use, which HMRC names as a headline error. The initial cost of buying work clothing is never allowable, and neither is everyday clothing worn for work, even where a colour or style is specified. Many hospital doctors get nothing from this relief for exactly those reasons, which is why the subscriptions and mileage lines matter more.</p>

<h2>How do you make the claim?</h2>

<p>Three routes, all free. Use the gov.uk online service if you are purely PAYE, form P87 by post for several closed years at once or an employment that has ended, or your Self Assessment return if you already file one, in which case gov.uk is explicit that you must claim through the return instead. You need your National Insurance number, your employer's PAYE reference from a payslip or P60, and the years you are claiming. Flat rate amounts need no evidence. Everything else does, so keep subscription receipts and a mileage record. If you file a return, <a href="/blog/locum-doctor-self-assessment-filing-guide">the filing guide</a> walks through where the figures go.</p>

<h2>How far back can you go?</h2>

<p>The current tax year and the 4 previous tax years. A claim made in 2026/27 reaches 2022/23, which closes on 5 April 2027. The current year is usually given as a coding adjustment and the closed years as a repayment, so a first claim produces a lump sum and a changed code. If a flat rate already sits in your code, claiming it again creates an underpayment rather than more relief, so check your P2 coding notice or your personal tax account first.</p>

<h2>What has changed in the rules on repayment agents?</h2>

<p>The mechanism, not the economics. Agents once took a deed of assignment, a binding transfer of the repayment that you could not undo. Assignments of an income tax repayment are now void and are not recognised for income tax purposes, so agents use a nomination instead, which is not legally binding and which can be cancelled at any point. What has not changed is that the liability stays with you: if the claim turns out not to be due, you are responsible for it, whoever submitted it.</p>

Where the picture is wider, because you hold locum or agency sessions alongside a substantive post, run private work, or are late with returns, a specialist reviews the whole position rather than one line of it.
