<p>The finance side of registering a domiciliary agency comes down to a formula and a gap. The formula is the annual CQC charge: under the current published scheme, a community social care location pays £239 plus £54.305 for every service user, capped at £92,558 at 1,700 service users or more. The gap is the months between the first cost and the first payment, which you have to evidence without a building, beds or a fit-out to point at. Both are checkable now, and both belong in the model before the application goes anywhere near CQC.</p>

<h2>What does CQC actually charge a domiciliary agency?</h2>

<p>It charges per registered location, and the amount follows your service-user count rather than a fixed band. The <a href="https://www.cqc.org.uk/guidance-providers/fees/provider-fees-payable-social-care-services">published community social care fee scheme</a>, labelled 2024/25, sets the calculation at £239 plus £54.305 for each service user, to a maximum of £92,558. Residential services are banded by registered places instead, which is why a care home operator and an agency owner describing the same regulator talk about the fee in completely different terms.</p>

<p>The practical consequence is that your regulatory cost is variable. A residential home knows its ceiling the day the certificate is issued because the bed count caps it. An agency does not. Every additional package you take on raises the charge, in the same period as it raises the income, so the fee line has to be rebuilt each year from the client list you expect rather than copied forward from last year's accounts. Our <a href="/calculators/cqc-fee-calculator">CQC fee calculator</a> runs the calculation for a given service type and size, and CQC's invoice to you is the definitive figure.</p>

<h2>What financial evidence does a service with no premises produce?</h2>

<p>The same statement every new provider produces, with different contents. New providers submit a financial viability statement on CQC's own template, and it is normally prepared or validated by an accountant. A residential applicant fills much of it with the property: purchase or lease, fit-out, capital allowances on plant, and beds that convert to a knowable maximum income.</p>

<p>A domiciliary applicant has none of that. What is left is the hours ramp, and the honesty of it is what the statement lives or dies on. Show the weekly hours you expect to deliver in each of the first twelve months, the loaded cost of an hour behind them, the office and coordination cost that does not move with hours, and the working capital that covers you until the invoices settle. The <a href="/blog/cqc-and-financial-compliance/cqc-financial-viability-statement-walkthrough">walkthrough of the statement</a> covers the template section by section.</p>

<p>Two inputs are the ones assessors press hardest on. The first is the ramp itself, because a hockey-stick curve in month two describes a wish rather than a plan. The second is the payment cycle. Care staff are paid weekly or fortnightly. Local authority and integrated care board invoices settle in arrears, often after a validation cycle on delivered hours. That mismatch is your real capital requirement, and a statement that shows it and funds it reads far better than one that quietly assumes payment on delivery.</p>

<h2>Which costs start before approval and which start on the day it is granted?</h2>

<p>Everything except revenue starts before approval. Carrying on a regulated activity without registration is a criminal offence under the Health and Social Care Act 2008, so no lawful fee income exists until the registration is in place, while the registered manager, the office, the systems and the insurance are all live before then. The <a href="/blog/cqc-and-financial-compliance/cqc-registration-timeline-cash-burn-before-trading">pre-trading cash burn</a> is modelled separately.</p>

<p>One thing genuinely does change on approval, and it is a VAT point people get backwards. The welfare exemption applies to services supplied by a state-regulated provider, so it attaches from the date registration is approved rather than from the date you applied. That is not a window of recovery in the meantime. An applicant making no taxable supplies has nothing to register for and no route to reclaim the VAT on its set-up spending, and once registered its income is exempt, so the position never opens up later either. Treat VAT on legal work, software, vehicles and professional support as a permanent cost and enter every set-up line VAT inclusive. The <a href="/blog/vat-and-welfare-exemption/care-home-vat-exemption-edge-cases">edge cases around exemption</a> cover the mixed-income situations where this is less clear cut.</p>

<h2>What does registration change in the accounts afterwards?</h2>

<p>Three things. The CQC fee becomes a recurring overhead that accrues across the year rather than a set-up cost, and it is deductible as an ordinary business expense of the trade. Your first accounting period is anchored by the entity, not by the approval date, so a company incorporated months before it was registered will have a first period containing a stretch of pre-trading expenditure that needs treating as such. And you have a regulator entitled to look at your financial position, so management accounts stop being optional information and start being evidence.</p>

<p>Set the ledger up so the service-user count is derivable from the sales data, not just from the care planning system. That number drives the fee calculation, it supports the annual return, and having to reconstruct it from rotas at year end is a wholly avoidable job.</p>

<h2>What happens to registration and cost when you grow?</h2>

<p>Growth touches the registration itself, not only the fee. A second office is a further registered location with its own base charge and its own service-user element in the calculation. Adding a regulated activity, changing the registered manager or moving the registered location are variations that have to be applied for and approved, each with its own lead time. Plan those into the commercial timetable, because a framework start date that assumes an instant variation is a date you can miss.</p>

<p>Scale brings one further layer, though it is well beyond the point most agencies reach. The largest providers are subject to CQC's market oversight regime under the Care Act 2014, which requires them to notify the regulator of material changes in their financial position. A single-location agency is not in that regime, but it is worth knowing it exists before an acquisition takes a group over the threshold.</p>

<h2>What to settle before the application goes in</h2>

<p>Price the fee from your expected service-user count rather than a placeholder, build the twelve-month hours ramp and the working capital that carries the payment gap, and cost every set-up line VAT inclusive. England is the default position here; Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland register providers through their own bodies with their own fee schemes, so none of the CQC figures transfer. If you are assembling the pack, a specialist reviews the financial evidence against the ramp and the funding mix before it is submitted, and your accountant prepares the accounts that follow from it.
