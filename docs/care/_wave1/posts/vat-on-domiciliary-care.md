<p>No, in almost every case you do not. Personal care delivered in a client's home by an agency registered with the Care Quality Commission is an exempt supply of welfare services under Group 7 of Schedule 9 to the Value Added Tax Act 1994, so no VAT goes on the invoice, whoever is paying it. Three consequences do more work than the answer itself: exemption starts on the date CQC approves your registration and not a day earlier, it blocks recovery of the VAT you pay on your own costs, and it keeps your care fees outside the £90,000 registration threshold entirely.</p>

<h2>Which supplies are exempt, and who has to be registered?</h2>

<p>The supply is exempt when a state regulated provider delivers welfare services, and CQC registration is what makes a domiciliary agency state regulated in England. HMRC's guidance describes state regulated private welfare institutions and agencies as providers registered with or regulated by a named body, and the Care Quality Commission is the body for England.</p>

<p>The identity of the payer does not change the analysis. A privately paying client, a local authority commissioning under a framework, a direct payment made to the client and an NHS funded package all buy the same exempt supply of care. What changes the analysis is who is regulated and who is legally responsible for delivering the care, which is the point most new agencies get wrong.</p>

<h2>When does the exemption actually start?</h2>

<p>It starts on the day the regulator approves your registration. HMRC's position is that a provider becomes state regulated when the relevant regulatory body approves its application, and that the provider is not state regulated while the application is under consideration. There is no back-dating to the application date.</p>

<p>For a genuinely new agency this is usually a theoretical problem rather than a live one, because carrying on a regulated activity before registration is a criminal offence under the Health and Social Care Act 2008, so there should be no care fees in that window at all. It stops being theoretical if you are trading in something adjacent while you wait, or if you take on a package through another registered provider before your own approval lands. The <a href="/blog/cqc-and-financial-compliance/cqc-registration-timeline-cash-burn-before-trading">cash burn during the registration window</a> is modelled separately, and the VAT status of anything you do bill during it is worth settling before the first invoice rather than after.</p>

<h2>What does exemption cost you?</h2>

<p>It costs you the VAT on everything you buy. None of the VAT on purchases used to make exempt supplies comes back, so opted-to-tax office rent, vehicles and fuel, rostering and care planning software, mobile phones, uniforms, equipment, recruitment and professional fees all sit in your accounts gross and stay there. Exemption is a cost, not a perk, and an agency that models its rates on VAT exclusive cost lines understates its own cost base by up to a fifth on every affected line.</p>

<p>So model it gross. Enter every cost VAT inclusive from the first draft, and treat irrecoverable VAT as part of the loaded cost of a care hour alongside wages, employer National Insurance, holiday accrual and paid travel time. Put those lines through our <a href="/calculators/true-cost-care-hour-calculator">true cost of a care hour calculator</a> and the rate you quote covers what the hour really costs.</p>

<h2>Do you have to register for VAT at £90,000?</h2>

<p>Only if your taxable turnover reaches it, and exempt care fees are not taxable turnover. Registration is compulsory once taxable supplies in a rolling twelve month period exceed £90,000, and a wholly exempt agency never gets there however large it becomes. Growth does not move you towards the test, it only grows a figure the test ignores.</p>

<p>What you do have to monitor is the taxable part, separately and from the start. Training sold to other providers, consultancy, rent charged on premises where an option to tax is in place, and staff supplied to third parties are all capable of being taxable, and they aggregate against the threshold on their own. Keep the ledger split by supply type from your first month, because reconstructing it a year later to answer a threshold question is far more work than coding it correctly as you go.</p>

<h2>What if you supply carers rather than care?</h2>

<p>Then you are making a standard rated supply of staff and the exemption does not reach it. HMRC's guidance is that employment businesses in the welfare sector make a taxable supply of staff to a third party where that third party is legally responsible for the onward supply of care to the final recipient. An introductory or staffing model that places carers with another registered provider, or with a local authority that carries the responsibility for the care, is selling labour rather than welfare.</p>

<p>This matters because the two models look identical from the outside. The same carers, in the same homes, doing the same tasks, can be an exempt supply or a standard rated one depending on which entity holds the regulated responsibility and what the contract says about it. If your agency runs both models, or sub-contracts in either direction, the contracts and the regulatory position need to be read together before the VAT treatment is fixed. A specialist reviews the contract chain rather than the job descriptions. Our <a href="/for/domiciliary-care">domiciliary care agency page</a> sets out where this sits among the other early decisions.</p>

<h2>What happens if some of your income is taxable?</h2>

<p>You fall into partial exemption, and you will usually lose the input VAT anyway. Where a provider makes both exempt and taxable supplies, input tax has to be attributed between them and the standard method or an approved special method applied. Recovery survives in full only where both de minimis limbs hold: exempt input tax averaging no more than £625 a month, and staying under half of the period's total input tax.</p>

<p>An agency whose income is overwhelmingly exempt care will fail the second limb of that test long before the first, so a small taxable sideline adds a calculation without adding recovery. That is not a reason to avoid taxable activity, but it is a reason to price it knowing the VAT it carries is unlikely to come back. The <a href="/blog/vat-and-welfare-exemption/care-home-vat-exemption-edge-cases">edge cases across the welfare exemption</a> cover the harder boundary questions, and a structured <a href="/services/care-vat-review">care VAT review</a> is where a mixed position gets tested properly.</p>

<h2>Does the answer change outside England?</h2>

<p>The VAT rules do not, because VAT is a United Kingdom tax, but the regulator does. HMRC's guidance names the care regulators in Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland alongside the Care Quality Commission, so what makes you state regulated is registration with the regulator for the nation you operate in. An agency working across a border registers with each relevant regulator, and the exemption for each nation's supplies follows that nation's approval date.</p>
