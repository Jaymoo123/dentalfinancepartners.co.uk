<p>Choose the structure before the application, because the Care Quality Commission registers a named legal entity rather than a business idea. For most new care providers the answer is a limited company: it pays corporation tax at 19% on profits up to £50,000 and 25% above £250,000 with marginal relief between, it keeps the trading liability away from your personal assets, and it is the wrapper lenders and local authority commissioners expect to contract with. If you register as an individual and incorporate later, CQC treats that as a change of business type and you complete the provider application again.</p>

<h2>What is CQC actually registering?</h2>

<p>CQC registers a provider, and a provider is a specific legal person: an individual, a partnership, or an organisation such as a limited company. Registration is mandatory before any regulated activity begins, and carrying on a regulated activity without registration is a criminal offence under the Health and Social Care Act 2008. <a href="https://www.cqc.org.uk/guidance-providers/registration">CQC's registration guidance</a> sets out the regulated activities and the requirement to be registered before you start.</p>

<p>The practical consequence is that the entity on the application form is the entity that holds the registration. Your registration is not attached to the service, the premises or to you personally as the person who built it. It belongs to whichever legal person applied.</p>

<h2>What happens if you change structure after you are registered?</h2>

<p>You apply again. CQC's guidance on <a href="https://www.cqc.org.uk/guidance-regulation/providers/registration/change-your-registered-business-type">changing your registered business type</a> confirms that moving from an individual provider to a partnership or limited company is a change in legal entity, that you must complete the provider application again with the relevant supporting documents, and that you must also send a completed insurance form and the additional information form for your sector. Registered managers at the location must apply to continue their registration under the incoming provider, cancel their manager registration, or remove the location from it.</p>

<p>That is the whole reason this decision belongs before the first application and not after the first year of trading. The cost of getting it wrong is not a form: it is a second registration process, a second financial viability exercise, and a period in which nobody can be providing care while unregistered.</p>

<h2>What does a limited company change about your tax?</h2>

<p>Profits are taxed inside the company at corporation tax rates: 19% on profits up to £50,000, 25% on profits above £250,000, and marginal relief in between, per <a href="https://www.gov.uk/corporation-tax-rates">HMRC's published rates</a>. You then pay personal tax only on what you take out. Owner-directors taking a small salary and the balance as dividends pay <a href="https://www.gov.uk/tax-on-dividends">dividend tax</a> at 10.75%, 35.75% or 39.35% from 6 April 2026, on amounts above the £500 dividend allowance.</p>

<p>A sole trader or partner has no such split. The whole profit is taxed personally in the year it is earned, whether or not you drew it, and Class 4 National Insurance applies on top. For a care business that must hold working capital back for payroll while local authority invoices settle, being taxed on money you cannot take out is the sharper problem of the two.</p>

<p>A propco and opco split, where one company owns the property and another operates the service, is common in residential care. Be aware that associated company rules divide the £50,000 and £250,000 thresholds between the companies, so a two-company group reaches the 25% rate on lower profits than a standalone operator would.</p>

<h2>Are there reasons to stay unincorporated?</h2>

<p>Yes, and they are mostly about scale and simplicity. A single registered provider who is also the registered manager, running one small service with modest profits, carries less administration as a sole trader: no company accounts, no confirmation statement, no separate corporation tax return, no dividend paperwork. The <a href="/blog/business-structure-and-acquisition/care-provider-formation-trends">formation data across the sector</a> counts new care companies, not new sole traders, so it tells you incorporation is busy rather than that it is the only workable route.</p>

<p>Two things to weigh against the simplicity. First, liability: a sole trader and the partners in an ordinary partnership are personally exposed to the business's debts, which matters in a sector with heavy fixed payroll and slow fee settlement. Second, <a href="/blog/care-home-accounts-and-funding/mtd-it-care-owner-operators">Making Tax Digital for Income Tax</a>, which brings sole traders and partners with combined self-employment and property income above £50,000 into quarterly reporting from 6 April 2026, with the threshold falling to £30,000 from April 2027. A company director is outside that regime for the company's trading profits.</p>

<h2>How does the structure affect the financial viability statement?</h2>

<p>New providers must submit a financial viability statement on CQC's template, and the structure decides whose finances the statement describes. A company applicant presents company projections, opening share capital and director funding. An individual applicant presents personal financial standing, because there is no separate entity to present. Neither is harder to pass, but they are different documents, and assembling one and then converting to the other duplicates the work. The <a href="/blog/cqc-and-financial-compliance/cqc-financial-viability-statement-walkthrough">walkthrough of the statement</a> and the wider <a href="/blog/cqc-and-financial-compliance/cqc-registration-requirements-financial-viability-leg">financial leg of registration</a> set out what the template asks for.</p>

<h2>Does the structure change your VAT or payroll position?</h2>

<p>No. Welfare services supplied by a CQC-registered provider are exempt from VAT because the provider is state-regulated, not because of its company law form, and the inability to recover input VAT on the costs of making exempt supplies follows a sole trader exactly as it follows a company. Payroll is the same on both routes: employer National Insurance at 15% above the £5,000 secondary threshold, and the Employment Allowance of up to £10,500 for eligible employers.</p>

<h2>What about the day you sell?</h2>

<p>Business Asset Disposal Relief applies to qualifying disposals on either route, taxing the gain at 18% for disposals from 6 April 2026 against the standard 24% higher rate. Both routes need two years of qualifying trading ownership before the disposal, which is another argument for settling the structure at the start rather than restarting the clock later. Note that propco and opco separation can break eligibility, so a structure built purely for property protection can cost relief on exit.</p>

<h2>The order to decide in</h2>

<p>Settle the entity, incorporate or register it, open the bank account in that name, then build the financial viability statement around it and apply. Doing it in that order means the name on the CQC application, on Companies House, on the insurance and on the bank mandate all match, which is what the regulator is checking for.
