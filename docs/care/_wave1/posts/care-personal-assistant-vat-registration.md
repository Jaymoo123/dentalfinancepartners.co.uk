<p>Only once your taxable turnover passes <strong>£90,000</strong> over any rolling 12 months. That threshold is the whole answer, but the reason it bites sooner for a care personal assistant than for a care agency is worth understanding: because a directly engaged personal assistant sits outside CQC registration, the welfare exemption does not reach you, so every pound you invoice is standard rated turnover counting in full towards the £90,000. A domiciliary agency billing £300,000 of exempt care can have zero taxable turnover. You billing £91,000 of the same work by hand do not.</p>

<h2>Why does the exemption not cover your work?</h2>
<p>Because the exemption looks at who supplies the care, not at what the care is. Group 7 of Schedule 9 to the Value Added Tax Act 1994 exempts welfare services supplied by a charity, a public body or a state regulated private welfare institution. A CQC registered agency is state regulated. You are not, and that is by design: Schedule 1 to the Health and Social Care Act 2008 (Regulated Activities) Regulations 2014 carves a carer engaged by the individual, or by a related third party acting for them, out of the regulated activity of personal care, provided no employment agency is in the middle and you work wholly under that person's direction and control.</p>
<p>So the same carve out that keeps you out of CQC registration keeps you out of the exemption. You avoid the registration fee and the inspection regime, and in exchange your fees behave like any other trading income. Our note on <a href="/blog/vat-and-welfare-exemption/care-home-vat-exemption-edge-cases">VAT exemption edge cases</a> sets out how the position works on the registered side of that line.</p>

<h2>What actually counts towards the £90,000?</h2>
<p>Everything you charge for supplying your services, whoever pays. The client paying privately, the family paying, a local authority direct payment, a personal health budget, a platform that takes a cut: all of it is consideration for a standard rated supply of services and all of it counts. The funding route never changes the liability.</p>
<p>The lines that catch people out sit at the edges:</p>
<ul>
  <li><strong>Client money you hold.</strong> Cash you are given to do the weekly shop, then account for, is not your turnover. You are not supplying groceries. Keep it in a separate record so the distinction survives a check.</li>
  <li><strong>Travel you recharge.</strong> Mileage or fares you bill the client as part of getting to and between their calls is normally part of the consideration for your service and counts, at the same standard rate as the service itself. That is separate from the income tax deduction you take at 55p per mile for the first 10,000 business miles from 6 April 2026, which belongs to the quarterly reporting timetable in the <a href="/blog/care-home-accounts-and-funding/mtd-it-care-owner-operators">Making Tax Digital guide</a>.</li>
  <li><strong>Agency shifts.</strong> If an agency engages you and pays you, you are supplying your services to the agency. That is a taxable supply too, and it counts.</li>
  <li><strong>Other trading income.</strong> Training you deliver, supervision, anything else you invoice through the same sole trader business, is added to the same running total.</li>
</ul>
<p>Work you do as an employee is not in scope at all. Only what you supply as a business counts.</p>

<h2>When does the clock actually trip?</h2>
<p>There are two tests and either one is enough. The backward test looks at your taxable turnover over the previous 12 months, checked at the end of every month, not at your year end. Pass £90,000 and you must tell HMRC within 30 days of the end of that month, and you are registered from the first day of the second month after you went over. The forward test looks at the next 30 days alone: if you expect more than £90,000 in that one month, you register by the end of it and you are registered from the day the expectation arose.</p>
<p>For a personal assistant the backward test is the realistic one, and it creeps. Around £1,730 a week of invoiced work is the annual equivalent. A support worker covering several clients, taking overnight and weekend cover, and billing through a couple of platforms as well gets closer to that than the monthly figures suggest, because the rolling year keeps a busy winter in the total long after it ends.</p>

<h2>Does incorporating give you a fresh threshold?</h2>
<p>Yes, in the sense that a limited company is a different person for VAT and begins with its own clean rolling total. That is a real consequence of changing who makes the supply, and it usually comes as a side effect of a decision made for other reasons: profits above the point where 19% corporation tax on the first £50,000 plus dividends at 10.75% beats income tax and Class 4 National Insurance on the whole amount.</p>
<p>What does not work is splitting one business into two to keep both under £90,000. Shared clients, shared equipment, shared phone number and one person doing all the work is the pattern HMRC treats as artificial separation, and it can direct that the parts are a single taxable person from a current date. Check as well whether the scheme, family or platform paying you will contract with a company at all; several direct payment schemes will not.</p>

<h2>What changes when you take on a second carer?</h2>
<p>Your VAT answer inverts. The carve out holds only while you are the carer the individual engaged, working under their direction. Employ someone, take a partner, or send a substitute, and you are carrying on a regulated activity, which must be registered with CQC before it starts. Trading unregistered is a criminal offence, so this is not a step to discover after the fact.</p>
<p>Once registered, you are a state regulated provider, your care supplies become exempt welfare, and they stop counting towards the £90,000. If you had already registered for VAT, deregistration comes into view. The cost arrives on the other side: you cannot recover VAT on the vehicles, software, training and professional fees used to make exempt supplies. Work out which side of the line leaves you better off before you take the step, and put the numbers on paper in <a href="/services/care-vat-review">a care VAT review</a>.</p>

<h2>What if you do cross £90,000?</h2>
<p>You charge 20% on top of your fees from your registration date, file returns, and recover input VAT on your costs. Private clients and direct payment families cannot recover that 20%, so in practice you either absorb it or renegotiate, which is why the threshold matters more to a personal assistant than the compliance work does. If some of your income is genuinely exempt and some is taxable, partial exemption applies, and you keep the exempt side of your input tax only while it clears both de minimis limbs: an average of no more than £625 a month, and below half your input tax overall.</p>
<p>The practical step is small: a running 12 month total of invoiced fees, updated monthly, alongside the records you already keep. The <a href="/for/self-employed-carers-and-personal-assistants">position for self-employed carers and personal assistants</a> covers the structure and expenses side that sits around it. Your accountant prepares the registration and the first returns if the total gets close.</p>
