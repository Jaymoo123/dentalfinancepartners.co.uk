<p>There are six money decisions behind a new domiciliary care agency, and the order they are made in matters more than any one of them. Your trading structure comes first because it is named on the CQC application. Opening capital and the financial viability statement come second because registration must be granted before you can lawfully raise a fee. Then the VAT position, then the pay model at £12.71 an hour from 1 April 2026, then your charge rate, then the reporting regime you will live with. Each decision narrows the one after it, which is why taking them out of order is expensive.</p>

<h2>Decision one: which trading structure do you register?</h2>

<p>Settle sole trader, partnership or limited company before you apply, because the applicant named on the CQC registration is the legal entity that will carry on the regulated activity. Moving into a company afterwards is a new registration, not an amendment.</p>

<p>The tax comparison is straightforward. A company pays corporation tax at 19% on profits up to £50,000 and 25% above £250,000, with marginal relief between. An owner director taking the balance as dividends pays 10.75% at the basic rate, 35.75% at the higher rate and 39.35% at the additional rate from 6 April 2026, above the £500 dividend allowance. A sole trader pays income tax and Class 4 National Insurance on the whole profit as it arises.</p>

<p>The commercial side often decides it before the tax does. Local authority framework bids, insurers and lenders read a limited company balance sheet more readily than sole trader accounts, and limited liability matters in a sector where a single safeguarding claim can outlast the contract that caused it.</p>

<h2>Decision two: how much opening capital, and can you evidence it?</h2>

<p>You need enough capital to fund the whole period before registration is granted, because trading in a regulated activity before that point is a criminal offence under the Health and Social Care Act 2008. Rent, your registered manager's salary, recruitment, insurance and systems all start before the first invoice can be raised, and all of it comes out of opening capital.</p>

<p>New providers submit a financial viability statement on CQC's own template as part of the application, and it is where your opening capital, monthly burn and contingency are set out and tested. A statement that cannot show survival through the registration window stalls the application. Our <a href="/blog/cqc-and-financial-compliance/cqc-financial-viability-statement-walkthrough">walkthrough of the financial viability statement</a> covers what goes in each section, and the <a href="/blog/cqc-and-financial-compliance/cqc-registration-timeline-cash-burn-before-trading">cash burn during registration</a> is modelled separately.</p>

<p>Build the model monthly from lease signature to first fee received, add a contingency for a request for further information, and treat the registration duration as a variable input rather than a fixed date. A specialist reviews the assumptions before the pack is submitted.</p>

<h2>Decision three: what is your VAT position, and what does exemption cost you?</h2>

<p>Welfare services supplied by a CQC-registered provider are exempt from VAT under Group 7 of Schedule 9 to the Value Added Tax Act 1994, so almost no domiciliary agency charges VAT on care fees. The decision is not whether to charge it but what exemption costs you, because it is a cost rather than a perk.</p>

<p>An exempt supplier cannot recover input VAT on the purchases used to make those supplies, so VAT on your office, vehicles, software, equipment and professional fees is a permanent overhead and every cost line in your model should be entered VAT inclusive. Exempt income does not count towards the £90,000 registration threshold either, which applies to taxable turnover only, so an agency with several million pounds of exempt fee income can have no registration obligation at all.</p>

<p>If you plan any taxable activity alongside the care, selling training to other providers for example, track those supplies separately from day one. Mixed providers run partial exemption calculations, and recovery is usually lost because exempt input tax exceeds the de minimis limit of £625 a month.</p>

<h2>Decision four: how are carers engaged and paid?</h2>

<p>Engage carers as employees unless the arrangement genuinely fails every employment test, because in a rota-based domiciliary model it will not. Workers who cannot send a substitute, who are told when and where to attend and who work under your direction are employees in substance regardless of the label on the contract. HMRC audit activity in care is concentrated on exactly this pattern, and the liability reaches years backwards.</p>

<p>Then price the hour honestly. The National Living Wage is £12.71 for workers aged 21 and over from 1 April 2026, and £10.85 for those aged 18 to 20. Employer National Insurance runs at 15% above the £5,000 secondary threshold, offset for most new agencies by the Employment Allowance of up to £10,500 a year. Travel time between one client and the next is working time for minimum wage purposes and has to be paid, mileage reimbursed at the approved rate is 55p a mile for the first 10,000 business miles from 6 April 2026, and holiday accrues at 12.07% of the hours worked in each pay period for irregular-hours staff. The <a href="/blog/payroll-and-workforce-costs/sleep-in-pay-travel-time-nmw">rules on travel time and sleep-in pay</a> set out where the underpayments usually appear.</p>

<h2>Decision five: what do you charge, and how is the rate built?</h2>

<p>Build the charge rate from a fully loaded cost per care hour, then add margin, rather than matching whatever local agencies advertise. The loaded cost includes the wage, employer National Insurance, holiday accrual, paid travel time and mileage, pensions, the non-chargeable hours your coordinators and registered manager work, and irrecoverable VAT. Our <a href="/calculators/true-cost-care-hour-calculator">true cost of a care hour calculator</a> assembles those lines.</p>

<p>The rate also depends on who pays. Local authority framework rates are negotiated against the fair cost of care framework in the Care Act statutory guidance, and a rate below your loaded cost does not become sustainable through volume. Model the funding mix you expect, not the one you would prefer.</p>

<h2>Decision six: which reporting regime will you be in?</h2>

<p>The last decision is really a consequence of the first. A company files annual accounts and a corporation tax return. A sole trader or partner falls into Making Tax Digital for Income Tax where combined self-employment and property income exceeds £50,000 from 6 April 2026, dropping to £30,000 from 6 April 2027 and £20,000 from 6 April 2028, which means quarterly updates from compatible software. Either way, payroll runs under real time information from your first employee.</p>

<p>Choose the bookkeeping system before the first month, and set it up so that fee income is analysed by funder from the start. Local authority, NHS and private income behave differently on cash collection and on contract risk, and a ledger that separates them is what makes the next framework bid and the next set of accounts straightforward, and your accountant prepares the year end from it.</p>

<p>England is the default position throughout. Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland register providers through the Care Inspectorate, Care Inspectorate Wales and the Regulation and Quality Improvement Authority, so the registration and financial evidence steps differ; the tax, VAT and minimum wage positions apply across the United Kingdom. The full setup is covered on our <a href="/services/start-a-domiciliary-care-agency">start a domiciliary care agency</a> page.</p>
