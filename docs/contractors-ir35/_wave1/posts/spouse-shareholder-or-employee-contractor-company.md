<p>You have two routes, and they are taxed differently. Paying a spouse a salary of <strong>£12,570</strong> for genuine work leaves a household roughly <strong>£6,000</strong> better off on that slice of profit than paying the same money to a higher-rate director as a dividend, and it usually unlocks the <strong>£10,500</strong> Employment Allowance as a second prize. Giving a spouse genuine ordinary shares saves nothing inside the company, but it moves dividends onto a second personal allowance and a second basic-rate band, worth about <strong>£4,670</strong> on the first £13,070 where the spouse has no other income. The routes are not rivals. Salary needs real work, shares need real ownership, and most households that qualify use both.</p>

<h2>What does a spouse salary actually save in 2026/27?</h2>

<p>About £6,000 on £12,570 of profit, where the spouse has no other income, the company can claim the Employment Allowance and you are a higher-rate taxpayer. Follow the same money down two paths. Paid as salary, the company deducts the £12,570 from its profit, the spouse pays no income tax because the personal allowance is £12,570, and no employee National Insurance because the primary threshold is also £12,570. The household keeps £12,570. Left in the company and drawn by you instead, that £12,570 bears corporation tax at 19%, leaving £10,182, then dividend tax at the upper rate of 35.75%, leaving £6,542. The difference is £6,028, and it widens to roughly £6,630 where the company sits in the marginal relief band at about 26.5%. The saving is an unused personal allowance being used, plus the deduction a wage attracts and a dividend does not, so it shrinks as the spouse's own income grows.</p>

<h2>Does a second person on the payroll unlock the Employment Allowance?</h2>

<p>Usually, and it is often worth more than the salary decision itself. The Employment Allowance is £10,500 for 2026/27 and offsets the company's secondary Class 1 National Insurance, but it is not available where the only employee is a single director. That restriction is why so many personal service companies sit at a low salary. Put a second genuinely employed person on the payroll above the <strong>£5,000</strong> secondary threshold and the restriction normally falls away.</p>

<p>The knock-on matters. Without the allowance, employer National Insurance at 15% above £5,000 is a real cost, so a single director often stops at the lower earnings limit of <strong>£6,708</strong>, enough for a qualifying National Insurance year. With the allowance relieving the employer National Insurance on a higher salary, £12,570 usually becomes the better number for the director too, as our <a href="/blog/limited-company-tax/director-salary-dividend-split-guide">director salary and dividend split guide</a> sets out.</p>

<h2>What does HMRC expect to see for a spouse salary?</h2>

<p>Evidence that the company got something for its money: a defined role you can describe in a sentence, a rate you could defend by pointing at what an unconnected person doing the same hours would charge, and the ordinary trail of an employment, meaning a PAYE record, payslips and real payments leaving the company account into the spouse's own. Bookkeeping, invoicing and credit control, scheduling, bid administration and company secretarial work are all genuine functions in a contractor company.</p>

<p>Two failures recur. Pay for no work is not deductible, so the company loses the relief while the spouse is still taxed on what they received. A commercially indefensible rate raises the same problem in a different form: £20,000 for two hours a week of filing invites the question of what the other £15,000 was for.</p>

<h2>Are shares gifted to a spouse caught by the settlements rules?</h2>

<p>Not where they are an outright gift of genuine ordinary shares. The settlements legislation at <strong>ITTOIA 2005 s.624</strong> can tax income back on the person who really provided it. Section 626 then carves out an outright gift between spouses or civil partners, provided the gift carries the right to the whole of the income and the property is not wholly or substantially a right to income. Ordinary shares carrying votes, a share of capital on a winding up and the real risk that no dividend is ever declared sit inside that exception. That is the point <em>Jones v Garnett</em> settled in 2007, and it remains the basis on which contractor households hold shares jointly.</p>

<p>The exception has edges. A share engineered to carry a dividend entitlement and nothing else looks like a right to income, which is the usual way out of s.626, and the same goes for a gift you can unwind or whose proceeds can return to you. Separate share classes carry their own conditions, covered in our note on <a href="/blog/limited-company-tax/alphabet-shares-contractor-company">alphabet shares</a>. Whatever the class, the shares must be genuinely transferred, entered in the register of members and reported to Companies House, with dividends declared properly and paid into the spouse's own account.</p>

<h2>Is there a tax charge on transferring the shares?</h2>

<p>No capital gains tax at the point of transfer, where you are married or in a civil partnership and living together. <strong>TCGA 1992 s.58</strong> treats the disposal as made for a consideration producing neither a gain nor a loss. Your spouse takes over your base cost rather than the value on the day, which matters on a later sale or winding up, because their gain runs from your original cost. Separated couples keep the treatment only for a limited window. If you both run companies, check the associated companies rules too, which divide the £50,000 and £250,000 limits and can push both into <a href="/blog/limited-company-tax/corporation-tax-contractor-limited-company">marginal relief</a>.</p>

<p>Read that in order rather than as a menu. Salary goes first because it is deductible and protects a National Insurance record; shares go second because dividends come out of profit that has already paid corporation tax, so they only change whose rate applies. The order flips only where the spouse does no work for the company, which closes the salary route entirely.</p>

<h2>What changes once the spouse has their own income?</h2>

<p>Most of the arithmetic. A spouse already earning £40,000 elsewhere has no spare personal allowance, so a salary from your company is taxed at 20%, carries employee National Insurance at 8% and costs the company 15% above £5,000. Dividends to that spouse fall at 10.75% only until their total income reaches £50,270, after which they are at 35.75% like you. Run the household numbers first, using the current <a href="/blog/pension-and-dividends/dividend-tax-rates-contractors-2026">dividend rates</a> rather than the 8.75% and 33.75% that applied before 6 April 2026.</p>

<p>Test both routes against the lever that often beats them. An employer pension contribution from the company is deductible, carries no National Insurance and is not capped by anyone's salary, so for a household that does not need the cash this year it can do more than any split between two people. A specialist reviews the working pattern, the household income and the share structure together, because the three decisions interact.</p>
