<h2>The cost and timescale in one paragraph</h2>

<p>Registering a lasting power of attorney (LPA) in England and Wales costs <strong>£92 per document</strong>, paid to the Office of the Public Guardian (OPG), and takes <strong>8 to 10 weeks</strong> if the forms contain no mistakes, according to <a href="https://www.gov.uk/power-of-attorney/register">gov.uk's registration guidance</a>. Most people make two LPAs, one for property and financial affairs and one for health and welfare, so the standard bill is £184 per person, or £368 for a couple doing the job properly together. Donors on a low income pay half, and donors on certain benefits pay nothing. What follows is general information about the process, not legal or financial advice, and it covers England and Wales (Scotland and Northern Ireland run separate systems).</p>

<p>One point worth stating early because it changes people's timing: an LPA is legally useless until it is registered. Signing the forms and putting them in a drawer protects nobody. If capacity is lost while an unregistered LPA sits waiting, your family may still face a deputyship application through the Court of Protection, the slow and expensive route our <a href="/blog/power-of-attorney/lpa-vs-deputyship-after-loss-of-capacity">LPA vs deputyship guide</a> compares in full.</p>

<h2>The registration process, step by step</h2>

<ol>
<li><strong>Complete the forms.</strong> Use the <a href="https://www.gov.uk/power-of-attorney">gov.uk LPA service</a> to fill in the forms on screen, or complete the paper LP1F (finances) and LP1H (health) forms. The online tool checks for common errors as you go, which matters because mistakes are the main cause of delay.</li>
<li><strong>Sign in the correct order.</strong> The donor signs first, witnessed; then the certificate provider (who confirms the donor understands what they are signing and is under no pressure); then each attorney, witnessed. Signing out of sequence is a classic reason forms bounce back.</li>
<li><strong>Notify anyone named to be told.</strong> If the LPA names people to notify, they are sent form LP3 and have 3 weeks to raise concerns with OPG.</li>
<li><strong>Post the forms with payment.</strong> Even where the form was created online, it must currently be printed, signed and posted to OPG. Pay the £92 per LPA by card or cheque.</li>
<li><strong>Wait for registration.</strong> OPG checks the application, holds it through the objection window, then stamps and returns the registered LPA. Budget 8 to 10 weeks for a clean application; errors can add months because corrected forms rejoin the queue.</li>
</ol>

<h2>Who pays less than £92</h2>

<p>The fee is tested against the donor's finances, never the attorney's, under the rules on <a href="https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/power-of-attorney-fees">gov.uk's power of attorney fees page</a>:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Half fee (£46 per LPA):</strong> where the donor's income before tax is under £12,000 a year. Income here means things like salary, pension and interest, not capital in the bank.</li>
<li><strong>No fee:</strong> where the donor receives certain means-tested benefits. Universal Credit is the exception: a donor on Universal Credit is assessed for the half-fee remission rather than a full exemption.</li>
<li><strong>Repeat applications:</strong> if OPG returns an application for correction and it is resubmitted in time, the repeat fee is £46 rather than £92.</li>
</ul>

<p>Claims go in on form LPA120, submitted with the LPA application and evidence of income or benefits. Fees must be dealt with at the point of applying; they are non-refundable and cannot be claimed back retrospectively.</p>

<h2>Where the "fully digital LPA" has got to</h2>

<p>You may have read that LPAs are going fully online. The legal groundwork is real: the Powers of Attorney Act 2023 allows for a completely digital creation and registration service, with identity checks and a retained paper route for people who prefer it. OPG has been building and piloting that service. But the position on gov.uk today is unchanged: the form can be <em>filled in</em> online, yet it must still be printed, signed by hand in the right order and posted. Until gov.uk itself says otherwise, treat any "register your LPA entirely online" claim from a third party with caution, and take the current 8 to 10 week paper timescale as the planning assumption.</p>

<h2>Worth doing alongside the LPA</h2>

<p>LPAs and wills are the two halves of the same planning conversation: the LPA governs decisions while you are alive but lack capacity, the will governs what happens after death, and neither does the other's job. Our <a href="/blog/power-of-attorney/power-of-attorney-vs-probate">power of attorney vs probate explainer</a> unpicks that boundary, and the <a href="/calculators/making-a-will-checklist">making a will checklist</a> is a quick way to see whether the will side of your affairs is in order while the LPA forms are in the post. If you are still deciding which LPAs to make, start with the difference between the <a href="/blog/power-of-attorney/property-and-finance-lpa-vs-health-and-welfare-lpa-whats-the-difference">property and finance LPA and the health and welfare LPA</a>, and see the <a href="/lasting-power-of-attorney">lasting power of attorney pillar guide</a> for the full picture.</p>

<p>Most straightforward LPAs can be completed without professional help using the gov.uk service. Where the estate is complex, family relationships are strained, or there is any doubt about the donor's capacity, an estate planning specialist can make sure the documents are signed correctly first time and hold up when they are eventually needed. Getting the registration right once, years before anyone has to rely on it, is precisely what these documents are for.</p>
