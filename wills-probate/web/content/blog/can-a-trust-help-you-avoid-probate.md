<h2>Yes, for the assets inside it. No, for everything else</h2>

<p>A trust can keep specific assets out of probate: anything you properly transfer into a lifetime trust is legally owned by the trustees, not you, so it passes under the trust deed when you die without your executors needing a grant. But a trust does not make probate disappear. Whatever is still in your sole name at death, the bank accounts, the investments, the house you never transferred, goes through probate exactly as before. This article covers England and Wales and is general information to help you weigh the options, not legal or financial advice.</p>

<p>Under <a href="https://www.gov.uk/trusts-taxes">GOV.UK's guidance on trusts</a>, the settlor (the person who sets up the trust) hands assets to trustees, who become the legal owners and manage them for the beneficiaries. That change of legal ownership is the whole trick: probate exists to give someone authority over a dead person's assets, and trust assets already have someone with authority, the trustees. A will trust is different. It is created by your will, on your death, so your executors still need the <a href="/probate">grant of probate</a> before anything can reach it.</p>

<h2>What avoiding probate is actually worth</h2>

<p>Here is the number that deflates most probate-avoidance sales pitches. The <a href="https://www.gov.uk/applying-for-probate/fees">probate application fee in England and Wales</a> is <strong>£526</strong>, and nothing at all for estates of £5,000 or less, plus £2 for each extra sealed copy of the grant ordered with the application. That is the entire fee a trust avoids. Setting up a lifetime trust professionally commonly costs from around £1,000 to several thousand pounds, before any ongoing administration.</p>

<p>What probate genuinely costs families is usually time rather than money: months of waiting before assets can be collected, and executor hours spent on forms and valuations. A trust does shortcut that for the trust assets, and in a handful of situations that speed matters, for example a family business that must keep trading. For a typical estate, though, the arithmetic is unforgiving: you are paying thousands up front, with certainty, to avoid a £526 fee and some delay that may fall away anyway. Our <a href="/calculators/probate-diy-vs-solicitor">DIY probate vs solicitor calculator</a> shows what the process would realistically cost your estate, which is the right baseline before considering anything more elaborate.</p>

<h2>The costs and duties a trust brings with it</h2>

<p>A lifetime trust is not a set-and-forget document. It creates a running legal structure:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Registration.</strong> Most UK express trusts must be registered with HMRC's <a href="https://www.gov.uk/guidance/register-a-trust-as-a-trustee">Trust Registration Service</a>, generally within 90 days of creation, and the register must be kept up to date. Our guide to <a href="/blog/inheritance-tax/trust-registration-service-requirements">Trust Registration Service requirements</a> covers who must register and when.</li>
<li><strong>Inheritance tax on the way in.</strong> Under <a href="https://www.gov.uk/guidance/trusts-and-inheritance-tax">HMRC's trusts and inheritance tax guidance</a>, lifetime transfers into most trusts above the £325,000 nil-rate band attract an immediate 20% charge where the trustees pay.</li>
<li><strong>Inheritance tax while it runs.</strong> Relevant property trusts face charges of up to 6% on each ten-year anniversary and when assets exit. See our explainer on the <a href="/blog/inheritance-tax/inheritance-tax-on-trusts-10-year-charge">10-year charge on trusts</a>.</li>
<li><strong>The reservation-of-benefit trap.</strong> Give your house to a trust and keep living in it rent-free, and HMRC still counts the house as part of your estate for inheritance tax. You have taken on trust costs and lost direct ownership without moving the tax needle.</li>
<li><strong>Trustee obligations.</strong> Trustees owe legal duties to the beneficiaries and may need to file annual tax returns for the trust.</li>
</ul>

<p>This is why trusts sold door-to-door or at seminars as "probate avoidance plans" or "asset protection trusts" deserve real scepticism. Schemes promising to shield your home from care fees or inheritance tax for a four-figure fee have been a recurring source of complaints and mis-selling, and deliberately giving assets away to avoid care costs can be unwound by a local authority as deprivation of assets. A trust that exists only to dodge a £526 fee is a product looking for a problem.</p>

<h2>Simpler routes that already skip probate</h2>

<p>Several ordinary arrangements keep assets out of probate with little or no cost:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Joint ownership.</strong> Homes held as joint tenants and joint bank accounts pass automatically to the survivor. Our guide to <a href="/blog/probate-process/do-you-need-probate-for-jointly-owned-assets">probate and jointly owned assets</a> explains the survivorship rules.</li>
<li><strong>Life insurance written in trust.</strong> A policy written in trust pays the beneficiaries directly, outside the estate, usually within weeks. See <a href="/blog/inheritance-tax/life-insurance-written-in-trust-explained">life insurance written in trust explained</a>.</li>
<li><strong>Pension death benefits.</strong> Most pension schemes pay death benefits at the trustees' discretion under your nomination form, without a grant.</li>
<li><strong>Small balances.</strong> Banks release modest sole-name balances without probate under their own thresholds, so a small estate may need no grant at all.</li>
</ul>

<p>For many families, checking these first answers the question. If everything of value passes by survivorship, nomination or an insurance trust, probate may barely feature, no new trust required.</p>

<h2>When a trust genuinely earns its place</h2>

<p>None of this means trusts are bad tools. They solve real problems that neither a simple will nor joint ownership can: providing for a disabled or vulnerable beneficiary over decades, protecting a share of the home for children from a first marriage (a <a href="/blog/inheritance-tax/property-protection-trust-in-a-will">property protection trust in a will</a> does this), holding assets for minors, or keeping a business running while an estate is sorted out. In those cases probate avoidance is a side effect, not the point, and the costs are being paid for something of substance.</p>

<p>The practical test is simple. Write down the problem you are trying to solve. If the honest answer is "probate seems expensive and slow", price the actual probate first with the <a href="/calculators/probate-diy-vs-solicitor">DIY vs solicitor calculator</a> and read our guides on <a href="/blog/probate-process/do-you-need-a-solicitor-for-probate">whether you need a solicitor for probate</a> and <a href="/blog/probate-process/probate-solicitor-cost">what probate solicitors charge</a>. If the answer is a genuine planning need, a second marriage, a vulnerable child, a business, that is when a trust discussion is worth having. An independent estate planning solicitor, one you choose rather than one selling a package, can tell you whether a trust fits your situation and what it will really cost over its lifetime.</p>
