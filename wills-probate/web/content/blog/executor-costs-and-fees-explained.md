<h2>The estate pays, but the executor pays first</h2>

<p>An executor in England and Wales is not expected to fund an estate out of their own savings. Costs properly incurred in administering it come back out of the estate, and the entitlement is statutory: <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2000/29/section/31">section 31 of the Trustee Act 2000</a> entitles a trustee to be reimbursed from the fund for expenses properly incurred, and section 35 applies that to personal representatives.</p>

<p>Two things stop that being the end of the story. Timing: the court fee is payable before the grant is issued, and the deceased's accounts are usually frozen until the grant exists. And liability: where an executor gets the administration wrong, the cost of the mistake does not automatically belong to the estate. That is the real financial exposure of the role. Our <a href="/calculators/probate-cost-calculator">probate cost calculator</a> gives you a figure for your own estate in about two minutes. This article covers England and Wales and is general information rather than legal or financial advice.</p>

<h2>The gap between paying and being repaid</h2>

<p>Reimbursement is a right, not a mechanism. Nothing pays you until there is estate money to pay you from, and the grant is what unlocks it. Executors bridge the gap three ways. Many banks release funds directly to a third party for probate fees and funeral costs before the grant on sight of the invoice, the cheapest route where it is available (see <a href="/blog/probate-process/paying-for-a-funeral-from-the-estate-before-probate">paying for a funeral from the estate</a>). Where inheritance tax is due, the <a href="/blog/inheritance-tax/paying-inheritance-tax-before-probate-direct-payment-scheme">direct payment scheme</a> pays the tax straight from the deceased's accounts. Failing both, some executors borrow, personally or through an <a href="/blog/probate-process/probate-and-inheritance-advance-loans">estate advance product</a>, which is a regulated credit decision with its own costs.</p>

<p>Whichever route you take, keep receipts from day one. Residuary beneficiaries are entitled to see how the estate's money was spent, and an expense you cannot evidence is one you may end up carrying. Our guide to <a href="/blog/executors/estate-accounts-for-executors">estate accounts</a> shows what that record looks like.</p>

<h2>Where the cost stops being the estate's</h2>

<p>This is the part that separates executor costs from probate costs generally. The estate bears the price of doing the job. The executor can bear the price of doing it wrong.</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Late inheritance tax.</strong> Tax is due by the end of the sixth month after death, per <a href="https://www.gov.uk/paying-inheritance-tax">GOV.UK guidance on paying inheritance tax</a>, and HMRC charges <a href="https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/rates-and-allowances-hmrc-interest-rates-for-late-and-early-payments/rates-and-allowances-hmrc-interest-rates">late payment interest, 7.75% since 9 January 2026</a>. It accrues against the estate, but it is an avoidable loss beneficiaries can ask about.</li>
<li><strong>A late account.</strong> Personal representatives must deliver an inheritance tax account within twelve months of the end of the month of death under <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1984/51/section/216">section 216 of the Inheritance Tax Act 1984</a>. <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1984/51/section/245">Section 245</a> sets penalties of £100, a further £100 if the failure passes six months, and up to £3,000 beyond a year where tax was payable.</li>
<li><strong>Distributing before creditors are flushed out.</strong> A notice under <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/Geo5/15-16/19/section/27">section 27 of the Trustee Act 1925</a>, running at least two months, protects the executor against creditors they did not know about. Skip it, pay everything out, and a late claim can land on you. See our guide to <a href="/blog/probate-process/section-27-notice-in-probate-explained">section 27 notices</a>.</li>
<li><strong>Distributing too soon after the grant.</strong> <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1975/63/section/20">Section 20 of the Inheritance (Provision for Family and Dependants) Act 1975</a> protects a personal representative who distributes after six months from the date the grant was first taken out. Before that, a family provision claim leaves the executor exposed.</li>
<li><strong>Assets you should have collected.</strong> <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1984/51/section/204">Section 204 of the Inheritance Tax Act 1984</a> limits liability to the assets a personal representative received, or might have received but for their own neglect or default. The second limb is the sting: overlooking an asset does not cap your liability at what you happened to gather in.</li>
</ul>

<p>None of this makes the role dangerous for a diligent executor. It explains why the sequence matters: value properly, file on time, advertise, wait, then distribute. Our <a href="/blog/executors/executor-duties-and-responsibilities">executor duties guide</a> sets out the order, and the rest of the role sits in our hub <a href="/for/executors">for executors</a>.</p>

<h2>What you cannot claim, and what you can charge</h2>

<p>Time is the cost the estate never reimburses. Weeks of evenings, calls to banks and days off work are real, and none of it is recoverable. Reimbursement covers money laid out, not effort expended. Whether you can go further and charge a fee is a separate question, answered differently for lay and professional executors and shaped by what the will says: our guide to <a href="/blog/executors/executor-fees-uk-what-can-you-charge">executor fees and what you can charge</a> covers the charging clause rules and the Trustee Act position.</p>
