<h2>One word, two very different trusts</h2>

<p>A <strong>declaration of trust</strong> (sometimes called a deed of trust) is a legal document that records who really owns a jointly held property and in what shares. It deals with <em>beneficial</em> ownership, the right to the property's value and income, which can be completely different from the names on the Land Registry title. Because it contains the word "trust", it is routinely muddled with the <a href="/blog/inheritance-tax/property-protection-trust-in-a-will">property protection trust</a>, and the two could hardly do more different jobs. A declaration of trust works <strong>now, while you are alive</strong>: it fixes each owner's share and drives how HMRC taxes the income. A property protection trust sits dormant inside a will and <strong>only springs into life on death</strong>, ring-fencing a share of the home for the next generation. If you are researching one and keep finding the other, this page covers the lifetime document; the sibling guide covers the will trust. What follows is general information about how these arrangements work, not legal or financial advice for your own situation.</p>

<h2>Legal title versus beneficial ownership</h2>

<p>English law splits property ownership into two layers. The <strong>legal title</strong> is what the Land Registry shows: who can sign the transfer deed, who the lender deals with. The <strong>beneficial interest</strong> is who actually owns the value. Joint owners hold the beneficial interest in one of two ways, set out in <a href="https://www.gov.uk/joint-property-ownership">GOV.UK's guidance on joint property ownership</a>:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Joint tenants</strong> have equal rights to the whole property. When one dies, their interest passes automatically to the survivor, and they cannot leave their share by will.</li>
<li><strong>Tenants in common</strong> can own different shares, there is no automatic survivorship, and each owner can pass their share on in their will.</li>
</ul>

<p>A declaration of trust only makes sense for tenants in common, because joint tenants by definition hold everything equally. Couples who bought as joint tenants and now want unequal shares first sever the joint tenancy (a simple notice plus a Land Registry restriction), then execute the declaration setting out the new split: 70:30, 90:10, whatever reflects the reality of who put in what and who is to receive what. The declaration can also record practical terms, such as who repays the mortgage, what happens if one owner wants to sell, and how sale proceeds are divided.</p>

<h2>The 50:50 rule for married couples, and Form 17</h2>

<p>Here is the part that catches most landlords. If you are married or in a civil partnership and hold an income-producing property jointly, HMRC taxes the income <strong>50:50 by default, regardless of your actual shares</strong>. Owning a rental 90:10 changes nothing on its own; each spouse is still assessed on half the rent. HMRC's <a href="https://www.gov.uk/hmrc-internal-manuals/trusts-settlements-and-estates-manual/tsem9842">Trusts, Settlements and Estates Manual at TSEM9842</a> confirms that a valid declaration under the legislation overrides this even split, and that the declaration must be made jointly: if one spouse will not sign, both stay on 50:50.</p>

<p>The mechanism is <a href="https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/income-tax-declaration-of-beneficial-interests-in-joint-property-and-income-17">Form 17, the declaration of beneficial interests in joint property and income</a>. Three rules do most of the work:</p>

<ol>
<li><strong>You can only declare your actual shares.</strong> Form 17 is not a menu. You cannot own a property 50:50 and elect to be taxed 90:10; the underlying beneficial ownership has to genuinely be 90:10 first, which is exactly what the declaration of trust establishes and evidences. The form must be accompanied by proof of the unequal interests, and a declaration or deed of trust is the evidence HMRC's own guidance names.</li>
<li><strong>The 60-day deadline is hard.</strong> Per <a href="https://www.gov.uk/hmrc-internal-manuals/trusts-settlements-and-estates-manual/tsem9860">TSEM9860</a>, the form must reach HMRC within 60 days of the date the last spouse signed it. Arrive on day 61 and the declaration is simply invalid; the couple must sign and submit afresh.</li>
<li><strong>It works forwards, not backwards.</strong> A valid declaration takes effect from the date of the last signature. Income already received under the 50:50 rule stays taxed 50:50, and the declaration covers only the assets listed on it, not property bought later.</li>
</ol>

<p>The declaration then runs until the couple's interests actually change, they separate permanently, or one of them dies. None of this applies to unmarried joint owners: they are taxed on their real beneficial shares automatically, no form required, which is why for them the declaration of trust is about evidence and protection rather than election.</p>

<h2>Worked example: moving rent to the lower-rate spouse</h2>

<p>Sofia and Marcus, married, own a buy-to-let flat producing <strong>£12,000</strong> of taxable rental profit a year. Sofia runs a consultancy and pays income tax at 40%; Marcus works part time and has basic-rate band to spare. They hold the flat as tenants in common but have never documented shares, so HMRC's default applies.</p>

<p>They execute a declaration of trust moving the beneficial ownership to 10:90 in Marcus's favour, then send Form 17 with a copy of the declaration, and it reaches HMRC within the 60-day window. From the date of the last signature the rent follows the real shares, saving the household <strong>£960 a year</strong> at these figures. Two honesty checks matter. The gift has to be real: Marcus genuinely owns 90% of the flat, including 90% of the sale proceeds if they ever sell, not just 90% of the rent. And the numbers only improve while the rate gap exists; if Marcus's other income rises into higher rate, the advantage shrinks.</p>

<h2>The knock-on taxes when you change shares</h2>

<p>Rewriting beneficial ownership is a real transfer of an asset, so three other taxes deserve a look before anyone signs.</p>

<p><strong>Capital gains tax.</strong> Transfers between spouses and civil partners who live together are free of CGT: <a href="https://www.gov.uk/capital-gains-tax/gifts">GOV.UK confirms</a> you do not pay capital gains tax on assets you give or sell to your husband, wife or civil partner. The recipient inherits the original base cost, so the gain is deferred rather than erased. Between <em>unmarried</em> owners the normal rules apply, so gifting a share of a property that has grown in value can crystallise a taxable gain even though no money changes hands.</p>

<p><strong>Stamp duty land tax.</strong> A pure gift of a share, with no cash and no mortgage, triggers no SDLT. But <a href="https://www.gov.uk/guidance/sdlt-transferring-ownership-of-land-or-property">HMRC's guidance on transferring ownership</a> treats taking over responsibility for a slice of an outstanding mortgage as chargeable consideration. If the mortgage debt assumed (plus any cash paid) exceeds the residential SDLT threshold, <a href="https://www.gov.uk/stamp-duty-land-tax/residential-property-rates">currently £125,000</a>, stamp duty is due on it. On heavily mortgaged rental property this catches people who assumed a "paper" transfer was free.</p>

<p><strong>Inheritance tax.</strong> Between spouses, the spouse exemption means the transfer has no IHT cost. Between unmarried owners, a gifted share is a potentially exempt transfer: survive seven years and it leaves your estate, die sooner and it comes back into the reckoning, as our guide to the <a href="/blog/inheritance-tax/7-year-rule-gifts-inheritance-tax">seven-year rule on gifts</a> explains. Just as importantly, holding as tenants in common means your share does <em>not</em> pass automatically to the co-owner when you die. It passes under your will, or under intestacy if you have none. That is the hinge between this document and estate planning: severing a joint tenancy and defining shares is precisely what makes a <a href="/blog/inheritance-tax/property-protection-trust-in-a-will">property protection trust in a will</a> workable, and it is why anyone signing a declaration of trust should check their will says what they now intend. Our <a href="/calculators/making-a-will-checklist">making a will checklist</a> is a quick way to test whether yours keeps up, and the <a href="/inheritance-tax">inheritance tax guide</a> covers how property shares fit into the wider £325,000 nil-rate band picture. Surviving spouses reorganising ownership after a death will find the specific issues collected in our <a href="/for/surviving-spouses">hub for surviving spouses</a>.</p>

<h2>When a declaration of trust earns its place</h2>

<ul>
<li><strong>Married landlords with unequal tax rates</strong>, as in the example above, pairing the declaration with Form 17.</li>
<li><strong>Unmarried couples buying with unequal deposits</strong>, so that a 70:30 contribution is not silently converted into 50:50 by a standard purchase.</li>
<li><strong>Parents helping a child buy</strong>, recording the parent's stake without putting them on the legal title (which can also have SDLT surcharge implications worth taking advice on).</li>
<li><strong>Siblings keeping an inherited property</strong>, fixing shares and exit terms before letting it out.</li>
<li><strong>Business partners or friends co-investing</strong> in a rental, where the document doubles as a mini shareholders' agreement for the property.</li>
</ul>

<p>The document itself is short, but the decisions inside it are not: shares, mortgage liability, sale mechanics, and the interaction with each owner's will all need to line up, and a badly drafted declaration can lock in a tax result nobody wanted. A solicitor or tax adviser who handles co-ownership work can draft the declaration, deal with the severance and Land Registry restriction, and make sure Form 17 lands inside its 60-day window, usually in a single short engagement. Getting the paperwork right once is far cheaper than untangling a disputed share, or a rejected Form 17, years later.</p>
