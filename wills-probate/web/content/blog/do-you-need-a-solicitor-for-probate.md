<p>No, you do not need a solicitor for probate. There is no legal requirement in England and Wales to use one, and <a href="https://www.gov.uk/applying-for-probate">the government's own guidance</a> is written for people applying personally. Executors named in a will, or the closest relatives where there is no will, can apply for the grant in their own name through the online service, and most people now do exactly that: HM Courts and Tribunals Service data shows 82.6% of grant applications were made digitally in January to March 2026.</p>

<p>The honest answer has a second half, though. "You do not need one" is not the same as "you never should use one". For a straightforward estate the application is genuinely manageable, but some estates carry legal and tax complexity where a mistake is expensive and, crucially, where the executor is personally on the hook for it. This guide gives you a plain decision framework: the green flags that say DIY is fine, the red flags that say get help, and the fixed-fee middle path most people have never heard of. Two minutes with our <a href="/calculators/probate-diy-vs-solicitor">DIY vs solicitor probate checker</a> will run your estate through the same framework and give you a personalised steer before you read on.</p>

<h2>What "doing probate yourself" actually involves</h2>

<p>Probate is the legal process of proving the will (or, without a will, obtaining letters of administration) so that banks, HM Land Registry and other institutions will release or transfer the deceased person's assets. Applying personally means you, as the executor or administrator, do the following:</p>

<ol>
<li>Register the death and locate the original will, if there is one.</li>
<li>Value the estate: write to banks, pension providers and insurers, get property valuations, and list debts.</li>
<li>Deal with inheritance tax: check whether the estate is an "excepted estate" (no full account needed) or must file a full <a href="https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/inheritance-tax-inheritance-tax-account-iht400">IHT400 account</a>, and pay any tax due.</li>
<li>Apply for the grant online (or by post) and pay the fee: £526 for estates over £5,000, no fee at or below £5,000, plus £2 for each sealed copy ordered with the application.</li>
<li>Collect in the assets, pay debts and expenses, prepare estate accounts and distribute to the beneficiaries.</li>
</ol>

<p>None of these steps legally requires a solicitor. The application itself is a set of statements of truth about the will, the estate value and your entitlement to apply. Waits vary, but the official statistics show a mean of 6.4 weeks from submission to grant across all channels, and 4.5 weeks for digital applications, in the latest <a href="https://www.gov.uk/government/collections/family-court-statistics-quarterly">Family Court Statistics Quarterly</a> period. Our guide to <a href="/blog/probate-process/how-long-does-probate-take">how long probate takes</a> covers the timeline in full.</p>

<p>Note that this is a separate question from whether probate is needed at all. Small estates and jointly held assets often skip the process entirely; see <a href="/blog/probate-process/do-you-need-probate">do you need probate</a> if you have not yet confirmed that a grant is required.</p>

<h2>Green flags: when DIY probate is a sensible choice</h2>

<p>A personal application is usually straightforward when most of the following are true:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>The estate is an excepted estate.</strong> No full IHT400 inheritance tax account is required, typically because the estate is below the £325,000 nil-rate band, or everything passes to a spouse, civil partner or charity within the limits. You simply report the values as part of the probate application.</li>
<li><strong>There is a valid, uncontested will</strong> naming executors who are willing to act, or a clear intestacy with an obvious closest relative to apply.</li>
<li><strong>The assets are simple and UK-based:</strong> bank accounts, ordinary investments, perhaps a house passing to a beneficiary or being sold on the open market without complications such as a sitting tenant, a disputed boundary or a business attached.</li>
<li><strong>The beneficiaries are adults, findable and cooperative.</strong> Nobody is threatening a claim, nobody is missing, and nobody is a minor whose share needs holding on trust.</li>
<li><strong>The estate is clearly solvent.</strong> Assets comfortably exceed debts, so the order in which you pay things matters less.</li>
<li><strong>You have the time.</strong> A full administration commonly absorbs 40 to 100 hours of letters, calls and form-filling spread over months. Manageable, but real.</li>
</ul>

<p>If that describes your situation, doing it yourself is not a reckless economy. It is what the digital service was built for, and the money saved goes to the beneficiaries rather than to fees.</p>

<h2>Red flags: when specialist help earns its fee</h2>

<p>Any one of the following is a good reason to at least take initial advice, and often to hand the whole matter over:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>A full IHT400 is required.</strong> Estates over the thresholds, or claiming reliefs such as business or agricultural property relief, must file the full account with its schedules. Valuation judgement calls, exemption claims and payment deadlines make this the single most common trigger for professional help, because personal representatives are liable to HMRC for errors. Our <a href="/blog/inheritance-tax/inheritance-tax-threshold-uk">inheritance tax threshold guide</a> explains where the lines sit.</li>
<li><strong>The estate is, or might be, insolvent.</strong> When debts exceed assets, the law prescribes a strict statutory order for paying creditors. Pay the wrong debt first and you can become personally liable for the shortfall.</li>
<li><strong>There is a dispute or a caveat.</strong> If someone questions the will's validity, threatens a claim under the Inheritance (Provision for Family and Dependants) Act 1975, or has lodged a caveat blocking the grant, do not press on alone.</li>
<li><strong>There are foreign assets.</strong> Property or accounts abroad usually need a separate legal process in that country, and interact with UK inheritance tax in ways that need care.</li>
<li><strong>The will creates a trust.</strong> Life interest trusts for a second spouse, trusts for minor children and discretionary trusts all impose ongoing trustee duties and tax registrations that go well beyond obtaining the grant.</li>
<li><strong>Beneficiaries are missing or unknown.</strong> Distributing without properly tracing entitled people leaves you exposed if they appear later.</li>
<li><strong>The deceased made substantial lifetime gifts, or left gifts to charity.</strong> Gifts in the seven years before death must be traced and reported for inheritance tax, and charity beneficiaries routinely scrutinise estate accounts and query valuations and costs, so accuracy matters more than usual.</li>
<li><strong>You are not up to it right now.</strong> Bereavement is a poor time to learn estate administration under deadline pressure. Handing the job over because you are grieving, unwell or simply stretched is a legitimate reason, not a failure.</li>
</ul>

<h2>The middle path: grant-only fixed-fee help</h2>

<p>The choice is not binary. Many firms and probate specialists offer a grant-only service: you gather the estate information, they prepare the legal statements and any tax forms, submit the application and hand you the grant. You then do the collection, payment and distribution work yourself. Fixed fees for grant-only work are typically a few hundred pounds up to around £1,500 depending on whether an IHT400 is involved, against £1,000 to £5,000 or more for full administration. It suits people who are organised and willing, but want the one genuinely legal step done by someone who does it every week.</p>

<p>The professional does not have to be a solicitor either. Probate work is also carried out by licensed probate practitioners, some chartered accountants and trust corporations, and many advisers hold STEP (Society of Trust and Estate Practitioners) membership. The things to check are the same regardless of title: that the firm is regulated for probate, insured, and clear about its fees up front.</p>

<h2>The personal liability point, in plain terms</h2>

<p>The strongest argument for caution is not convenience. It is that executors and administrators are <strong>personally liable</strong> for the proper administration of the estate. If you distribute everything and an unpaid tax bill, an overlooked creditor or a missed beneficiary surfaces afterwards, they can claim against you personally, not just against the estate you no longer hold. Protections exist, such as advertising for creditors under <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/Geo5/15-16/19/section/27">section 27 of the Trustee Act 1925</a> before distributing, but knowing which protections to use is itself the kind of judgement that separates a simple estate from one that needs advice.
