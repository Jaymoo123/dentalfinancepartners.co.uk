<h2>Is a RICS valuation compulsory? No. Is it optional in practice? That depends on the estate</h2>

<p>No law requires executors to commission a RICS surveyor before applying for probate. What the law does require, under <a href="https://www.gov.uk/hmrc-internal-manuals/inheritance-tax-manual/ihtm09703">section 160 of the Inheritance Tax Act 1984</a>, is that every asset in the estate is valued at the price it might reasonably be expected to fetch if sold on the open market on the date of death. For most estates, the house is the single biggest number in that calculation, and it is the number HMRC is most likely to look at.</p>

<p>So the honest answer splits in two. Where the estate is clearly nowhere near an inheritance tax bill, an estate agent's appraisal, or even a careful comparison of recent local sales, is usually accepted. Where tax is in play, or could be, a formal RICS Red Book valuation is the evidence that protects the executors. This guide explains where that line falls, what a professional valuation costs, and what an under-cooked figure can end up costing instead. It is general information, not legal or financial advice, and an estate with anything unusual in it deserves proper professional input.</p>

<h2>The standard every valuation has to meet</h2>

<p>GOV.UK's guidance on <a href="https://www.gov.uk/valuing-estate-of-someone-who-died/estimate-estate-value">estimating an estate's value</a> asks personal representatives to work out what each asset would have sold for on the date the person died. For land and buildings, HMRC's compliance guidance at <a href="https://www.gov.uk/hmrc-internal-manuals/inheritance-tax-manual/ihtm36275">IHTM36275</a> spells out what a defensible figure looks like: it says it is "entirely appropriate" for taxpayers to obtain a professional opinion of value, that the valuer should be properly instructed to value on the open market basis in section 160, and that the valuer should consider development potential, not just bricks and mortar as they stand.</p>

<p>That last point matters more than people expect. A large garden with space for a plot, a loft that neighbouring houses have converted, or a paddock beside a village boundary can all add value that an asking-price appraisal ignores. HMRC expects the date-of-death figure to reflect it.</p>

<p>A practical middle path on borderline estates is to obtain two or three agent appraisals and return a defensible figure supported by all of them, keeping the written appraisals on file. But once the estate is taxable, or within touching distance of being taxable, the free option stops being cheap, for reasons the worked example below puts numbers on.</p>

<h2>When an estate agent estimate is usually enough</h2>

<ul>
<li><strong>Everything passes to a surviving spouse or civil partner.</strong> The spouse exemption means no inheritance tax on the first death, so there is no tax figure for HMRC to challenge, though a sensible record of the value still helps with the transferable allowances later.</li>
<li><strong>The estate is comfortably below the £325,000 nil-rate band</strong>, or below the combined allowances where the home passes to children and the residence nil-rate band applies. See our <a href="/inheritance-tax">inheritance tax guide</a> for how the thresholds stack.</li>
<li><strong>The property is a small share of a clearly excepted estate</strong>, where no full IHT400 account is needed. Our <a href="/blog/inheritance-tax/iht400-vs-iht205-excepted-estates-walkthrough">excepted estates walkthrough</a> explains which reporting route applies.</li>
</ul>

<h2>When a RICS valuation is strongly advisable</h2>

<ul>
<li><strong>The estate is taxable or close to the thresholds.</strong> HMRC's manual at <a href="https://www.gov.uk/hmrc-internal-manuals/inheritance-tax-manual/ihtm23002">IHTM23002</a> confirms that most UK land chargeable to tax on a death is referred to the Valuation Office Agency for professional checking. Your figure will be looked at by a valuer, so it should be produced by one.</li>
<li><strong>The residence nil-rate band or the £2 million taper is in play</strong>, where a modest shift in the house value can move the whole estate's allowances.</li>
<li><strong>The property is unusual</strong>: development potential, agricultural ties, tenants in place, short leases, or disrepair that a drive-by appraisal cannot price.</li>
<li><strong>Beneficiaries may disagree.</strong> An independent surveyor's figure forestalls arguments about whether the house was sold too cheaply to one sibling or valued too high against another's share.</li>
<li><strong>Capital gains tax is on the horizon.</strong> The probate value normally becomes the beneficiaries' base cost, so a low figure now can simply shift tax onto them when they sell. Our guide to <a href="/blog/inheritance-tax/capital-gains-tax-on-inherited-property">capital gains tax on inherited property</a> covers that second leg.</li>
</ul>

<h2>What it costs, in context</h2>

<p>Quotes for a standard residential Red Book probate valuation typically run <strong>£200 to £600</strong>, rising for large, tenanted or unusual properties. (That range reflects the market rather than any official tariff, so obtain quotes locally.) The fee is an estate administration expense, paid from estate funds. Set it against the other fixed costs of administration: the probate application fee in England and Wales is <a href="https://www.gov.uk/applying-for-probate/fees">£526 for estates over £5,000</a>, with no fee below that and extra copies of the grant at £2 each when ordered with the application. Our <a href="/calculators/probate-cost-calculator">probate cost calculator</a> puts the whole picture together, and our guides to <a href="/blog/probate-process/how-much-does-a-probate-valuation-cost">probate valuation costs</a> and <a href="/blog/probate-process/how-much-does-probate-cost">the overall cost of probate</a> break the numbers down further.</p>

<h2>Worked example: the £400,000 guess that cost £27,000</h2>

<p>Two brothers, Daniel and Marcus, are executors of their late father's estate in Bristol. The estate is the family home plus £320,000 of savings and investments, with everything split between the two of them. A local agent walks through the house and suggests "around £400,000 for a quick sale". The brothers return £400,000 on the IHT400. With a £325,000 nil-rate band and a £175,000 residence nil-rate band, the reported £720,000 estate produces tax of £88,000, which they pay.</p>

<p>HMRC refers the property to the Valuation Office Agency. The district valuer finds three comparable sales in the same street and notes the wide plot with side access that two neighbours have already built on. After negotiation, the date-of-death value is agreed at <strong>£460,000</strong>. The consequences:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Extra inheritance tax:</strong> £60,000 more estate at 40% = <strong>£24,000</strong>.</li>
<li><strong>Late payment interest:</strong> HMRC's late payment rate has been <a href="https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/rates-and-allowances-hmrc-interest-rates-for-late-and-early-payments/rates-and-allowances-hmrc-interest-rates">7.75% since 9 January 2026</a> (base rate plus 4%). Nine months of interest on £24,000 is roughly <strong>£1,395</strong>.</li>
<li><strong>Possible penalty:</strong> if HMRC decides the low figure came from a failure to take reasonable care, the penalty for a careless inaccuracy is <a href="https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/compliance-checks-penalties-for-inaccuracies-in-returns-or-documents-ccfs7a/compliance-checks-for-penalties-of-inaccuracies-in-returns-or-documents-ccfs7a-factsheet">up to 30% of the extra tax</a>, here up to £7,200. Relying on a single verbal quick-sale figure for a house with obvious development potential is exactly the fact pattern that invites the question.</li>
</ul>

<p>A £350 Red Book valuation would have produced a defensible figure at the outset, and even if the VOA had still negotiated it upward, a properly instructed professional valuation is the classic evidence of reasonable care that keeps penalties off the table. The extra tax on a genuinely higher value is owed either way; the interest and the penalty exposure are what the surveyor's fee buys off.</p>

<h2>More than one property? Each one needs its own figure</h2>

<p>Estates with a buy-to-let, an inherited half-share in a relative's house or a holiday home need a date-of-death value for <strong>each</strong> property, and the shortcuts that just about work for a single family home scale badly:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Tenanted property is not valued like vacant property.</strong> A sitting tenant, especially on a regulated or long tenancy, can reduce open market value materially, and only a surveyor can put a defensible discount on it.</li>
<li><strong>Jointly held investment property</strong> may attract a deduction for the undivided half-share, which again needs professional support rather than a percentage plucked from a forum.</li>
<li><strong>Holiday homes sit in markets executors often do not know.</strong> A Cornish agent's view of a Cornish cottage beats a London executor's guess.</li>
<li><strong>Portfolios draw scrutiny.</strong> The more land value in the estate, the more likely a VOA referral covers all of it, so one weak figure can put every figure under the microscope.</li>
</ul>

<p>The property is also only one line of the estate. For the full method across bank accounts, shares, and household contents, see <a href="/blog/inheritance-tax/how-to-value-an-estate-for-probate">how to value an estate for probate</a> and our companion piece on <a href="/blog/probate-process/valuing-personal-possessions-chattels-for-probate">valuing personal possessions for probate</a>.</p>

<h2>Deciding which way to go</h2>

<p>The decision is a risk calculation, not a rule. If no inheritance tax is conceivable, save the money. If tax is possible, the surveyor's fee is the cheapest insurance in the whole administration. And if you are unsure which side of that line the estate falls, that uncertainty is itself the signal: a probate specialist can look at the asset list, tell you whether the estate needs a Red Book valuation or an agent's letter, and take the reporting off your hands before any figure is committed to HMRC. Getting the valuation question right at the start is far easier than renegotiating it with the district valuer later.</p>
