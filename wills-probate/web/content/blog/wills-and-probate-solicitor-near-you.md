<p>If you have typed "solicitor for wills near me" into a search engine, here is the honest answer up front: for most wills and probate work in 2026, "near me" matters far less than it used to. Probate applications go to a central HM Courts and Tribunals Service system, 82.6% of them are now made digitally, and wills can be drafted and approved by post, phone and video call. What actually matters is finding a genuine specialist, with transparent fees and the right accreditation, and that specialist can be in your town or three counties away.</p>

<p>That said, there are real situations where a local firm earns its keep, and there are firms near you and far from you that you should avoid. This guide, which applies to England and Wales, walks through what a wills and probate solicitor actually does, when local genuinely helps, how to compare firms properly, the red flags, the questions to ask in a first call, and the alternatives, including doing it yourself. If you are not yet sure whether you need a professional at all, two minutes with our <a href="/calculators/probate-diy-vs-solicitor">DIY vs solicitor checker</a> will tell you which parts of the job you can safely handle and which are worth paying for.</p>

<h2>What "wills and probate solicitor" actually covers</h2>

<p>The phrase bundles two related but distinct jobs, and it helps to know which one you are shopping for.</p>

<h3>Planning work (while you are alive)</h3>

<ul>
<li><strong>Wills.</strong> Drafting a legally valid will, advising on guardianship for children, and structuring gifts to reduce inheritance tax. See our <a href="/wills">wills hub</a> for what a good will covers.</li>
<li><strong>Lasting powers of attorney (LPAs).</strong> Documents that let people you trust make decisions for you if you lose capacity. The Office of the Public Guardian charges <a href="https://www.gov.uk/power-of-attorney/register">£92 per LPA to register</a>. Our <a href="/lasting-power-of-attorney">LPA guide</a> covers the process.</li>
<li><strong>Trusts and inheritance tax planning.</strong> Advice on the £325,000 nil-rate band, the £175,000 residence nil-rate band and lifetime gifting. Our <a href="/inheritance-tax">inheritance tax hub</a> explains the framework.</li>
</ul>

<h3>Administration work (after a death)</h3>

<ul>
<li>Applying for the <a href="https://www.gov.uk/applying-for-probate">grant of probate</a>, or letters of administration where there is no will.</li>
<li>Valuing the estate and completing inheritance tax reporting to HMRC.</li>
<li>Collecting in assets, paying debts and distributing the estate to beneficiaries.</li>
</ul>

<p>Many firms let you buy these separately: a "grant-only" service where they obtain the grant and you do the rest, or full administration where they run the whole estate. Which one you need is exactly the question our sibling guide on <a href="/blog/probate-process/what-does-a-probate-solicitor-do">what a probate solicitor does</a> answers in depth, and the cost difference is significant, as covered in our guide to <a href="/blog/probate-process/probate-solicitor-cost">probate solicitor costs</a>.</p>

<h2>Local vs remote: why most probate work no longer needs a high-street office</h2>

<p>Probate in England and Wales is administered centrally. There is no local probate registry counter to visit any more, and the official statistics show how digital the process has become: in the latest <a href="https://www.gov.uk/government/collections/family-court-statistics-quarterly">Family Court Statistics Quarterly</a> (January to March 2026), 82.6% of probate applications were made digitally, and those digital grants were issued in a mean of 4.5 weeks. A specialist firm working remotely uses exactly the same system as the firm on your high street.</p>

<p>In practice, remote instruction means documents by post and secure email, identity checks done online, and meetings by phone or video. Original wills and signed forms still travel by post, but that is true whether the firm is one mile or two hundred miles away.</p>

<h3>When local genuinely matters</h3>

<ul>
<li><strong>There is a property to deal with.</strong> Someone needs to check, secure, insure and eventually clear the deceased's home, and accompany estate agents or surveyors. A local firm (or a local executor) makes this far easier.</li>
<li><strong>Home visits.</strong> If the person making a will or an LPA has mobility problems, is in hospital or a care home, or there are any concerns about mental capacity, a solicitor who can visit in person, assess capacity properly and witness signing is genuinely valuable.</li>
<li><strong>Signing formalities.</strong> Wills must be signed in the physical presence of two witnesses. Firms often host signing appointments, which is simpler locally.</li>
<li><strong>You simply prefer face-to-face.</strong> A perfectly good reason. Bereavement is hard, and some people want a person in a room, not a voice on a call.</li>
</ul>

<p>If none of those apply, widen your search to the whole of England and Wales and pick on quality and price rather than postcode.</p>

<h2>Where to actually search</h2>

<p>Rather than relying on whatever a search engine surfaces first, start with sources that only list regulated providers:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>The Law Society's Find a Solicitor service.</strong> A free public search of solicitors in England and Wales regulated by the Solicitors Regulation Authority (SRA). You can filter by area of law (wills and probate) and by location if local matters to you.</li>
<li><strong>The SRA's public register.</strong> Whatever route brings you to a firm, check it here before instructing. Every genuine firm of solicitors appears on the register with its SRA number, and the firm will tell you that number without hesitation if asked.</li>
<li><strong>Personal recommendations.</strong> Friends, family and other professionals (an accountant or financial adviser, for example) who have used a firm for the same type of work. Weigh these the same way as reviews: relevant experience counts, general niceness does not.</li>
<li><strong>Review directories.</strong> Useful for shortlisting, but treat them as a starting point, not a verdict, for the reasons covered below.</li>
</ul>

<p>If your priority is a will rather than probate, note that free and reduced-cost routes exist: Will Aid (each November, solicitors write wills in exchange for a charity donation), Free Wills Month (March and October, generally for those aged 55 and over), many trade unions offer a free will service to members, and some charities fund simple wills in the hope, but not the requirement, of a legacy gift. These schemes use regulated professionals, so the checks in this guide still apply.</p>

<h2>How to compare firms: the three things that matter</h2>

<h3>1. Fixed-fee transparency</h3>

<p>Regulated law firms are required to publish price information for probate services on their websites. Use that. The pricing models you will meet are:</p>

<table>
<tr><th>Pricing model</th><th>Typical use</th><th>Watch for</th></tr>
<tr><td>Fixed fee</td><td>Grant-only applications, simple estates</td><td>What is excluded: IHT forms, property sale, extra beneficiaries</td></tr>
<tr><td>Hourly rate</td><td>Full administration, complex estates</td><td>Ask for a capped or banded estimate, and the hourly rate of who actually does the work</td></tr>
<tr><td>Percentage of estate</td><td>Full administration, often 1% to 5%</td><td>Can be very poor value on a simple but valuable estate (a house and two bank accounts)</td></tr>
</table>

<p>Get at least three quotes in writing, for the same scope, and compare like with like. Our guide to <a href="/blog/probate-process/how-much-does-probate-cost">how much probate costs</a> breaks down what a fair total looks like, including the court's own £526 application fee for estates over £5,000 (no fee at or below £5,000) and £2 for each sealed copy ordered with the application.</p>

<h3>2. Specialist accreditation</h3>

<p>Anyone at a law firm can be given wills and probate files. What you want is evidence of genuine specialisation. Look for membership of recognised specialist accreditation schemes for private client, wills and estates work: these schemes typically require examined qualifications, minimum levels of relevant experience and ongoing training. A firm will usually display these logos and name the accredited individuals. Ask directly: "Who will handle my matter, and what accreditation or specialist qualification do they personally hold?" Also confirm the firm itself is regulated: every legitimate firm will tell you its regulator and registration number without hesitation, and you can verify it on the regulator's public register.</p>

<h3>3. Reviews, but read them properly</h3>

<p>Review scores are noisy for legal work because most clients cannot judge technical quality. Read the text, not the stars, and look for patterns: repeated mentions of slow responses, surprise bills or unreturned calls are meaningful. One-off complaints about outcomes usually are not. Reviews that describe the same type of work you need (estate administration, not conveyancing) carry the most weight.</p>

<h2>Red flags</h2>

<ul>
<li><strong>No published pricing, or a refusal to give a written quote.</strong> Regulated firms must publish probate prices. Evasiveness here predicts evasiveness later.</li>
<li><strong>Percentage fees pushed on a simple estate</strong> without offering a fixed-fee or hourly alternative.</li>
<li><strong>Pressure to appoint the firm as executor</strong> in your will, or to store your will only if you commit to using them later. You can name anyone as executor, and paid professional executors add cost your family may not need.</li>
<li><strong>Cold calls or doorstep approaches</strong> after a death. Reputable firms do not chase the bereaved.</li>
<li><strong>Unregulated "will writing" or "estate services" businesses presenting as solicitors.</strong> Will writing itself is not a reserved legal activity, so check who regulates the business before handing over money or original documents.</li>
<li><strong>Vagueness about who does the work.</strong> A partner quotes; an unsupervised junior does the file. Ask, and get the answer in the engagement letter.</li>
</ul>

<h2>Questions to ask in the first call</h2>

<ol>
<li>Is my estate (or the estate I am administering) one you would class as simple or complex, and why?</li>
<li>Do you offer a grant-only service as well as full administration, and what does each cost, fixed?</li>
<li>What is excluded from that price? What would trigger extra fees?</li>
<li>Who will personally handle the matter, what specialist accreditation do they hold, and who covers when they are away?</li>
<li>How long do you expect the grant to take, and the full administration? (Sense-check the answer against our <a href="/blog/probate-process/how-long-does-probate-take">guide to probate timescales</a>.)</li>
<li>Will there be inheritance tax to report or pay, and is that work included?</li>
<li>How will you keep me updated, and how quickly do you return calls and emails?</li>
<li>Can you work fully remotely, or will anything need an in-person meeting?</li>
</ol>

<p>A good firm answers all eight comfortably. Hesitation on questions 2, 3 and 4 is your cue to keep looking.</p>

<h2>Alternatives to a solicitor</h2>

<p><strong>Licensed probate practitioners.</strong> Probate is a reserved legal activity, but solicitors are not the only professionals authorised to do it. Some accountancy and specialist probate firms are licensed for probate through their own regulators. They can be strong value where the estate's complexity is mainly tax rather than disputes. Verify the authorisation, exactly as you would check a solicitor's regulator.</p>

<p><strong>Doing it yourself.</strong> Thousands of personal representatives apply for probate themselves every quarter using <a href="https://www.gov.uk/applying-for-probate">GOV.UK's online service</a>. If the estate has a valid will, no inheritance tax to pay, ordinary UK assets and no family friction, DIY is realistic, and our sibling guide on <a href="/blog/probate-process/do-you-need-a-solicitor-for-probate">whether you need a solicitor for probate</a> covers the decision in detail. Remember personal representatives are personally liable for errors, so be honest about complexity. The fastest way to test yourself is the <a href="/calculators/probate-diy-vs-solicitor">DIY vs solicitor checker</a>: answer a few questions about the estate and it flags which parts are safe to self-handle.</p>

<p><strong>Check probate is even needed first.</strong> Small estates, and assets passing automatically to a surviving joint owner, often need no grant at all. Run the <a href="/calculators/do-i-need-probate-checker">do I need probate checker</a> before spending anything.</p>
