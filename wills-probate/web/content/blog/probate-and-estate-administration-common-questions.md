<h2>What happens if you don't need probate</h2>

<p>If an estate does not need probate, nothing dramatic happens: the estate is simply administered without a grant. The executor (or the closest relative where there is no will) sends each bank or organisation a copy of the death certificate and its own claim form, jointly owned assets pass straight to the surviving owner, debts and the funeral are paid from the money released, and whatever remains is distributed under the will or the intestacy rules. There is no application to the probate registry, no court fee and no waiting for a grant.</p>

<p>What does not disappear is the work. Someone still has to value the estate, check whether inheritance tax is due, settle liabilities before paying beneficiaries and keep basic records. This page answers the questions families raise most often at that early stage. It is general information about how the process works in England and Wales, not legal or financial advice for your specific situation.</p>

<h2>When is probate not needed?</h2>

<p><a href="https://www.gov.uk/applying-for-probate">GOV.UK's probate guidance</a> says you may not need a grant where the person who died:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>only had savings</strong>, because many banks release modest balances against a death certificate alone;</li>
<li><strong>owned shares or money jointly with others</strong>, which pass automatically to the surviving owners unless they agreed otherwise; or</li>
<li><strong>owned land or property as joint tenants</strong>, which likewise passes automatically to the surviving owner.</li>
</ul>

<p>That is why many married couples find no grant is needed on the first death: the house, the joint account and the joint savings all pass by survivorship. Take two sisters, Priya and Meena, who shared a flat as joint tenants and ran a joint household account. When Priya died, Meena became sole owner of both by survivorship; the only sole-name asset was a current account of a few thousand pounds, which the bank released against the death certificate. No grant, no fee, no registry.</p>

<p>The catch is that <strong>every organisation has its own rules</strong>. One bank might release £20,000 without a grant while another insists on one for a fraction of that, so you must ask each holder of assets individually rather than rely on a single threshold. Joint accounts have their own wrinkles (including how HMRC views who really owned the money), covered in our guide to <a href="/blog/probate-process/joint-bank-accounts-and-survivorship-after-death">joint bank accounts and survivorship</a>.</p>

<h2>Do you still have to value the estate and deal with inheritance tax?</h2>

<p>Yes, valuation is not optional. You need to establish what the estate is worth to know whether inheritance tax applies, whichever way the assets pass. The helpful concession is on reporting: under <a href="https://www.gov.uk/valuing-estate-of-someone-who-died/check-type-of-estate">GOV.UK's excepted-estates rules</a>, if the estate counts as an excepted estate and you are not applying for probate, you do not need to report its value at all. Most estates below the £325,000 nil-rate band, or left entirely to a spouse, civil partner or charity, fall into this category. If tax is due, the estate must be reported to HMRC on form IHT400 within a year of the death, grant or no grant.</p>

<h2>Who actually runs things when there is no grant?</h2>

<p>The same people who would otherwise apply. A will's executors are still the ones with authority to carry out its terms; without a will, the closest relative typically steps in. Their core duties do not change: collect the assets, pay debts and expenses first, then distribute to the right beneficiaries. Skipping the grant removes a court process, not the responsibility. If the estate later turns out to need a grant after all (a forgotten shareholding, a sole-name property), the administration simply pauses while one is obtained.</p>

<h2>And if you do need probate: fee and timescale at a glance</h2>

<p>Where a grant is required, the England and Wales application fee is <strong>£526 for estates over £5,000</strong>; estates of £5,000 or less pay nothing, and extra copies of the grant cost £2 each when ordered with the application (£16 afterwards), per <a href="https://www.gov.uk/applying-for-probate/fees">GOV.UK's fees page</a>. A help-with-fees scheme exists for applicants on low incomes or certain benefits. Once submitted, <a href="https://www.gov.uk/applying-for-probate/after-youve-applied">GOV.UK says</a> you will usually receive the grant within 12 weeks, longer if the registry needs more information. Our guide to <a href="/blog/probate-process/how-long-does-probate-take">how long probate takes</a> breaks down the full timeline, and if you are at the very start of the process, begin with <a href="/blog/probate-process/registering-a-death-step-by-step">registering the death</a> before anything else.</p>

<h2>Where to go from here</h2>

<p>Start by listing every asset and how it was owned, then ask each organisation what it needs. If the answer everywhere is "just the death certificate", you can administer the estate without a grant and keep the £526 in the estate. If even one organisation or a sole-name property demands a grant, our <a href="/probate">probate pillar guide</a> maps out the application route. Estates with unclear ownership, disputed wills or tax at stake are worth a conversation with a probate specialist before money moves anywhere; getting the order of payments right at the beginning is far cheaper than untangling it later.</p>
