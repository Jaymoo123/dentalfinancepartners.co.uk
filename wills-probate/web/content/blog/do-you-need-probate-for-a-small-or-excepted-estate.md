<h2>Three questions people ask as one</h2>

<p>"Is the estate too small for probate?" is three separate questions wearing one coat, with three different answers. Whether you need a grant is decided by the organisations holding the assets, one at a time. Whether inheritance tax is due is decided by the £325,000 nil-rate band. Whether the estate is <strong>excepted</strong> is decided by HMRC conditions that govern what paperwork goes with the application. None of the three controls the others, which is why a £231,000 estate can be comfortably excepted for tax and still need a grant before anyone can touch the main asset.</p>

<p>"Small" and "excepted" are not get-out-of-probate cards. This guide separates the three tests, works an estate through all of them, and sets out what the application costs when the estate is modest. For a quick steer first, our <a href="/calculators/do-i-need-probate-checker">do I need probate checker</a> asks what the estate contains and tells you whether a grant is likely. Everything here covers England and Wales and is general information, not legal or financial advice.</p>

<h2>There is no small estate threshold, so stop hunting for one</h2>

<p>Some jurisdictions publish a figure below which an estate can be settled without a court process. England and Wales does not. <a href="https://www.gov.uk/applying-for-probate">GOV.UK's probate guidance</a> puts the burden on the executor: contact the financial organisations the person used, because "every organisation has its own rules". That is the whole position on small estates, and it is why no two families get the same answer.</p>

<p>Each bank and building society sets an internal release limit, commonly between £5,000 and £50,000, below which it pays out against a death certificate and a signed indemnity. Those limits are policy rather than law, they differ by institution, and they apply to the total held with that provider rather than per account. Our companion guide on <a href="/blog/probate-process/do-you-need-probate-for-bank-accounts-isas-and-life-insurance">probate for bank accounts, ISAs and life insurance</a> works through how the individual providers handle it.</p>

<p>Two things override the arithmetic entirely:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Property in the deceased's sole name.</strong> The Land Registry will not register a transfer or sale without a grant, whatever the property is worth. A £140,000 ex-council flat triggers probate as surely as a £1.4 million house. The same goes for a share held as tenants in common, covered in our guide to <a href="/blog/probate-process/do-you-need-probate-to-sell-or-transfer-a-property">selling or transferring a property</a>.</li>
<li><strong>Certificated shares in the deceased's own name.</strong> Registrars want formal proof of authority regardless of value, which is how a forgotten privatisation shareholding worth a few thousand pounds drags an otherwise grant-free estate into the process.</li>
</ul>

<p>Running the other way, anything passing by survivorship to a surviving <a href="/blog/probate-process/do-you-need-probate-for-jointly-owned-assets">joint owner</a> sits outside the estate for probate purposes from the moment of death. For the full asset by asset method, see <a href="/blog/probate-process/how-to-know-if-you-need-probate">how to know if you need probate</a>.</p>

<h2>"Excepted estate" is a tax label, not a probate exemption</h2>

<p>An excepted estate is simply one HMRC has decided it does not need a full account for. It says nothing about whether a court grant is required.</p>

<p>Before 2022, estates with no tax to pay filed the short form IHT205. That form was abolished for deaths on or after <strong>1 January 2022</strong>, and since then an excepted estate files no inheritance tax return at all. The executor calculates three figures, the gross value, the net value and the net qualifying value for inheritance tax, and enters them on the probate application itself (PA1P where there is a will, PA1A where there is not). Per <a href="https://www.gov.uk/valuing-estate-of-someone-who-died/check-type-of-estate">GOV.UK's guidance on checking the type of estate</a>, where no probate is needed "you do not need to report the value of an excepted estate" at all.</p>

<p>There are three excepted categories:</p>

<ol>
<li><strong>Low value estates.</strong> Gross value at or below the nil-rate band of <strong>£325,000</strong>, or up to <strong>£650,000</strong> where a predeceased spouse or civil partner's threshold transfers across in full.</li>
<li><strong>Exempt estates.</strong> Gross value under <strong>£3 million</strong>, with the net chargeable value within the nil-rate band once spouse, civil partner and charity exemptions come off.</li>
<li><strong>Non-resident estates.</strong> UK assets of <strong>£150,000</strong> or less where the deceased was not a long-term UK resident.</li>
</ol>

<p>The conditions underneath matter more than the headline figures, and they are why genuinely modest estates sometimes fail. HMRC's Inheritance Tax Manual at <a href="https://www.gov.uk/hmrc-internal-manuals/inheritance-tax-manual/ihtm06012">IHTM06012</a> (low value) and <a href="https://www.gov.uk/hmrc-internal-manuals/inheritance-tax-manual/ihtm06013">IHTM06013</a> (exempt) require all of the following:</p>

<ul>
<li>Specified transfers (broadly, chargeable lifetime gifts in the seven years before death) with a chargeable value of no more than <strong>£250,000</strong>.</li>
<li>Foreign assets with a gross value of no more than <strong>£100,000</strong>.</li>
<li>Any trust assets held in a single trust: gross value within £250,000 for a low value estate, or £1 million for an exempt estate provided the net chargeable value stays within £250,000.</li>
<li>No gift with reservation of benefit, the classic case being a home signed over to the children while the parent lived on there rent free.</li>
</ul>

<p>One wording change matters if the death was recent: for deaths on or after <strong>6 April 2025</strong>, IHTM06012 applies a long-term UK residence test in place of the old domicile test. For the full decision path between excepted status and the IHT400 account, including the residence nil-rate band trap, see our <a href="/blog/inheritance-tax/iht400-vs-iht205-excepted-estates-walkthrough">IHT400 versus IHT205 walkthrough</a>.</p>

<h2>Worked example: £231,000, excepted for tax, probate still required</h2>

<p>Bernadette, a retired peripatetic music teacher, dies at 74. She never married and had no children, and her will appoints her neighbour Caroline as sole executor, splitting everything between two nieces and a brass band charity. Her estate:</p>

<table>
<thead>
<tr><th>Asset</th><th>Value</th><th>Grant needed?</th></tr>
</thead>
<tbody>
<tr><td>Flat in her sole name</td><td>£215,000</td><td>Yes, always</td></tr>
<tr><td>Building society account</td><td>£4,300</td><td>No, well under the limit</td></tr>
<tr><td>Current account</td><td>£1,900</td><td>No</td></tr>
<tr><td>Premium Bonds</td><td>£3,000</td><td>No</td></tr>
<tr><td>Car and household contents</td><td>£6,800</td><td>No</td></tr>
<tr><td><strong>Gross estate</strong></td><td><strong>£231,000</strong></td><td><strong>Yes, because of the flat</strong></td></tr>
</tbody>
</table>

<p>Run the three tests separately:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Probate test:</strong> every financial asset sits under its provider's release limit, so on the money alone Caroline would need nothing. The flat decides it. She needs a grant.</li>
<li><strong>Tax test:</strong> £231,000 is well below £325,000, so no inheritance tax is due, and the charitable legacy reduces the chargeable estate further.</li>
<li><strong>Excepted test:</strong> gross value under the nil-rate band, gifts of about £6,000 to the nieces, no foreign assets, no trusts, no gift with reservation. Excepted, so no IHT forms.</li>
</ul>

<p>Caroline's route is the simplest available: value everything, put the three inheritance tax figures on the PA1P, pay <strong>£526</strong>, wait. No HMRC account, no unique reference code, no tax to fund. What she cannot do is skip the grant, because no conveyancer will take the flat to exchange without it. Tax-free and form-free but still court-bound is the most common shape of small estate in England and Wales, and it is exactly what "too small for probate" gets wrong.</p>

<h2>The mirror case, and the trap inside it</h2>

<p>Flip the facts and the answers flip too. Marek, in his fifties, dies with no property, £46,000 spread across three banks (nothing at any one near its release limit) and a workplace death in service benefit paid at the scheme's discretion to his partner. No institution asks for a grant, so there is no application, no fee, and no report to HMRC.</p>

<p>The trap sits one step past that. <strong>Being under the tax threshold and being excepted are not the same thing.</strong> Had Marek gifted a sibling £300,000 four years earlier, the estate would breach the £250,000 specified transfers limit and need a full IHT400 account delivered to HMRC within 12 months of the death, even though no grant is required and quite possibly no tax is payable once the allowances are applied. The reporting duty survives the absence of probate, so check the seven-year gift history before concluding there is nothing to do. Our guide to <a href="/blog/inheritance-tax/inheritance-tax-threshold-uk">the UK inheritance tax threshold</a> explains how the allowances interact.</p>

<h2>What the application costs on a small estate</h2>

<p>The fee does not scale with the estate, which makes it proportionally heaviest at the bottom end. Per <a href="https://www.gov.uk/applying-for-probate/fees">GOV.UK's probate fees page</a>:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>£526</strong> where the estate is worth more than £5,000, online or on paper, personally or through a professional. The fee rose from £300 on 13 July 2026 under the <a href="https://www.gov.uk/government/news/court-and-tribunal-fees-updates-from-july-2026">Ministry of Justice's July 2026 fee update</a>.</li>
<li><strong>No fee at all</strong> where the estate is £5,000 or less.</li>
<li><strong>£2 per extra sealed copy</strong> ordered with the application. Copies requested afterwards cost £16 each, so order enough up front for every institution at once.</li>
<li><strong>£22</strong> for a second application where a grant has already been issued.</li>
</ul>

<p>A <a href="https://www.gov.uk/get-help-with-court-fees">Help with Fees scheme</a> operates across court and tribunal fees, assessed on income, savings and certain benefits. Beyond the fee, a small excepted estate is the cheapest kind to administer: no IHT400, no schedules, often no professional valuation. Our breakdown of <a href="/blog/probate-process/how-much-does-probate-cost">what probate costs</a> covers the optional extras.</p>

<h2>Where small estates go wrong</h2>

<ol>
<li><strong>Treating "no tax due" as "excepted".</strong> Gifts, trusts, foreign assets and a gift with reservation each break the excepted route on estates that owe nothing.</li>
<li><strong>Undervaluing to stay under a line.</strong> HMRC can enquire into an excepted estate after the grant issues, so keep the workings from <a href="/blog/inheritance-tax/how-to-value-an-estate-for-probate">valuing the estate</a>.</li>
<li><strong>Forgetting the residence nil-rate band cannot be claimed on the excepted route.</strong> If a home passes to children or grandchildren and that allowance is needed, the estate takes the IHT400 path whatever its size.</li>
</ol>
