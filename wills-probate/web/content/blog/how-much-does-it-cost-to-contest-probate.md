<h2>From £4 upwards: why there is no single price</h2>

<p>Contesting probate in England and Wales has no fixed cost. The only standard, government-published fee at the very start is the <strong>£4 caveat fee</strong>, which stops a grant of probate being issued while you investigate; everything after that depends on how far the dispute travels and how it is funded. A disagreement resolved by a few solicitors' letters sits at one end of the scale; a fully fought court claim with expert evidence sits at the other, and the gap between the two is enormous. That is why reputable firms quote ranges and stages rather than a single number.</p>

<p>This article explains what drives the bill, how claims are commonly funded, and who ends up paying. It covers England and Wales, and it is general information rather than legal or financial advice. For the grounds on which a will or grant can be challenged, and the process itself, see our full guide to <a href="/blog/probate-process/how-to-contest-a-will-in-the-uk">how to contest a will in the UK</a>; this page stays focused on the money.</p>

<h2>The £4 caveat: the cheapest step, and often the first</h2>

<p>If you believe there is a genuine problem, perhaps a dispute about who should apply for probate, or doubt about whether a valid will exists, you can apply to <a href="https://www.gov.uk/stop-probate-application">stop a probate application</a> by entering a caveat. It costs <strong>£4</strong>, lasts <strong>6 months</strong>, and can be extended for a further 6 months. While it is in place, no grant of probate can be issued on the estate, which buys time to investigate without the estate being distributed underneath you.</p>

<p>Two cautions belong next to that low price. A caveat is a pause button, not a claim: it resolves nothing by itself, and the real costs begin with what you do during the pause. And a caveat entered without reasonable grounds can backfire; if the other side challenges it, the court can order you to pay the costs the delay caused. Our overview of <a href="/blog/probate-process/probate-disputes-and-caveats-an-overview">probate disputes and caveats</a> explains how the caveat process unfolds, including warnings and appearances.</p>

<h2>What actually drives the cost</h2>

<p>Five factors do most of the work in determining the final bill:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>How far the dispute goes.</strong> The staged escalation, an exchange of letters, then negotiation, then mediation, then court proceedings, is also a cost escalator. Most contested estates settle before trial, and each stage avoided saves substantially.</li>
<li><strong>The evidence the claim needs.</strong> Challenges based on mental capacity typically involve obtaining medical records and sometimes a retrospective expert report. Forgery allegations may need a handwriting expert. Undue influence claims lean on witness evidence. Each expert, record request and statement adds time and fees.</li>
<li><strong>How the other side responds.</strong> A dispute where the executors or beneficiaries engage constructively costs far less than one where every point is fought. You only control one side of that equation.</li>
<li><strong>Court fees and disbursements.</strong> Issuing a claim carries a court fee, and fees for money claims are banded by the amount in dispute. The current schedules are published on GOV.UK's <a href="https://www.gov.uk/court-fees-what-they-are">court and tribunal fees</a> pages. Barristers' fees and VAT come on top of solicitor time.</li>
<li><strong>Who represents you and how.</strong> Hourly rates vary by region and seniority, and some firms offer fixed fees for defined early stages, such as an initial assessment of the claim's merits.</li>
</ul>

<p>For scale, it helps to remember what the uncontested version of the process costs: the standard probate application fee is <strong>£526</strong> for estates over £5,000, per <a href="https://www.gov.uk/applying-for-probate/fees">GOV.UK's probate fees page</a>, plus any professional help the executors choose to buy. Our <a href="/calculators/probate-diy-vs-solicitor">probate DIY vs solicitor calculator</a> models that baseline. A contested estate adds dispute costs on top of, not instead of, the ordinary administration.</p>

<h2>How people fund a claim</h2>

<p>Four funding routes come up repeatedly in probate disputes:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Paying privately by the hour</strong>, the traditional model. You carry the cost as it accrues, win or lose.</li>
<li><strong>Fixed fees for early stages</strong>, often an initial merits review, which caps your exposure while you decide whether the claim is worth pursuing.</li>
<li><strong>No win no fee agreements</strong>, formally conditional fee agreements (CFAs). You pay little or nothing if the claim fails, and if it succeeds your solicitor charges their fee plus a success fee, which comes out of what you recover. The success fee is not recoverable from the other side, so a CFA changes when you pay, not whether the work costs money.</li>
<li><strong>Legal expenses insurance</strong>, sometimes attached to home insurance policies, which can cover disputes you did not expect to fund. It is worth checking existing policies before assuming you have no cover.</li>
</ul>

<p>Which of these fits, if any, depends on the strength of the claim and the value at stake, which is a judgement for a contentious probate specialist rather than a general guide.</p>

<h2>Who ends up paying</h2>

<p>A common assumption is that the estate pays for everything. Usually it does not. The general rule in contested litigation is that the losing party pays a significant share of the winner's costs on top of their own, which is why cost risk, not just cost, shapes decisions in these disputes. Probate has some recognised exceptions where the court can order costs out of the estate, for instance where the conduct of the person who made the will was the real cause of the dispute, or where there were reasonable grounds for investigating the will's validity. Those exceptions are applied case by case, and no one should bank on them at the outset.</p>

<p>Time limits add a final financial dimension: some claims against an estate, notably those under the <a href="/blog/probate-process/inheritance-act-1975-claims-explained">Inheritance Act 1975</a>, must generally be brought within six months of the grant, and acting late can add cost or close the door entirely.</p>

<h2>Working out whether it is worth it</h2>

<p>The economics come down to three numbers: what you might recover, what it might cost to get there, and the cost risk if it fails. The third is the one people most often forget. A contentious probate solicitor can put realistic figures on all three for your circumstances, and most disputes are shaped in the first conversation about merits, not in a courtroom. Before that conversation, our <a href="/probate">probate pillar guide</a> covers how the underlying process works, and the contesting guide above maps which ground, if any, fits your situation. If the numbers and the grounds line up, specialist advice stops being a cost and becomes the claim's foundation.</p>
