<h2>Nothing was needed the first time, so why now?</h2>

<p>For most families the answer is that yes, a grant is needed when the second parent dies, even though the first death passed off without one. That is not a change in the law and it is not something anyone got wrong. It is the survivorship rule doing its job the first time and having nobody left to pass to the second time. If your parents owned the family home as joint tenants, <a href="https://www.gov.uk/applying-for-probate">GOV.UK confirms</a> that it passed automatically to the surviving owner, with no court paperwork at all. On the second death there is no surviving co-owner. The house sits in one sole name, and cannot be sold or transferred until someone proves they have authority to deal with it.</p>

<p>There is no rush in the first week or two. Registering the death and finding the will come first, and nothing about a later probate application gets harder for having waited. When you are ready to work out where the estate stands, the <a href="/calculators/do-i-need-probate-checker">do I need probate checker</a> walks through the assets one at a time and usually settles the question in a couple of minutes. This page covers England and Wales and is general information, not legal or financial advice.</p>

<h2>What actually happened on the first death</h2>

<p>Two mechanisms usually explain why the first parent's death needed no grant, and both of them are one-shot.</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Joint tenancy on the house.</strong> Most married couples and civil partners hold their home as beneficial joint tenants, so the whole property belongs to the survivor the moment the first owner dies. The Land Registry updates the title on production of a death certificate. Our guide to <a href="/blog/probate-process/do-you-need-probate-for-jointly-owned-assets">probate and jointly owned assets</a> sets out the survivorship rules, including the different position where a home is held as tenants in common.</li>
<li><strong>Joint accounts and modest sole balances.</strong> Joint current and savings accounts passed to the surviving parent in the same way, and any sole-name balances were often small enough for the bank to release under its own internal limit.</li>
</ul>

<p>If you are reading this at the other end of that sequence, with one parent still living, our page on <a href="/blog/probate-process/do-you-need-probate-when-spouse-dies">whether probate is needed when a spouse dies</a> covers the first death directly.</p>

<h2>What is different when the second parent dies</h2>

<p>Everything the surviving parent inherited is now in one name, and the assets that passed quietly the first time are the exact ones that trigger the requirement the second time.</p>

<table>
<thead>
<tr><th>Asset</th><th>On the first death</th><th>On the second death</th></tr>
</thead>
<tbody>
<tr><td>Family home, held jointly</td><td>Passes to the surviving parent by survivorship, no grant</td><td>Sole name, grant needed to sell or transfer</td></tr>

</tbody>

<p>The property line decides most cases. Where a house is in the sole name of the person who has died, a grant is effectively unavoidable, and our guide to <a href="/blog/probate-process/do-you-need-probate-to-sell-or-transfer-a-property">selling or transferring a property after a death</a> explains what the conveyancer and the Land Registry will expect. Where there is no property, the answer turns on the individual institutions, since <a href="https://www.gov.uk/wills-probate-inheritance">GOV.UK notes that every organisation has its own rules</a>. Those thresholds are covered in our page on <a href="/blog/probate-process/do-you-need-probate-for-bank-accounts-isas-and-life-insurance">probate for bank accounts, ISAs and life insurance</a>.</p>

<h2>A worked example</h2>

<p>Raymond died in 2021. He and Glenys owned their house in Shropshire as joint tenants and banked jointly, so the title moved into Glenys's sole name on production of the death certificate and their children, Owen and Rhiannon, never applied for anything.</p>

<p>Glenys dies five years later leaving a will naming Owen as executor. Her estate is the house at £342,000, a cash ISA of £36,000, a current account holding £11,400, and household contents and a car worth about £4,000, so £393,400 in total. The house alone settles the question: it is in her sole name, the children want to sell it, and no conveyancer will exchange without a grant. Owen applies, pays the £526 fee because the estate is over £5,000, and orders four sealed copies at £2 each for the bank, the ISA provider, the conveyancer and his own file.</p>

<p>Had Glenys downsized into rented accommodation and left only the ISA and the current account, the answer could have gone the other way, and our page on <a href="/blog/probate-process/do-you-need-probate-for-a-small-or-excepted-estate">probate for a small or excepted estate</a> would apply instead.</p>

<h2>Needing a grant is not the same as owing tax</h2>

<p>These two get tangled together constantly, and they are separate. Glenys's estate needs a grant but owes no inheritance tax. Every estate has a <a href="https://www.gov.uk/inheritance-tax">nil-rate band of £325,000</a>, and anything left to a spouse or civil partner is exempt, so Raymond used none of his. Glenys's executors can claim his unused band on top of her own, giving £650,000 before any tax arises. Where the home passes to children or grandchildren, the <a href="https://www.gov.uk/guidance/inheritance-tax-residence-nil-rate-band">residence nil-rate band</a> can add more again, and it too transfers from a first spouse who did not use it. Most second-parent estates pay nothing and still need a grant. Our <a href="/inheritance-tax">inheritance tax pillar guide</a> covers the thresholds and the claim forms.</p>

<h2>The first practical steps</h2>

<ol>
<li><strong>Register the death</strong> and order several copies of the death certificate. Institutions will want their own.</li>
<li><strong>Find the will.</strong> Executors named in it are the people who apply. Where there is no will, GOV.UK says the closest living relative applies, and our page on <a href="/blog/probate-process/do-you-need-probate-if-there-is-no-will-or-no-assets">probate where there is no will or no assets</a> covers what changes.</li>
<li><strong>Write to every institution</strong> with a death certificate and ask what they require to release the asset. Their written answer decides whether you need a grant, not a general rule.</li>
<li><strong>Value the estate</strong> at the date of death, including the house.</li>
<li><strong>Apply</strong>, online or on the paper form, once you know the totals.</li>
</ol>
