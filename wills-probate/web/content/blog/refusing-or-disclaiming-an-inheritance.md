<h2>Nobody can force you to accept an inheritance</h2>

<p>Yes, you can refuse an inheritance in England and Wales. The legal route is called a <strong>disclaimer</strong>: you give up the gift entirely, before taking any benefit from it, and the law then treats you as if you had never been entitled. The catch sits in the second half of the rule, and it is the part that surprises most people: <strong>if you disclaim, you have no say in where the gift goes instead</strong>. It falls back into the estate and passes under the will's residue clause, or under the intestacy rules, to whoever is next in line. What follows is general information about how the two refusal routes work, not legal or financial advice for your situation.</p>

<p>That loss of control is why many people who start out asking "can I refuse?" end up using a different tool altogether: a <a href="/blog/inheritance-tax/deed-of-variation-explained">deed of variation</a>, which lets you redirect your share to a person you choose, within two years of the death, with the same favourable tax treatment. The full guide to variations covers the conditions in depth; this page focuses on the refusal decision itself.</p>

<p>Both routes share one powerful feature. Under section 142 of the Inheritance Tax Act 1984, a written disclaimer or variation made <strong>within two years of the death</strong>, with no payment or reward changing hands, is read back for inheritance tax as if the deceased themselves had made the gift that way. HMRC's Inheritance Tax Manual confirms at <a href="https://www.gov.uk/hmrc-internal-manuals/inheritance-tax-manual/ihtm35162">IHTM35162</a> that disclaimers are treated in largely the same way as variations for this purpose, and that a disclaimer does not need the formal statement of intent a variation requires. GOV.UK's guidance on <a href="https://www.gov.uk/alter-a-will-after-a-death">changing a will after a death</a> sets out the variation side: everyone left worse off must agree, and if the change increases the inheritance tax bill a copy must go to HMRC within six months.</p>

<h2>The rules for a valid disclaimer</h2>

<p>HMRC's guidance at <a href="https://www.gov.uk/hmrc-internal-manuals/inheritance-tax-manual/ihtm35161">IHTM35161</a> sets out the general law conditions:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>All or nothing.</strong> You cannot disclaim half a legacy. You can, however, accept one gift under a will and disclaim a separate one, so a beneficiary left both a painting and a share of residue could keep the painting and refuse the share.</li>
<li><strong>No benefit first.</strong> If you have already taken any benefit from the property, such as income from it or use of it, a disclaimer is no longer available.</li>
<li><strong>Unconditional.</strong> You cannot attach strings or direct the gift onward. It simply devolves to whoever the will or the intestacy rules say comes next.</li>
<li><strong>Retraction is possible but fragile.</strong> A disclaimer can be withdrawn only while nobody has altered their position in reliance on it.</li>
</ul>

<p>Consider two brothers, Marcus and Dele, each left half of their late aunt's estate. Marcus is financially comfortable and would rather his half went to Dele's children. If Marcus disclaims, his half falls into residue and passes under the will's default provisions, which might send it to Dele, to a charity, or somewhere neither of them intended. If instead the brothers sign a deed of variation redirecting Marcus's half to Dele's children, the money goes exactly where Marcus wants it, and for inheritance tax it is treated as the aunt's own gift, so it never enters Marcus's estate. Whether either move changes the estate's overall tax bill depends on the numbers; the <a href="/calculators/iht-threshold-calculator">IHT threshold calculator</a> shows how an estate sits against the nil-rate bands, and our <a href="/inheritance-tax">inheritance tax guide</a> explains the allowances in full.</p>

<h2>Refusing to protect benefits or avoid care fees: the deprivation trap</h2>

<p>A common motive for refusing an inheritance is to stay below a means-test threshold, either for benefits such as Universal Credit or for a local authority care fee assessment. This is where refusal can go badly wrong, and the point here is informational: how the rules respond, not a strategy for navigating around them.</p>

<p>For benefits, DWP guidance on <a href="https://www.gov.uk/guidance/universal-credit-money-savings-and-investments">money, savings and investments for Universal Credit</a> is blunt: if you deliberately reduce your capital to get or increase an award, your claim is assessed as if you still had the money, known as <strong>notional capital</strong>. Turning down an inheritance you were entitled to can fall squarely within that rule. For care fees, the <a href="https://www.gov.uk/guidance/care-and-support-statutory-guidance/annexes">Care Act statutory guidance (Annex E)</a> gives local authorities a matching power: where avoiding care charges was a significant motivation for giving up an asset, the authority can treat the person as still possessing it. In both systems the practical outcome of a deprivation finding is the worst of both worlds: the money has gone to someone else, yet the means test counts it against you anyway. Anyone weighing a refusal against a means test should take advice on their specific facts before signing anything.</p>

<h2>Deciding which route fits</h2>

<p>A disclaimer suits the rare case where you simply want no part of a gift and are content with wherever the will sends it next, perhaps because the property carries debts or obligations you do not want. In almost every situation where you care about the destination, the deed of variation is the better instrument, and its <strong>two-year clock starts at the date of death</strong>, so the decision cannot be parked indefinitely. Getting the paperwork right matters because a defective disclaimer or variation loses the section 142 reading-back and can turn your refusal into a taxable gift from you instead. An estate planning or probate specialist can check which route achieves what you actually want, draft it so the tax treatment holds, and flag any means-test consequences before you commit; we can put you in touch with one who handles redirected inheritances regularly.</p>
