<h2>Roughly a year, and the court is the quick part</h2>

<p>For a straightforward estate in England and Wales, expect around <strong>9 to 12 months</strong> from the date of death to the final payment reaching beneficiaries, and around <strong>£550 to £900</strong> in outlays if the executor does the work personally. Those two numbers behave very differently. The cost is largely fixed and knowable in week one, because the court application fee is a flat <strong>£526</strong> for any estate over £5,000 per <a href="https://www.gov.uk/applying-for-probate/fees">GOV.UK's probate fees guidance</a>, whether the estate is £60,000 or £6 million. The timetable is not knowable in week one, and almost none of it is spent waiting for the probate registry.</p>

<p>That last point surprises people. In the Ministry of Justice's <a href="https://www.gov.uk/government/statistics/family-court-statistics-quarterly-january-to-march-2026/family-court-statistics-quarterly-january-to-march-2026">Family Court Statistics Quarterly for January to March 2026</a>, the most recent published quarter, a grant of probate took a mean of <strong>5 weeks</strong> from submission to issue, with a median of one week, and <a href="https://www.gov.uk/applying-for-probate/after-youve-applied">GOV.UK tells applicants</a> to expect the grant "within 12 weeks of submitting your application". So if an estate takes a year, roughly eleven months of that happened somewhere other than the court. This page is general information rather than legal or financial advice, and it covers England and Wales.</p>

<p>The money side is covered in detail elsewhere: the complete fee schedule sits in <a href="/blog/probate-process/how-much-do-probate-court-fees-cost">how much do probate court fees cost</a>, and the full line by line receipt in <a href="/blog/probate-process/how-much-does-diy-probate-cost">how much does DIY probate cost</a>. This page is about the clock, and about the places where the clock quietly starts charging you.</p>

<h2>What actually adds the months</h2>

<ul>
<li><strong>The inheritance tax gate.</strong> Where the estate needs a full IHT400, you cannot apply for the grant until HMRC has issued a unique code, and <a href="https://www.gov.uk/valuing-estate-of-someone-who-died/inheritance-tax-to-pay">GOV.UK says</a> you will usually get it "within 20 working days of them receiving your IHT400 form or Inheritance Tax payment, whichever is later". Note the last three words. If you send the form promptly but cannot raise the tax for six weeks, the clock starts on the payment.</li>
<li><strong>A stopped application.</strong> This is the biggest swing in the court stage and it is the one an applicant controls. In January to March 2026, stopped probate grants took <strong>13.8 weeks</strong> on average to issue, against <strong>1.8 weeks</strong> for those that were not stopped. A name that does not match the will, a figure that will not reconcile with the tax forms, or a missing original document is worth three months.</li>
<li><strong>No will, or an awkward one.</strong> In the same quarter, letters of administration where there was no will averaged <strong>10.5 weeks</strong>, and letters of administration with a will annexed (used where a will exists but no executor can act) averaged <strong>20.4 weeks</strong>, against <strong>5.0 weeks</strong> for a straightforward grant of probate.</li>
<li><strong>A property that has to sell.</strong> The grant arrives long before the buyer does. An estate cannot be wound up until its largest asset becomes money, and a chain collapse in month eight resets that part of the timetable completely.</li>
<li><strong>Beneficiaries who cannot be found, or will not reply.</strong> Every beneficiary needs identifying, and most need to supply identification documents and bank details before anything can be paid. One relative abroad who takes three months to answer emails holds up the final distribution for everyone else.</li>
<li><strong>The two month creditor notice.</strong> A <a href="/blog/probate-process/section-27-notice-in-probate-explained">section 27 notice</a> must give claimants at least two months to come forward before it is safe to distribute. That is a floor, not a queue position, which is exactly why it should be published early.</li>
</ul>

<h2>Where waiting turns into money</h2>

<p>Court fees are indifferent to time. Almost nothing else is. Once the end of the sixth month after the death passes, HMRC <a href="https://www.gov.uk/paying-inheritance-tax">charges interest</a> on unpaid inheritance tax, so slow valuations have a direct price. An empty property carries insurance on a specialist unoccupied policy, council tax once any exemption expires, standing charges and basic upkeep, which together can run to several hundred pounds a month for as long as the sale drags. And where professional help is charged by the hour rather than as a fixed quote, elapsed time and cost move together, which is the strongest practical argument for asking for a fixed fee before instructing anyone.</p>

<p>The reverse also holds, and it is worth saying plainly: spending a little early usually buys time back. A proper <a href="/blog/probate-process/how-much-does-a-probate-valuation-cost">probate valuation</a> at £200 to £600 is cheaper than an HMRC challenge to an estate agent's guess a year later, and six sealed copies ordered with the application cost £12 rather than the £96 the same six cost afterwards, while letting you deal with six institutions simultaneously instead of posting one original around the country.</p>

<h2>The realistic ways to shorten it</h2>

<p>Request date of death valuations in the first fortnight rather than waiting for everything else to settle, because they are the long pole. Apply online, as 82.6% of applicants did in the latest quarter. Reconcile the inheritance tax figures against the probate application before you submit, since avoiding a stop is worth more than any other single action. Publish the section 27 notice early so its two months run alongside the valuations rather than after them. Order more sealed copies than you think you need. And market the property before the grant lands, so that only completion, not the search for a buyer, is waiting on the court.</p>
