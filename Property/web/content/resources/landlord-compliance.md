<p>Landlord compliance is not one obligation. It is six or seven separate statutory duties, created at different times by different departments, enforced by different bodies, on cycles that do not line up. They have almost nothing in common except that they all land on the same person and all get paid for out of the same rent. This guide maps them, points at the primary source for each so you are never relying on a summary of a summary, and sets out the tax treatment, which is the part most compliance guidance skips.</p>

<p>It covers residential property let in <strong>England</strong>. That matters more than it sounds, because the regimes do not share a footprint, and the single most common error in landlord content is applying one country's rule to another. It is general information, not advice for your specific situation.</p>

<h2>Jurisdiction, before you read anything else</h2>

<ul>
<li><strong>Gas safety:</strong> Great Britain. Northern Ireland has a parallel regime.</li>
<li><strong>Electrical safety (EICR):</strong> England only. Scotland has required electrical inspections since 2015 under separate rules; Wales and Northern Ireland differ.</li>
<li><strong>EPCs and minimum energy efficiency:</strong> England and Wales. Scotland runs its own regime; Northern Ireland is separate again.</li>
<li><strong>Fire safety order:</strong> England and Wales.</li>
<li><strong>Smoke and carbon monoxide alarms:</strong> England.</li>
<li><strong>Licensing under the Housing Act 2004:</strong> described here in its England form. Wales, Scotland and Northern Ireland run parallel but distinct schemes, including Rent Smart Wales.</li>
</ul>

<h2>Gas safety</h2>

<p>Regulation 36 of the Gas Safety (Installation and Use) Regulations 1998 requires an annual check of every gas appliance and flue by a Gas Safe registered engineer. A copy of the record goes to each existing tenant within 28 days of the check and to any new tenant before occupation. The record is kept until there have been two further checks of that appliance or flue, and for two years from the last check where an appliance is removed.</p>

<p>Regulation 36A, inserted in 2018, is the provision worth knowing: a check carried out within the two months ending with the deadline date is treated as made on the deadline date. Booking up to two months early therefore fixes the price without shortening the cycle.</p>

<p>Enforcement runs through the Health and Safety Executive on a criminal track rather than through a civil penalty regime. There is no fixed fine figure, and the specific figures circulating in agent guidance are unsourced. Treat the exposure as a substantial fine, unlimited on indictment, with a custodial sentence available in serious cases.</p>

<h2>Electrical safety and the EICR</h2>

<p>The Electrical Safety Standards in the Private Rented Sector (England) Regulations 2020 require the fixed installation to be inspected and tested by a qualified person at intervals of no more than five years, or sooner where the previous report specifies. The report is the EICR. There is no separate document called an electrical safety certificate in law, whatever an agent asks for.</p>

<p>The service and action deadlines are where councils find breaches: the report to each existing tenant within 28 days of the inspection, to a new tenant before occupation, to the local housing authority within 7 days of a written request, and any further investigative or remedial work completed within 28 days or any shorter period the report specifies.</p>

<p>The financial penalty under regulation 11 is <strong>up to £40,000 per breach</strong>. The cap was £30,000 when the regulations were made and was raised by SI 2025/1043 with effect from 1 November 2025, so a great deal of guidance still online understates it. It is a per-breach cap rather than a total, and there is a right of appeal to the First-tier Tribunal.</p>

<h2>EPCs, and the separate question of the band</h2>

<p>Two statutes operate here and merging them causes real errors. The Energy Performance of Buildings (England and Wales) Regulations 2012 answer whether a certificate must exist for the transaction: an EPC before marketing, made available free of charge to a prospective tenant at the earliest opportunity, valid for ten years from the date it went on the register. The Energy Efficiency (Private Rented Property) (England and Wales) Regulations 2015 answer a different question: whether the band is good enough to let.</p>

<p>The answer to the second is band E. Letting a domestic property rated F or G has been prohibited for new tenancies since 1 April 2018 and for all continuing lets since 1 April 2020, unless a valid exemption is registered on the PRS Exemptions Register. Where the property cannot reach band E within the <strong>£3,500 including VAT</strong> cost cap, an exemption can be registered on that basis. Most exemption classes run five years; the temporary class for someone who has recently become a landlord runs six months.</p>

<p>Check the national register before booking anything. An EPC lasts ten years and survives both a sale and a re-let, so a valid certificate may already exist.</p>

<p><strong>EPC C by 2030 is not law.</strong> Neither is the £10,000 cap quoted alongside it. Both are policy aspiration; no statutory instrument has been laid to give either statutory force. The enforceable floor today is band E.</p>

<h2>Fire safety and alarms</h2>

<p>The Regulatory Reform (Fire Safety) Order 2005 applies to HMOs and to the common parts of buildings containing two or more sets of domestic premises. It does <strong>not</strong> apply inside a single self-contained house let to one household, and any guide saying every rental needs a fire risk assessment is wrong. Where it does apply, the responsible person must make a suitable and sufficient assessment under article 9 and, since October 2023, record it in full rather than recording only the significant findings.</p>

<p>High-rise residential buildings carry an additional layer under the Fire Safety (England) Regulations 2022, covering secure information boxes, external wall information, floor plans, lifts and wayfinding signage. Duties to give fire safety information to residents, and duties about fire doors, reach further down the stock than the high-rise duties do.</p>

<p>Separately, and in every let, the Smoke and Carbon Monoxide Alarm (England) Regulations 2015 require a smoke alarm on each storey with living accommodation, carbon monoxide alarms in rooms with a qualifying combustion appliance, and a working-order check on the day the tenancy begins. The penalty cap is £5,000 under regulation 8(2). Check the current wording of regulation 4 before relying on any statement of the carbon monoxide scope, because it has been amended.</p>

<h2>Licensing</h2>

<p>England runs three licensing regimes under the Housing Act 2004. <strong>Mandatory HMO licensing</strong> applies nationwide to any HMO occupied by five or more people forming two or more households, regardless of what the council has designated. <strong>Additional HMO licensing</strong> catches smaller shared houses, but only inside a designated area. <strong>Selective licensing</strong> catches every private rental inside a designated area, including an ordinary single-family let.</p>

<p>Designations run for up to five years and then lapse or are re-made, so a scheme that ended last year does not bind you and one that started last month does. Fees are set locally: mandatory HMO licences commonly run from around £600 to £1,900 and selective licences from around £500 to £1,000, usually split between a payment on application and a second on grant.</p>

<p>The civil penalty for letting without a required licence is <strong>up to £40,000 per offence</strong>, raised from £30,000 with effect from 1 May 2026 by the Financial Penalties (Housing Offences and Breach of Banning Orders) Regulations 2026 (SI 2026/319). The alternative is criminal prosecution with an unlimited fine on summary conviction. On top of either, a rent repayment order can recover up to two years of rent, and repeat offenders face banning orders, which end the rental business and bring capital gains tax cessation consequences with them.</p>

<h2>Commercial property is a different regime</h2>

<p>Do not import the domestic figures. The £3,500 cost cap is domestic only. On the non-domestic side the equivalent filter is a seven-year simple payback test: improvements that do not pay for themselves in energy savings within seven years are not relevant improvements. Penalties are linked to rateable value with a split between breaches under three months and breaches of three months or more, and the non-dwelling EPC penalty is a percentage of rateable value rather than the fixed £200 notice a dwelling attracts. EPC C or B for commercial stock remains consultation stage; no statutory instrument has been made.</p>

<h2>The tax treatment, which is where the money is</h2>

<p>Every compliance pound falls into one of three buckets, and they are treated completely differently.</p>

<h3>Revenue: deducted against rental profit in the year incurred</h3>

<p>The CP12 fee, the EICR fee, the EPC fee, a fire risk assessment fee, a licence fee, alarm servicing, redress scheme membership, and like-for-like repairs the inspection flags. These are compulsory regulatory costs of running the letting business and are deductible under ITTOIA 2005 s.272. A licence fee is deductible in full in the year it is incurred, even though the licence covers a term of up to five years. Compliance spend before the first tenant moves in is not lost either, because revenue expenditure incurred pre-letting is relieved under the pre-trading rules.</p>

<h3>Capital: added to base cost, relieved against CGT on sale</h3>

<p>A first-time full rewire that materially upgrades the installation, a new alarm or compartmentation system, insulation, glazing and heating upgrades that lift the EPC band. These are improvements rather than repairs, so they are added to the base cost under TCGA 1992 s.38(1)(b). Grant receipts under schemes such as ECO4, GBIS and the Boiler Upgrade Scheme reduce the expenditure they fund and therefore reduce the base cost you can claim, so grants and works have to be tracked together.</p>

<h3>No relief at all: penalties</h3>

<p>Civil penalties, penalty charge notices, criminal fines and rent repayment orders are not incurred wholly and exclusively for the purposes of the business, so nothing is deductible. HMRC sets the position out at BIM38500 onwards. The contrast is the point: a £150 report is fully deductible and the £40,000 penalty for skipping it attracts no relief whatsoever.</p>

<h3>Two points that are easy to miss</h3>

<p>None of these costs are finance costs, so the Section 24 restriction does not apply to them. They come off rental income at your full marginal rate, unlike mortgage interest. And the dividing line that causes most amended returns is repair against improvement: restoring the installation to the condition it was in is a repair, making it materially better than it was is capital. A consumer unit swapped like for like after a C2 code is a repair; a first-time rewire that brings a 1960s installation up to a modern standard is not.</p>

<h2>The wider regime, and what is coming</h2>

<p>Beyond the certificates, the Renters' Rights Act 2025 adds a redress scheme obligation and a private rented sector database, and extends the rent repayment order window. Commencement is being phased, so the operative question for any duty is which provisions have actually been brought into force rather than what the Act says on its face. The Decent Homes Standard for the private rented sector is on the same footing: preliminary provisions only, with the substantive standard awaiting a further statutory instrument.</p>

<h2>Get your own position checked</h2>

<p>This guide will tell you which duties bite and where the source law sits. What it cannot see is your portfolio: which remedial jobs were misposted as repairs when they were improvements, which pre-letting costs were never claimed, whether a licence fee was spread when it should have been deducted in full, or whether an energy grant was netted off correctly. Those are the points where a property tax specialist repays the fee several times over. Before your next return, get someone to run your actual figures.</p>
