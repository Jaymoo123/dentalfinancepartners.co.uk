<p>A semi-commercial property, the shop with a flat above, the office with living accommodation, the pub with a manager's maisonette, is one of the most common assets in UK property investment and one of the most misunderstood to finance. It is not a residential mortgage, it is not a standard buy-to-let, and it is not a pure commercial mortgage either. It sits in the middle, financed as a specialist mixed-use commercial loan, valued as two things at once, and governed by a regulatory test that decides whether the loan is even regulated in the first place. This guide sets out how a semi-commercial mortgage works, where the boundaries sit, and the three tax quirks that make mixed-use property genuinely different.</p>

<p><strong>This is educational information, not a financial promotion.</strong> Semi-commercial mortgages taken for a business or investment purpose are unregulated business lending, but their promotion is restricted under <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2000/8/section/21" rel="nofollow">section 21 of the Financial Services and Markets Act 2000</a>. We do not arrange finance, compare live rates, or invite you into a credit agreement. Where a mixed-use property is occupied as your own home, a different, regulated set of rules applies and you should speak to an FCA-authorised mortgage adviser. What we do here is explain the mechanics and, crucially, the tax treatment that sits alongside them.</p>

<h2>What counts as semi-commercial or mixed-use property</h2>
<p>A property is semi-commercial when it combines a commercial and a residential use within a single building or title. The textbook case is a ground-floor retail unit with one or more self-contained flats on the upper floors. The same category captures offices with living accommodation, guest houses, shops with a maisonette, and pubs or restaurants with residential quarters above. What matters to a lender is that the asset is neither wholly residential (so mainstream residential and buy-to-let lenders will not touch the whole title) nor wholly commercial.</p>
<p>The proportion of each use is not just a labelling question. It drives the valuation, the affordability test, the tax position, and the regulatory status of the loan. A building that is 90% commercial with a small caretaker flat behaves very differently from a single shop beneath four large apartments. Lenders will look at the split by floor area and by value, and the "predominant use" of the building shapes which lenders will consider it and on what terms.</p>

<h2>Why it is financed as commercial, not residential</h2>
<p>Because part of the building is in commercial use, standard residential and buy-to-let lenders cannot lend against the whole title. Their products, criteria and permissions are built around wholly residential security. So a mixed-use property is financed through a commercial or specialist semi-commercial lender that is comfortable underwriting both elements as a single asset.</p>
<p>That routes you into commercial lending conventions rather than residential ones. Expect a larger deposit, typically in the region of 25% to 35% of the blended value, giving a loan to value of roughly 65% to 75% (as at July 2026, verify current criteria with a lender or broker). Pricing is set deal by deal as a margin over a reference rate such as the Bank of England base rate or SONIA, rather than off a published residential rate sheet, and it moves with the commercial split, the lease, the tenant and your experience. Terms commonly run 5 to 25 years, on either a capital-and-interest or an interest-only basis. For a fuller treatment of how commercial pricing is built up, see our guide to <a href="/blog/property-finance/commercial-mortgage-rates">commercial mortgage rates</a> and the wider <a href="/blog/property-finance/commercial-mortgages-guide">commercial mortgages guide</a>.</p>

<h2>The regulated-boundary test: when a mixed-use loan is regulated</h2>
<p>This is the single most important point on the page, because it decides whether the loan is a business product at all. Under article 61 of the Financial Services and Markets Act 2000 (Regulated Activities) Order 2001, and as explained in <a href="https://www.handbook.fca.org.uk/handbook/PERG/4/" rel="nofollow">FCA PERG 4</a>, a loan secured on land is a <em>regulated mortgage contract</em> only where <strong>at least 40% of the land is used, or is intended to be used, as or in connection with a dwelling by the borrower or a related person</strong>.</p>
<p>Read that carefully. The test is not simply "is more than 40% of the building residential". It is whether 40% or more is used as a dwelling <em>by the borrower or a related person</em>. On a genuine mixed-use investment, where the flat above is let to a third-party tenant and the borrower does not live there, the dwelling is not occupied by the borrower, the 40% test is not met, and the loan is unregulated business lending. That is the normal position for an investor buying a shop-with-flat to let out.</p>
<p>The boundary bites the other way when the borrower or their family occupies the residential part. A trading business owner who buys a shop and lives in the flat above, or an investor who houses a relative there, can push the building over the 40%-used-as-a-dwelling line and into regulated territory. At that point it is a regulated mortgage contract, MCOB conduct rules apply, and it is outside the scope of this guide. <strong>If you will live in the residential part, treat the loan as regulated and speak to an FCA-authorised mortgage adviser.</strong> The same fence applies to any borrowing secured on your own home.</p>

<h2>How a lender values a mixed-use building</h2>
<p>A semi-commercial valuation is really two valuations stitched together. The valuer assesses the commercial element and the residential element separately, then blends them into a single figure that the lender applies its LTV against.</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>The commercial part</strong> is valued on a vacant-possession basis if it is empty, or on an investment (yield) basis where it is let and producing income. A longer lease to a stronger tenant lifts the investment value, because the income stream the lender is buying into is more secure. An empty or short-let unit is worth less and borrows less.</li>
<li><strong>The residential flat</strong> is valued much like any other flat, on comparable evidence from similar local properties, with regard to its condition, size and whether it is currently let.</li>
</ul>
<p>The two figures are added, occasionally with a modest adjustment for the mixed nature of the asset or for management drag, to produce the blended value. This blended-valuation mechanic is why an investor cannot simply borrow against the flat as if it were a normal residential unit: the lender is looking at, and lending against, the whole building as one asset. The valuation approach draws on the <a href="https://www.rics.org/profession-standards/rics-standards-and-guidance/sector-standards/valuation-standards/red-book" rel="nofollow">RICS Red Book</a> bases of value (market value, vacant possession, investment value), which set the professional standards a surveyor works to.</p>

<h3>A worked example</h3>
<p>Take a high-street building bought for £400,000: a retail unit on the ground floor let to a shop tenant, with a self-contained two-bedroom flat above let to a residential tenant. The valuer assesses the shop on an investment basis at, say, £260,000 given its lease and tenant, and the flat on comparables at £140,000. The blended value is £400,000. Because the flat is let to a third-party tenant and is not occupied by the borrower, the 40% dwelling-use test is not met (and here the residential share is 35% in any case), so this is unregulated business lending, financed as a commercial mortgage.</p>
<p>A lender offering 70% LTV against the blended value would advance £280,000, leaving a £120,000 deposit. Affordability would then be tested on the combined rent from the shop and the flat, stressed against a higher interest rate, using a debt service cover ratio (commonly around 125% to 140%, verify current criteria). On the tax side, because the building is mixed-use, the buyer pays the <strong>non-residential</strong> rates of Stamp Duty Land Tax: nothing on the first £150,000, 2% on the slice to £250,000 (£2,000), and 5% on the slice from £250,000 to £400,000 (£7,500), a total of £9,500, with no additional-dwelling surcharge. Residential SDLT on the same price, particularly for a second property, would be materially higher. All figures are illustrative and current pricing must be confirmed with a lender or broker.</p>

<h2>The three tax quirks that make mixed-use different</h2>
<p>Mixed-use property does not just finance differently, it is taxed differently at three separate points. Each of these is a genuine planning issue and each has its own detailed treatment, so we summarise here and link up to the full guides.</p>

<h3>1. Stamp Duty Land Tax at the non-residential rates</h3>
<p>Mixed-use property in England and Northern Ireland is charged SDLT at the non-residential (commercial) rates, not the residential rates. As at 2026/27 those bands are 0% up to £150,000, 2% from £150,001 to £250,000, and 5% above £250,000, and crucially there is no 5% additional-dwelling surcharge and no higher-rate residential charge. For many buyers that is a real saving over residential SDLT on the same consideration, which is exactly why the mixed-use classification is valuable. It is also why <a href="https://www.gov.uk/stamp-duty-land-tax/nonresidential-and-mixed-use-rates" rel="nofollow">HMRC's guidance on non-residential and mixed-use rates</a> is applied strictly: the commercial element must be a genuine, substantive commercial use, not a token strip of land or a nominal arrangement, or HMRC will challenge the mixed-use treatment and reassess at residential rates.</p>

<h3>2. VAT, and why the option to tax stops at the flat</h3>
<p>Commercial property letting is VAT-exempt by default, but the owner can exercise the option to tax over the commercial element, converting its rent and sale into a standard-rated 20% supply and unlocking input VAT recovery on acquisition and refurbishment of that part. On a mixed-use building there is a hard limit: under VATA 1994 Schedule 10 paragraph 5, the option to tax is <strong>automatically disapplied in respect of any dwelling</strong>. So you can opt over the shop, but you cannot opt over the flat above. The flat's rent stays exempt whatever you do, input VAT on the residential part is not recoverable through the opt, and you are left apportioning costs between the two elements under the partial-exemption rules. This is a common trap on mixed-use refurbishments. The mechanics, cooling-off period and disapplication rules are set out in full in our guide to the <a href="/blog/property-types-and-specialist-tax/vat-option-to-tax-commercial-property-mechanics-election-revocation">VAT option to tax on commercial property</a>.</p>

<h3>3. Capital allowances on the commercial element</h3>
<p>The commercial part of a mixed-use building can carry qualifying plant and machinery and integral features, heating and ventilation, wiring, air conditioning, sanitaryware and similar, which attract capital allowances. That includes the Annual Investment Allowance (£1m, permanent) and, for companies, full expensing on qualifying new and unused main-rate plant. From 1 January 2026 a new 40% first-year allowance is also available on qualifying new main-rate plant to both companies and unincorporated businesses. The residential dwelling itself is excluded from plant and machinery allowances, though plant in common parts can qualify in limited cases. An embedded capital allowances review on the commercial portion of a mixed-use purchase frequently uncovers relief the buyer did not know was there. The detail sits with our <a href="/blog/property-types-and-specialist-tax/commercial-property-tax-landlords-rates-reliefs-allowances">commercial property tax guide</a>, which covers allowances, reliefs and rates for landlords.</p>

<p>One further point that spans finance and tax: how the interest is relieved depends on your ownership vehicle. A company deducts its finance costs against profit under the loan-relationship rules in CTA 2009 and is not caught by the residential finance-cost restriction. The Section 24 restriction (<a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2005/5/section/272A" rel="nofollow">ITTOIA 2005 s272A</a>) applies to individuals letting residential dwellings, and on a mixed-use property held personally it is the residential element's finance cost that falls within it, while the commercial element's interest is fully deductible. Getting the vehicle right before you buy is a structuring decision worth taking early.</p>

<h2>Who lends and how affordability is tested</h2>
<p>Semi-commercial mortgages come from commercial and specialist lenders rather than the mainstream residential market: challenger banks, specialist property lenders and some building societies with commercial arms. Because the security is a blended asset, they underwrite it commercially. Instead of a residential income multiple, they apply a debt service cover ratio (DSCR) or interest cover ratio (ICR): the combined rent from the commercial unit and the flat, stressed against a higher assumed interest rate, must exceed the stressed debt cost by a set margin (commonly around 125% to 140%, verify current criteria at the time you borrow).</p>
<p>Lease length, tenant covenant strength, void assumptions and any owner-occupier trading income all feed that calculation. A short remaining lease on the commercial unit, or a weak tenant, reduces the income the lender will rely on and therefore the loan available, even where the headline value supports more. <h2>How semi-commercial sits against the rest of property finance</h2>
<p>Semi-commercial is a hybrid, so it helps to place it. Against a <strong>standard buy-to-let</strong>, the difference is the commercial element: any material commercial use pushes the whole building out of residential lending and into commercial. Against a <strong>pure commercial mortgage</strong>, the difference is the residential flat, which brings the 40%-dwelling-use regulatory test into play and adds the residential-tax overlay on part of the building. Against <strong>bridging finance</strong>, a semi-commercial term mortgage is the long-term hold, whereas a bridge is the short-term tool used to buy, refurbish or make a mixed-use unit lettable before refinancing onto exactly this kind of term loan.</p>
<p>For most investors the sequence is simple: if the building is bought ready to let, a semi-commercial term mortgage does the job from the start; if the commercial unit is empty, tired or needs a tenant, a bridge may come first. Either way, the tax questions, mixed SDLT on purchase, the VAT option on the commercial part, and capital allowances, arise at the same moment, at acquisition, and are best settled before completion rather than after.</p>

<h2>The bottom line</h2>
<p>A semi-commercial mortgage is a commercial product for a hybrid asset. It is valued as two things blended into one, it is underwritten on covered rental income rather than a residential multiple, and its regulatory status turns on whether 40% or more of the building is used as a dwelling by you or your family. Get that boundary right, and the mixed-use classification then unlocks non-residential SDLT rates, a VAT option that reaches only the commercial part, and capital allowances on the commercial plant. Those three tax points are where the real planning value sits, and where an early review pays for itself.</p>
