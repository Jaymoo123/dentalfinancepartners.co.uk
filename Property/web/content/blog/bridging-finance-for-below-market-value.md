<h2>What bridging for a below-market-value purchase is</h2>
<p>A below-market-value purchase is exactly what it sounds like: buying a property for less than it is genuinely worth. A motivated seller who needs a fast, certain sale, a repossession, a probate disposal, a divorce or a relocation can all put a property on the market below its open market value, in exchange for speed and certainty. Bridging finance is the tool investors reach for because it can complete quickly enough to satisfy that kind of seller, where a term mortgage cannot.</p>
<p>The distinctive question on a below-market deal is not how fast the money moves, it is what the lender lends against. Buy a property worth 200,000 pounds for 150,000 pounds and the tempting idea is to borrow against the 200,000 pound true value, not the 150,000 pound price, and fund most or all of the purchase without much of your own cash. Whether a lender will do that, and on what evidence, is the whole subject of this page. Before you read on, our <a href="/blog/property-finance/bridging-loans-guide">bridging loans guide</a> covers how short-term lending works in general; this page is about the value-versus-price mechanics that are unique to buying under the odds.</p>

<h2>Open market value versus the price you pay: the "no money in" myth</h2>
<p>Two numbers sit at the centre of every below-market deal, and confusing them is where buyers go wrong.</p>
<p><strong>The purchase price</strong> is what you actually agree to pay the seller. <strong>The open market value</strong> is what a RICS surveyor, valuing to the profession's Red Book standard, judges the property is worth on the open market between a willing buyer and a willing seller. On a genuine below-market buy, the value is higher than the price, and the gap is your instant equity.</p>
<p>The "no money in" pitch runs like this: if a lender advances, say, 75 percent of the 200,000 pound value, that is 150,000 pounds, which covers the entire 150,000 pound price, so you complete without a deposit. It is a real mechanism, and it does happen. But it is the exception, not the rule, and it rests on the lender agreeing to lend against day-one value rather than price. Most do not, at least not at first. Fees, rolled interest and valuation costs also still have to be funded, so even a genuine no-deposit deal is rarely truly no cash. Treating "no money in" as the normal outcome is the single biggest mistake below-market buyers make.</p>
<p>The definition of value here is not yours to set. It is the surveyor's, and lenders rely on their own valuer's <a href="https://www.rics.org/profession-standards/rics-standards-and-guidance/sector-standards/valuation-standards" rel="nofollow">RICS Red Book</a> figure, not the price you quote or the value you hope for.</p>

<h2>The six-month rule: why most lenders anchor to the lower figure</h2>
<p>The reason day-one value lending is the exception is a long-standing piece of lender caution often called the six-month rule. Many mortgage lenders, and the guidance the industry works to, will only lend against the original purchase price rather than an increased valuation until the borrower has owned the property for six months. Some apply it strictly, some flex it, but the principle is widespread.</p>
<p>It exists to guard against price-inflation fraud and back-to-back sales, where a property is bought and quickly resold at an inflated figure to extract cash. Anchoring to the lower of price and value removes the incentive. For a below-market buyer the practical effect is twofold. First, a term or buy-to-let lender refinancing you day one may recognise only the 150,000 pound price, not the 200,000 pound value, so you cannot immediately pull the discount out as equity. Second, it pushes many buyers towards a bridge precisely because a specialist bridging lender can be more willing to lend against day-one open market value where the below-market purchase is genuine and evidenced. The six-month rule therefore shapes the exit as much as the purchase, a point we return to below.</p>

<h2>When a lender will lend against day-one value</h2>
<p>A specialist bridging lender will consider lending against the open market value on day one, rather than the price, when it is satisfied the discount is real. That satisfaction rests on evidence, and the more of it you can show, the better the outcome.</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>A clear reason for the discount.</strong> A distressed or motivated seller, a repossession, a probate or executor sale, a divorce, an emigration, or a portfolio being wound up. The lender wants to understand why a rational seller would sell below value.</li>
<li><strong>An independent RICS valuation</strong> that supports the open market value. The valuer's figure, not the price, is what the lender lends against, so the valuation is the linchpin of the whole deal.</li>
<li><strong>Gifted equity, properly documented.</strong> Where a family member sells to you below value, the difference can be treated as a <strong>vendor-gifted deposit</strong>: the gifted equity stands in place of a cash deposit. Some lenders accept this, subject to a gift letter and full disclosure, which is what allows a genuinely low or no cash purchase.</li>
<li><strong>A credible exit.</strong> The lender underwrites how the bridge is repaid before it lends a penny.</li>
</ul>
<p>Even then, appetite varies by lender and moves with the market. Loan-to-value on a below-market bridge is typically capped as a percentage of the open market value, and where day-one value is accepted the buyer can fund most or all of the price, but the fee stack still bites. Because pricing and appetite change constantly, treat any figure here as indicative as at July 2026 and verify current terms with a lender or broker before you rely on them.</p>

<h2>A worked example: a £200,000 property bought at £150,000</h2>
<p>Consider an investor buying a house from a seller who needs a fast, certain exit. An independent RICS valuation puts the open market value at 200,000 pounds. The agreed price, reflecting the seller's need for speed, is 150,000 pounds, a genuine 25 percent discount. The numbers below are illustrative, use round assumptions, and reflect pricing as at July 2026 that must be verified with a lender or broker at the point of borrowing.</p>
<p><strong>The day-one-value scenario (a lender that recognises open market value):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Open market value:</strong> 200,000 pounds</li>
<li><strong>Purchase price:</strong> 150,000 pounds</li>
<li><strong>Gross bridge at 75 percent of value:</strong> 150,000 pounds</li>
<li><strong>Purchase price covered:</strong> the whole 150,000 pounds, in principle</li>
<li><strong>But fees and rolled interest come off the top:</strong> arrangement fee, valuation, legal costs and a rolled-interest reserve, together often several thousand pounds</li>
<li><strong>Your own cash needed:</strong> the fee stack, not a deposit, so the "no money in" idea is nearly true, but not quite</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>The price-anchored scenario (a lender applying the six-month rule):</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Loan against the lower of price and value:</strong> 75 percent of 150,000 pounds, so 112,500 pounds</li>
<li><strong>Your own cash needed towards the price:</strong> 37,500 pounds, plus the fee stack</li>
</ul>
<p>The same deal, the same discount, and the cash you need swings from a few thousand pounds to nearly 40,000 pounds, purely on whether the lender recognises day-one value or anchors to price. That is the single most important variable on a below-market purchase, and it is why the identity of the lender matters more here than on almost any other bridge. <h2>The fraud and money-laundering red flags, and the regulated fence</h2>
<p>Below-market and undervalue sales sit close to a well-known fraud pattern, so they attract scrutiny, and rightly. Understanding the red flags protects you as much as the lender.</p>
<p><strong>Back-to-back and sub-sale flipping.</strong> A property bought and resold within a short window at a rising price is the textbook price-inflation red flag. It is not automatically improper, but it invites questions, and the six-month rule exists largely to blunt it.</p>
<p><strong>Undisclosed gifted deposits.</strong> A vendor-gifted deposit is legitimate when it is fully disclosed to the lender and the conveyancer. Hiding it, or dressing a discount up as a cash deposit that never existed, is mortgage fraud. Disclosure is the line between a genuine mechanism and a crime.</p>
<p><strong>Unexplained sellers and sources.</strong> A seller with no clear reason to sell cheaply, or an unexplained source of the discount or the buyer's funds, is a money-laundering flag. Solicitors and lenders must carry out customer due diligence and source-of-funds checks under the <a href="https://www.gov.uk/guidance/money-laundering-regulations-your-responsibilities" rel="nofollow">Money Laundering Regulations</a>, and a below-market deal can take longer to clear those checks, not less. A genuine purchase with a documented reason and an honest valuation clears them cleanly; a disguised one does not.</p>
<p>There is also a hard regulated line to respect. This guide covers unregulated, business-purpose below-market buying by investors and developers. It is not for consumers. If you are buying a property below value to live in yourself, for example a parent selling you the family home at a discount, a loan secured on that home is likely a regulated mortgage contract and sits outside the scope of this page. In that situation you should speak to an FCA-authorised mortgage adviser. The restriction on promoting qualifying credit is set out in <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2000/8/section/21" rel="nofollow">section 21 of the Financial Services and Markets Act 2000</a>, and the regulated-mortgage boundary is in the FCA's <a href="https://www.handbook.fca.org.uk/handbook/PERG/4/" rel="nofollow">Perimeter Guidance (PERG 4)</a>. This page is educational information about how below-market bridging works and how it is taxed. It is not an invitation to enter a credit agreement, we do not arrange finance, and there is no rate quote, comparison or application here. For the market's own standards, the <a href="https://bdla.org.uk/" rel="nofollow">Bridging and Development Lenders Association</a> is the relevant trade body.</p>

<h2>The exit: refinance to true value, or sell</h2>
<p>Because a bridge is short term and interest accrues throughout, the lender underwrites the exit before it lends. On a below-market purchase there are two credible routes, and both interact with the six-month rule.</p>
<p><strong>Refinance onto a term mortgage.</strong> The most common plan is to hold the property, wait past the six-month ownership point, and refinance onto a term or buy-to-let mortgage that recognises the higher true value. At that stage you may be able to pull the original discount out as equity, repaying the bridge and often releasing cash. This is the "buy below, refinance at value" play, and it only works if a term lender will lend against the open market value once the six months are up. A below-market buy-to-let held this way often exits exactly like a refurbishment deal does; the mechanics overlap with a standard bridge-to-let refinance.</p>
<p><strong>Sale.</strong> If the plan is to buy cheaply, add value and sell, the exit is the sale proceeds. Where the discount was the whole opportunity, some buyers sell on soon after completion, though that route runs straight into the back-to-back scrutiny above and needs to be handled openly. Buying under value with the intention of selling on at a profit tends to look like trading to HMRC, which changes the tax entirely, as the next section explains. <h2>The tax angle: investor or developer, and what stamp duty catches</h2>
<p>Two tax points matter on a below-market purchase, and both are easy to get wrong.</p>
<p><strong>How the interest is treated turns on investor versus developer.</strong> If you buy the discounted property to let and hold it, you are an investor, and for an individual holding residential property the finance costs are relieved only as a basic-rate 20 percent tax reducer under the Section 24 rules, not a full deduction. Hold it in a company and the finance costs are deducted under the loan-relationship rules instead. If you buy below value to refurbish and flip, HMRC will usually see that as trading, the property is stock, and the bridging interest is a trading finance cost that may be capitalised into work in progress or expensed against the trading profit. The discount does not change this analysis; your intended use does. Our guide on whether <a href="/blog/property-types-and-specialist-tax/are-you-a-property-investor-or-developer">you are a property investor or a developer</a> works through the tests HMRC applies, and our detailed treatment of <a href="/blog/property-types-and-specialist-tax/property-development-tax-trading-vs-investment-income">trading versus investment income</a> explains why the same purchase can be taxed two very different ways. For the deductibility of the interest itself across company, individual, residential and commercial situations, see <a href="/blog/property-finance/is-bridging-loan-interest-tax-deductible">is bridging loan interest tax deductible</a>, and HMRC's treatment of interest as a cost of finance is in its <a href="https://www.gov.uk/hmrc-internal-manuals/business-income-manual/bim45650" rel="nofollow">Business Income Manual (BIM45650)</a>.</p>
<p><strong>Stamp duty is charged on what you pay, not what it is worth.</strong> As a general rule, stamp duty land tax is calculated on the chargeable consideration, meaning the price you actually give, so a genuine below-market purchase is taxed on the lower figure, not the open market value. There are exceptions, most notably where a property is transferred to a company connected with the seller, in which case the rules can deem the market value as the consideration. The higher rates for additional dwellings, and the separate regimes in Scotland and Wales, can also apply. Because a discount can interact with the connected-party rules and the surcharge, the land-tax position is worth checking before completion rather than after.</p>
