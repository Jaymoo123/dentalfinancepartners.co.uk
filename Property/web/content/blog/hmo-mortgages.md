<p>An HMO mortgage is the finance behind a house in multiple occupation, a property let room by room to three or more tenants who form more than one household and share a kitchen or bathroom. The reason it is a specialist product, priced and underwritten differently from a standard buy-to-let, is simple arithmetic: the same four walls let by the room can produce two or three times the rent of a single family tenancy, and for larger HMOs that income can be turned directly into the valuation. This page is about the finance mechanics, how HMO lenders value the property, what licensing and planning they check, the experience they expect and how the interest coverage ratio behaves on HMO rent. The HMO <em>tax</em> treatment (expense ratios, Section 24, incorporation) lives on our property tax guides, and we cross-link it rather than repeat it.</p>

<p>Throughout, the framing is a business one. HMO buy-to-let held for investment is unregulated business lending under the <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2001/544/contents">Regulated Activities Order 2001</a>, and everything below assumes a landlord buying or refinancing to let, not an owner-occupier. The one important exception, a live-in-landlord HMO where you or a family member occupy a room, is a regulated mortgage contract, and we flag where that line sits.</p>

<h2>Why an HMO mortgage is a specialist product</h2>
<p>Three features push HMO lending out of the mainstream buy-to-let pool and into a smaller group of specialist and challenger lenders. First, the income is higher and more granular: five or six tenancies rather than one, often with bills included, which lenders read as both higher gross yield and higher management intensity. Second, HMOs carry a licensing and compliance regime that a single let does not, so there is a legal condition (the licence) that has to be satisfied before and during the loan. Third, larger HMOs can be valued on their income rather than on comparable house prices, which changes the whole borrowing calculation and requires a valuer comfortable with that basis.</p>
<p>The practical consequence is a narrower lender panel, deposits that tend to sit at the higher end of the buy-to-let range (commonly 25%, sometimes 30% on large or complex cases), rates typically half a point to a point and a half above an equivalent standard buy-to-let, and underwriting that pays close attention to the licence, the planning position and your track record. None of this makes an HMO harder to fund than it is worth. It makes lender selection the whole game, which is exactly what a specialist broker is for.</p>

<h2>How lenders value an HMO: bricks-and-mortar vs investment value</h2>
<p>This is the single most important mechanic on the page, because the valuation basis decides how much you can borrow. Lenders use one of two approaches.</p>
<p><strong>Bricks-and-mortar (comparable) value</strong> treats the HMO as if it were an ordinary house of the same size in the same location. The room-by-room income is ignored for valuation; the property is worth what a similar family house down the road is worth. Smaller HMOs, typically up to five or six lettable rooms, are frequently valued this way, and the lender pool that does this is wider.</p>
<p><strong>Investment (commercial) value</strong> capitalises the net rental income at a yield to arrive at a figure. Because HMO rent is high, this can produce a valuation well above the bricks-and-mortar figure for the same building. Larger HMOs (often seven rooms and up), purpose-built or heavily converted HMOs, and small blocks are more often valued this way, by a narrower group of specialist and semi-commercial lenders. The upside is more borrowing against the income; the trade-off is a tighter lender pool, usually a larger deposit and more scrutiny of the sustainability of that income.</p>

<p>Confirm the basis a lender will use before you commit, because a case that stacks up on an investment valuation can fall short on bricks-and-mortar, and vice versa. This is a criterion a broker maps to the property before an application, not something to discover at the survey.</p>

<h2>Worked example: the room-count yield in action</h2>
<p>Take a six-bedroom licensed HMO let at £500 per room, £3,000 per month in total. A specialist lender values it at £400,000 and advances 75% loan-to-value, a £300,000 loan. The question is whether the rent supports that loan under the stress test. At a 5.5% stress rate the notional interest on £300,000 is £16,500 a year, £1,375 a month. Held in a limited company (or by a basic-rate borrower) the rent must clear a 125% interest coverage ratio, so the lender needs the property to earn about £1,719 a month. The £3,000 HMO rent clears that with wide headroom.</p>
<p>Now run the same building as a single-family let. As one house it might command £1,400 a month. That is below the £1,719 the same £300,000 loan requires, so the loan fails on rental cover. Same bricks, same borrowing target, opposite outcome. The room-count yield is the entire HMO finance case: the model works because the rent is high relative to the debt, so the interest coverage ratio, which sinks so many standard buy-to-let cases, is rarely the binding constraint on an HMO.</p>

<h2>Licensing, Article 4 and the planning conditions lenders check</h2>
<p>An HMO mortgage is underwritten against a legally compliant HMO, so the licence and planning position are not paperwork, they are conditions of the loan. Mandatory HMO licensing applies to any HMO let to five or more people forming more than one household, under the <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2004/34/contents">Housing Act 2004</a>, and many local authorities operate additional or selective licensing that catches smaller HMOs too. The <a href="https://www.gov.uk/house-in-multiple-occupation-licence">gov.uk HMO licence guidance</a> sets out the framework. An HMO that legally needs a licence but does not have one exposes the landlord to fines and rent repayment orders, which is a direct threat to the income securing the mortgage, so lenders check it.</p>
<p>Two points that catch landlords out. First, the licence caps the number of permitted occupants, and therefore caps the rent the valuation and the ICR can lean on: you cannot underwrite eight rooms of income on a six-person licence. Second, planning. An <a href="https://www.gov.uk/guidance/when-is-permission-required">Article 4 direction</a> removes the permitted-development right to convert a family home into a small HMO in a defined area, so creating (or in some cases continuing) an HMO there needs a planning application. Lenders and valuers check the planning position in Article 4 zones, because an HMO operating without the required consent carries enforcement and value risk. An established, lawful HMO with a licence and clear planning is straightforward; a proposed conversion in an Article 4 area needs the planning route confirmed before finance is sensible.</p>

<h2>Landlord-experience requirements (and the no-experience route)</h2>
<p>Many HMO lenders want a track record. The common bar is one to two years of standard buy-to-let ownership, and for larger or investment-valued HMOs a number of lenders want prior HMO experience specifically. The logic is management intensity: a multi-tenant house with communal areas, licensing obligations and higher turnover is harder to run than a single tenancy, and lenders price and gate for that.</p>
<p>It is not a universal wall, though. A minority of lenders accept first-time landlords onto smaller HMOs, sometimes at a lower loan-to-value, a slightly higher rate, or on condition that a professional letting agent manages the property. If you are buying your first property as an HMO, the answer is lender selection, not abandonment: the route exists, it is just narrower. We cover the general first-property criteria (existing-homeowner requirements, minimum income, top-slicing) in our <a href="/blog/property-finance/first-time-landlord-mortgage">first-time landlord mortgage guide</a>, and a broker maps the no-experience HMO lenders to your case.</p>

<h2>Limited-company and SPV HMO lending</h2>
<p>Most portfolio HMO landlords hold their properties in a limited company or special-purpose vehicle, and HMO lending inside a company is well served. It is generally available at the same loan-to-value bands as personal HMO lending, often with a small rate premium, and underwritten the same way: on the rental income and the interest coverage ratio, with a personal guarantee from the directors and a check that the company's SIC code is one the lender accepts for property letting. Some specialist HMO lenders prefer a clean, single-purpose company holding HMOs rather than a mixed trading entity.</p>
<p>The reason so many HMO landlords incorporate is a tax reason, not a finance one: a company deducts mortgage interest in full against profit, whereas an individual landlord is restricted to a 20% basic-rate tax credit under Section 24. On a highly geared HMO that difference is large, which is why HMO landlords often reach the incorporation breakeven sooner than standard-let landlords. That is a tax decision, and we do not re-argue it here. Work it through with our <a href="/blog/incorporation-and-company-structures/limited-company-vs-personal-ownership-tax-comparison-2026">limited company vs personal ownership tax comparison</a>, the <a href="/blog/section-24-and-tax-relief/section-24-tax-relief-complete-guide">Section 24 tax relief guide</a>, and, specific to this property type, the <a href="/blog/incorporation-and-company-structures/incorporating-an-hmo-into-a-limited-company-pros-and-cons">incorporating an HMO into a limited company guide</a>.</p>

<h2>The interest coverage ratio and stress test on HMO rent</h2>
<p>Every buy-to-let lender applies the rental affordability rules the <a href="https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/prudential-regulation/publication/2016/underwriting-standards-for-buy-to-let-mortgage-contracts">PRA sets out in Supervisory Statement SS13/16</a>. Rent has to cover the mortgage interest by a set margin, the interest coverage ratio (ICR), tested at a stressed interest rate rather than the pay rate. As a market norm in 2026/27, limited-company and basic-rate individual borrowers are stressed at 125% ICR; higher-rate and additional-rate individual borrowers at 145%, reflecting the Section 24 finance-cost restriction that bites on individuals but not companies. Lenders commonly stress the rate at around 5.5%, or the product rate plus 2%, with five-year fixes and pound-for-pound remortgages often stressed lower. Treat those as typical, lender-specific figures and verify at the time you apply.</p>
<p>On an HMO the ICR is usually generous rather than tight, because the room-count rent is high against the loan. Two cautions, though. The rent the lender uses is the achievable rent for the licensed number of occupants with a realistic view on voids, not the theoretical sum of every advertised room at permanent full occupancy. And a higher-rate individual landlord facing the 145% test, rather than a company at 125%, needs meaningfully more rent for the same loan, which is one more reason the personal-versus-company question tends to follow the landlord into HMOs. Model your own figures on the <a href="/calculators/buy-to-let-rental-stress-test-calculator">rental stress-test calculator</a> before you offer.</p>

<h2>When an HMO mortgage becomes a regulated contract (the fence)</h2>
<p>Almost all HMO lending is unregulated business lending, but there is one line to watch. If the borrower, or a member of the borrower's family, occupies one of the rooms, a live-in-landlord or resident-family HMO, the loan can be a <em>regulated</em> mortgage contract rather than business buy-to-let, because more than 40% of the property is then used as a dwelling by the borrower or a related person. That is a consumer product governed by the FCA's conduct rules, and it is not something we introduce. If you plan to live in your HMO, or house a relative in it, speak to an FCA-authorised mortgage adviser about the regulated route. Everything on this page, and everything we introduce, is unregulated, business-purpose HMO finance for property let entirely to unconnected tenants.</p>

<h2>Bringing the tax and finance sides together</h2>
<p>The HMO case is a two-sided one. On the finance side, the room-count yield gives you strong rental cover, but you have to land on the right valuation basis, satisfy the licence and planning, and clear the experience bar with the right lender. On the tax side, HMOs carry higher deductible expense ratios, the Section 24 restriction shapes whether you hold personally or in a company, and geared HMO landlords often incorporate sooner. Those are the two levers that decide the net return, and they interact: the company structure that fixes the Section 24 drag is also the structure most HMO lenders are entirely comfortable lending to.</p>
