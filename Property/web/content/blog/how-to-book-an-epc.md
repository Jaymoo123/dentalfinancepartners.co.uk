<p>The fastest way to get a valid Energy Performance Certificate is often not to book one at all. EPCs last ten years, they survive a sale and any number of re-lets, and every certificate ever lodged sits on a free public register you can search in under a minute. So the correct first step, the one most booking websites will not tell you, is to check whether your property already has one. If it does not, booking is genuinely quick: find an accredited assessor through the government's own service, get the visit done inside a week in most areas, and the certificate is lodged a day or two later.</p>

<p>This page walks that process in order: the register check, who is legally allowed to produce your certificate, the three ways to book and what each adds to the price, the realistic timeline, what happens on the day, and the preparation that can improve the band you end up with. The rules cited here are the <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2012/3118/contents" rel="nofollow noopener">Energy Performance of Buildings (England and Wales) Regulations 2012</a>, so they apply in England and Wales; the gov.uk register and booking services also cover Northern Ireland under its own parallel regulations, while Scotland runs a separate system through its own register, covered at the end.</p>

<h2>Step 0: check whether you already have a valid EPC</h2>

<p>Before you spend anything, search the official register at <a href="https://find-energy-certificate.service.gov.uk" rel="nofollow noopener">find-energy-certificate.service.gov.uk</a>. Enter the property's postcode and the service lists every certificate lodged for that address, with the band, the expiry date and the full recommendation report. The search is free and no login is needed.</p>

<p>The legal test for whether an existing certificate still counts is in <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2012/3118/regulation/9" rel="nofollow noopener">regulation 9(2)</a> of the 2012 Regulations: an EPC is valid if it was entered on the register no more than ten years before the date you make it available, and no later certificate for the building has been registered since. Two practical consequences follow from that wording:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Validity travels with the property, not the owner or the tenancy.</strong> A certificate lodged by the previous owner, or before your current tenant moved in, is still yours to use. You do not need a new EPC for every re-let; if the register shows a certificate under ten years old, that certificate does the job.</li>
<li><strong>A newer certificate always replaces an older one.</strong> The moment a later EPC is lodged, the old one stops being valid, whatever date it carries. This is the supersession rule, and it cuts both ways: it lets you replace a poor band after improvement works, but it also means commissioning a casual re-assessment can overwrite a band you were relying on.</li>
</ul>

<p>Reading the result takes a moment longer than finding it. The register entry shows the band, the score behind it, the certificate number and expiry date, and the full recommendation report: the list of improvements the last assessor identified, with indicative costs and the band each would achieve. That report is worth skimming even when the certificate is valid, because it tells you where the property sits relative to the next band boundary, which is precisely the information you need if you are weighing improvement works before a re-let. Note the certificate number too; agents, conveyancers and mortgage lenders ask for it, and quoting it saves everyone repeating the search.</p>

<p>If the register shows a valid certificate with a band you are content with, you are done. Download it, keep a copy for your compliance file, and spend the booking fee on something else. If there is no certificate, it has expired, or the band is a problem for letting, carry on to the booking steps below.</p>

<h2>Who is allowed to produce your EPC</h2>

<p>Not anyone with a clipboard. Under <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2012/3118/regulation/22" rel="nofollow noopener">regulation 22</a> of the 2012 Regulations, an energy assessor must be a member of an accreditation scheme approved by the Secretary of State. Approved schemes are required to vet their members as qualified, fit and proper persons, to operate a published code of conduct and a complaints procedure, to carry indemnity arrangements, and to lodge their members' certificates on the official register. An assessment from someone outside an approved scheme cannot be lodged, and a certificate that is not on the register does not exist for legal purposes.</p>

<p>For a house or flat you need a domestic energy assessor, often abbreviated to DEA. Commercial buildings need a non-domestic assessor holding a different qualification, and larger or more complex commercial buildings need higher-level assessors still; if you hold commercial property, the process and pricing differ enough that you should read the commercial guidance separately rather than assume the domestic rules carry across.</p>

<p>Finding an accredited assessor is deliberately easy. The government runs a service at <a href="https://www.gov.uk/get-new-energy-certificate" rel="nofollow noopener">gov.uk/get-new-energy-certificate</a>: enter the property's postcode, say what type of property it is, and it lists accredited assessors covering that area with their contact details. Everyone on the list has already passed the accreditation test, so your choice comes down to price, availability and reviews.</p>

<h2>The three ways to book, compared</h2>

<p>Every EPC is produced the same way by the same kind of accredited assessor, so what you are really choosing between the booking routes is speed, convenience and how many margins sit between you and the person doing the work.</p>

<ol>
<li><strong>Direct to an assessor via the gov.uk service.</strong> Use the find-an-assessor lookup above, ring or email two or three local assessors, and book with whoever offers the best combination of price and date. This is usually the cheapest route because there is no intermediary, and it is often the fastest too, because you are speaking to the diary owner directly. Ten minutes of phoning typically beats any comparison site.</li>
<li><strong>Online EPC brokers.</strong> A large slice of the search results for EPC booking are broker platforms: you pay them, they subcontract a local assessor. The convenience is real, one form and done, but the broker's margin sits inside the price, and you have no control over which assessor turns up or when the certificate gets lodged. If a broker's headline price undercuts local assessors, check what turnaround it actually commits to; the cheapest tier is often the slowest.</li>
<li><strong>Letting or estate agent add-on.</strong> Agents will happily arrange the EPC as part of taking a property to market. It is the zero-effort route, but the agent typically adds a handling margin to an assessor they use in volume, so it tends to be the most expensive way to buy exactly the same certificate. If you use it, ask what the EPC line on the invoice actually costs versus a direct booking; the difference is often the price of the phone call you did not make.</li>
</ol>

<p>None of these routes changes the certificate you receive. The band is determined by the property and the assessor's survey, not by who took the booking fee.</p>

<p>Whichever route you use, four questions before you confirm the booking will save most of the problems people complain about afterwards:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>"When will the certificate be lodged?"</strong> Not when the visit is; when the certificate will be on the register. Get a date.</li>
<li><strong>"Is that price final?"</strong> Confirm it covers the whole job with no travel charge, no urgency supplement, and VAT included if the assessor is registered.</li>
<li><strong>"What do you need access to?"</strong> Loft, boiler, cylinder, meters, every room. Agreeing this up front avoids a second visit fee, especially in a tenanted property where access needs arranging with notice.</li>
<li><strong>"What is your accreditation number?"</strong> Any legitimate assessor gives it without hesitation, and it takes seconds to confirm they are practising. A booking route that cannot name the assessor before payment cannot answer this, which tells you something about the route.</li>
</ul>

<h2>Booking to certificate: the real timeline</h2>

<p>There is no statutory turnaround standard for producing an EPC, so treat the figures here as typical market practice, not a legal entitlement. With that label attached: in most of England and Wales an assessor can visit within two to five working days of booking, and in cities with plenty of assessors, next-day or same-day visits are widely available for an express fee. After the visit, the assessor processes the survey data and lodges the certificate on the register, usually within one to two working days. Booking to lodged certificate is therefore commonly under a week end to end.</p>

<p>What stretches the timeline is worth knowing in advance:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Lodgement, not the visit, is the finish line.</strong> The certificate has legal effect once it is entered on the register. An assessor who visits Monday but lodges Friday has not given you an EPC until Friday. When booking, ask when they lodge, not just when they can come.</li>
<li><strong>Rural coverage is thinner.</strong> Fewer assessors covering a wider area means longer waits and sometimes travel charges. Book earlier and expect less same-day availability.</li>
<li><strong>Access problems cause repeat visits.</strong> A locked loft hatch, an inaccessible boiler cupboard or a tenant who refuses entry can turn one visit into two. The preparation list below removes most of these.</li>
<li><strong>Broker queues add days.</strong> The subcontracting step between paying a broker and an assessor actually ringing you is where cheap-tier bookings quietly lose a week.</li>
</ul>

<p>If you need the certificate urgently because marketing is imminent, the law is more forgiving than the folklore. Under <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2012/3118/regulation/7" rel="nofollow noopener">regulation 7</a>, what must happen before a building is put on the market is that an EPC has been <em>commissioned</em>; you then have to use all reasonable efforts to obtain it within 7 days of marketing beginning, with a further 21 days as a backstop if that genuinely fails. So a confirmed booking with an accredited assessor, made before the listing goes live and evidenced in writing, is what regulation 7 asks of you on day one. What you cannot do is market first and think about the EPC later.</p>

<h2>What happens during the assessment</h2>

<p>A domestic EPC assessment is a visual, non-invasive survey. For a typical house or flat it takes between 40 minutes and an hour and a half; large or heavily extended properties take longer. The assessor does not drill holes, lift floorboards or dismantle anything, and the property does not need to be empty. A tenanted property can be assessed perfectly well, subject to the tenant's cooperation and your usual notice obligations for access.</p>

<p>Working room by room, the assessor records the things the EPC methodology scores: the construction type and age of the walls, the loft and its insulation depth, the windows and their glazing, the main heating system and its controls, the hot water arrangement, the proportion of low-energy lighting, and any renewables such as solar panels. They measure the floor area, photograph the evidence, and note the meter types. Everything feeds a standard calculation model which produces the band, from A down to G, and the recommendation report that accompanies it.</p>

<p>The lodged certificate comes with a recommendation report generated from the same survey data: a ranked list of improvements, from cheap wins such as low-energy lighting up to wall insulation and heating replacement, each with an indicative cost range and the band the property would reach if it were done. Treat the indicative costs as rough national figures rather than quotes, but treat the band projections seriously; they are calculated on your property's actual data, and for a landlord they amount to a free feasibility study for the next band boundary.</p>

<p>Two points about how the scoring works explain why preparation matters. First, the assessor can only credit what they can see or verify: features hidden from view with no supporting documents are recorded by default assumptions based on the property's age, and defaults are usually worse than reality. Second, the assessment is a snapshot; nothing you say about planned works counts. If improvements are close to done, finish them before the visit rather than the week after.</p>

<h2>What to have ready for a better band</h2>

<p>You cannot talk an assessor into a better band, but you can make sure the property gets every point it has actually earned. Before the visit:</p>

<ol>
<li><strong>Clear access to the loft hatch</strong> and put a ladder nearby. If the assessor cannot see the loft insulation, they cannot credit its true depth, and a defaulted loft can cost a band boundary on its own.</li>
<li><strong>Unblock the boiler and hot water cylinder</strong>, and find the boiler manual or at least the make and model. An identified modern condensing boiler scores better than an unidentifiable one recorded by age assumptions.</li>
<li><strong>Gather insulation evidence.</strong> Cavity wall insulation is invisible from inside and out; the installation certificate or guarantee is the only way it gets credited. The same goes for internal or external solid wall insulation and underfloor insulation: invoices, certificates and building control sign-offs all count.</li>
<li><strong>Collect improvement invoices</strong> for windows (FENSA or CERTASS certificates), heating controls, new radiators and any renewables, with dates. Dated paperwork lets the assessor record the actual specification rather than a default.</li>
<li><strong>Note the meters and lighting.</strong> Know where the meters are, and if you are mid-way through swapping bulbs to LED, finish the job before the visit; low-energy lighting percentage is one of the cheapest scoring inputs to improve.</li>
</ol>

<p>For a landlord there is a second, quieter reason to assemble that invoice file: the same paperwork that gets the band credited is the paper trail your accountant needs to split improvement works between revenue repairs and capital improvements when your tax return is prepared. One folder serves both purposes, and it is much easier to build at booking time than to reconstruct years later.</p>

<h2>Landlord timing traps</h2>

<p>Three timing rules catch landlords specifically, and all three are about sequence rather than cost.</p>

<p><strong>The EPC comes before marketing, not before completion.</strong> Regulation 7's commissioning duty, described above, bites the day the listing goes live, and it binds your letting agent as well as you: a person acting on your behalf must be satisfied an EPC has been commissioned before they market the property. Separately, <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2012/3118/regulation/6" rel="nofollow noopener">regulation 6</a> requires you to show a valid EPC free of charge to every prospective tenant or buyer at the earliest opportunity, and to give a copy to whoever ultimately takes the property. Booking the assessment the same day you instruct the agent keeps both duties comfortably satisfied.</p>

<p><strong>The band decides whether you can let at all.</strong> The EPC regulations answer only whether a certificate exists for the transaction. A separate regime, the minimum energy efficiency standard, uses the band on that certificate to decide whether the property may lawfully be let, currently requiring band E or better for most tenancies unless an exemption is registered. If the register check at step 0, or a fresh assessment, lands the property near that floor, read our <a href="/blog/landlord-tax-essentials/mees-regulations-landlords">MEES guide for landlords</a> before granting the next tenancy, because the time to deal with a failing band is before you have a tenant waiting.</p>

<p><strong>Re-book straight after improvement works.</strong> Because a newer certificate supersedes the old one under regulation 9(2), a voluntary re-assessment immediately after insulation, a new boiler or new glazing is how the better band becomes real. Landlords who improve a property mid-certificate and never re-assess are letting against a stale band, which can mean sitting closer to the MEES floor on paper than the property actually is. If the works were part-funded through a grant scheme, our guide to <a href="/blog/landlord-tax-essentials/epc-improvement-grant-schemes-landlords-eco4-bus-gbis">EPC improvement grants for landlords</a> covers what ECO4, the Boiler Upgrade Scheme and GBIS expect, and the same completion paperwork feeds the re-assessment.</p>

<h2>Cost in one line, and where the depth lives</h2>

<p>Most domestic EPCs cost between about £35 and £120, with the spread driven by property size, region, urgency and how many intermediaries sit in the booking chain; the full breakdown, including typical ranges by property type and the trade-offs hiding inside the cheapest quotes, lives in our <a href="/blog/landlord-tax-essentials/epc-certificate-cost-uk">EPC certificate cost guide</a>.</p>

<p>For a landlord the fee has one pleasant footnote: it is a deductible revenue expense of the property business. Property business profits are computed using the same rules as trading profits under ITTOIA 2005 section 272, including the wholly and exclusively test, and HMRC's <a href="https://www.gov.uk/hmrc-internal-manuals/property-income-manual/pim2010" rel="nofollow noopener">Property Income Manual at PIM2010</a> confirms the trading expense rules apply to rental income. A certificate commissioned to let or re-let a property is incurred wholly and exclusively for the rental business, so it belongs among your running costs for the year alongside gas safety checks and agent fees; our <a href="/calculators/portfolio-profitability-calculator">portfolio profitability calculator</a> is a quick way to see what your full cost stack does to the net position. The works the EPC recommends are a different tax question entirely, revenue repair or capital improvement depending on what is done, which is exactly why the invoice file from the preparation step earns its keep twice.</p>

And if the register check or the new certificate leaves you weighing improvement spend against tax relief, that is a conversation worth having with a property tax specialist before the invoices are committed, because how the work is described and evidenced can change how it is treated.</p>
