<p>An EICR for a typical rental property costs between £120 and £250 in 2026, rising to £350 or more for large houses and £450 or more for HMOs, based on market rates published by national trade directories and assessor booking platforms in August 2026. The report is valid for up to five years, so even at the top of the range it works out at well under £100 a year per property, and the whole fee is deductible against your rental income. This page covers the rules for England under the Electrical Safety Standards in the Private Rented Sector and Social Rented Sector (England) Regulations 2020; Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland run their own versions, covered near the end.</p>

<p>The price question is rarely just the report fee, though. The quote varies with the size and age of the installation, the region, and whether you bundle it with other compliance checks, and an unsatisfactory report triggers remedial work that can cost anything from a £100 socket repair to a four-figure rewire. There is also a tax layer that electrician directories cannot write: the fee and most remedial work are deductible, some remedial work is capital, and the penalty for not having a report, now up to £40,000 per breach, attracts no tax relief at all. All of that is below.</p>

<h2>What drives the quote up or down</h2>

<p>Five drivers explain almost every gap between two quotes for the same postcode:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Circuits, not bedrooms.</strong> The inspector tests every circuit on the consumer unit. A three-bed flat with six circuits is a shorter job than a three-bed house with twelve, which is why quoting by bedroom count alone produces surprises. Ask how many circuits the price covers and what an extra circuit costs.</li>
<li><strong>Region.</strong> London and the South East price above the national range, often by 20% to 40% for the same property size. Rural areas can also carry a premium where travel time is built into the quote.</li>
<li><strong>Age and condition of the installation.</strong> A property last rewired decades ago, with an old fuse board, mixed wiring colours or previous DIY work, takes longer to test and is far more likely to generate codes. Some assessors price the extra time in upfront once they hear the age of the property.</li>
<li><strong>Access and urgency.</strong> A vacant property with clear access to the consumer unit is the cheap version. Tenanted properties needing appointment coordination, or a report needed within days because a new tenancy is starting, push the price up. Booking ahead of your renewal date is the easiest saving available.</li>
<li><strong>Bundling.</strong> Many firms discount when the EICR is booked alongside the annual gas check or an EPC renewal. If your certificates can be aligned to the same visit windows, the combined cost drops; our <a href="/blog/landlord-tax-essentials/gas-safety-certificate-cost">gas safety certificate cost</a> and <a href="/blog/landlord-tax-essentials/epc-certificate-cost-uk">EPC certificate cost</a> pages give the standalone ranges to compare bundle quotes against.</li>
</ul>

<h2>HMOs and licensed properties: why they pay more</h2>

<p>An HMO is the expensive row of the table for mechanical reasons: more circuits, more accessories, and frequently multiple kitchens and shower rooms, each adding testing time. Shared houses also see harder wear on sockets and switches, so the probability of C2 codes, and therefore a remedial bill, is higher than in a comparable single let. On top of the mechanics sits licensing. A licensed HMO's conditions require the landlord to produce the electrical report to the authority on demand, and licence application forms commonly ask for the current EICR date, so an expired report can hold up an application or renewal. That interaction cuts both ways on cost: the licensing deadline removes your flexibility on timing, which is exactly the urgency driver that inflates quotes, so HMO landlords gain most from booking inspections well before both the report expiry and the licence renewal date. The licence fee itself raises its own deduction questions, which our <a href="/blog/property-types-and-specialist-tax/hmo-licensing-fees-tax-deductible-uk-landlords">HMO licensing fees deductibility</a> page works through.</p>

<h2>What the price includes, and what costs extra</h2>

<p>The fee buys an inspection and test of the fixed electrical installation, the wiring, consumer unit, earthing and bonding, sockets, switches and light fittings, by a qualified person working to BS 7671, the IET Wiring Regulations. BS 7671 is the technical standard the law points at, not legislation itself. The deliverable is the Electrical Installation Condition Report. One terminology point worth knowing: there is no separate "landlord electrical safety certificate" document in law; the EICR is the report, and anyone selling you a certificate on top of it is selling paper.</p>

<p>Who does the work also shapes the price. The regulations require a "qualified person", in practice an electrician competent to inspect and test to BS 7671, and membership of a recognised competent person scheme such as NICEIC or NAPIT is the usual evidence. Scheme-registered firms sometimes quote slightly more than an unaffiliated electrician, but a report from an inspector whose competence you cannot evidence is a false economy: if the authority questions the report, proving the inspector was qualified is your problem. Ask for scheme registration or equivalent qualifications with the quote.</p>

<p>The report classifies observations with four codes, and the codes are what determine your follow-on bill:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>C1, danger present.</strong> Immediate risk. The inspector will usually make it safe on the spot and the underlying fix is urgent.</li>
<li><strong>C2, potentially dangerous.</strong> Requires remedial work. One or more C2s makes the report unsatisfactory.</li>
<li><strong>C3, improvement recommended.</strong> Does not make the report unsatisfactory and triggers no legal obligation. You can decline C3 work, though some items are cheap resilience worth buying while the electrician is there.</li>
<li><strong>FI, further investigation required.</strong> Treated like a fail: the investigation must happen, and promptly.</li>
</ul>

<p>Where the report is unsatisfactory, regulation 3 requires the remedial or investigative work to be completed within 28 days, or sooner if the report says so, with written confirmation then supplied to the tenants and the local housing authority within 28 days of completion. Illustrative remedial ranges: small C2 fixes such as socket replacements or bonding corrections commonly land between £100 and £500; a consumer unit replacement is usually a mid-hundreds job; a partial rewire runs into the low thousands; and a full rewire of a three-bed house spans roughly £3,000 to £8,000 depending on size, access and redecorating. An unsatisfactory report is not money wasted, and you do not pay for a whole new EICR after the fixes: written confirmation of the completed work, often a modest re-visit fee if a different electrician did the remedials, closes it out until the five-year cycle ends.</p>

<h2>Why you are buying this at all: the duty cycle in brief</h2>

<p>The purchase is compulsory. Under <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2020/312/regulation/3">regulation 3 of the 2020 Regulations</a>, a landlord in England must have the installation inspected and tested by a qualified person at least every five years, or sooner if the last report requires it; give a copy of the report to each existing tenant within 28 days of the inspection; give the most recent report to a new tenant before they occupy; supply it to the local housing authority within seven days of a written request; and complete any remedial work within 28 days or the shorter period the report specifies.</p>

<p>A small number of tenancy types are excluded under <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2020/312/schedule/1">Schedule 1</a>, principally long leases of seven years or more, accommodation shared with the landlord or the landlord's family, student halls of residence, hostels and refuges, care homes and hospital accommodation. Two changes from November 2025 are worth noting because older summaries miss them: the exclusion for registered providers of social housing was removed, extending the regime to the social rented sector, and the regulations were renamed accordingly. If you hold a flat on a long lease and let it out, the exclusion for long leases does not help you as a landlord: it excludes the long lease itself, not the assured shorthold tenancy you grant your tenant, so your letting still needs a valid report.</p>

<p>Letting outside England, the cost is similar but the legal hook is different. Scotland has required an electrical inspection of private rented property since December 2015 under the repairing standard, also on a five-year cycle, so Scottish landlords have been buying EICRs for a decade. Wales requires electrical testing at least every five years under the Renting Homes (Wales) Act 2016 fitness framework for occupation contracts. Northern Ireland has taken powers for an equivalent scheme under the Private Tenancies Act (Northern Ireland) 2022, with the detail being phased in, so NI landlords should check current official guidance before assuming no duty applies. Market prices for the inspection itself do not differ much by nation; what differs is the enforcement machinery behind it.</p>

<p>That is deliberately the compressed version. The full compliance mechanics, who counts as a qualified person, the remedial and urgent remedial procedure, local authority enforcement powers and the penalty machinery, are covered on our companion page on the <a href="/blog/landlord-tax-essentials/landlord-electrical-safety-certificate">landlord electrical safety certificate obligations</a>. This page stays on cost.</p>

<h2>What non-compliance costs instead</h2>

<p>The alternative to a three-figure report is exposure to a five-figure penalty. Under <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2020/312/regulation/11">regulation 11</a>, the local housing authority can impose a financial penalty of up to £40,000 for a breach of the electrical safety duties. The cap was £30,000 when the regulations were made in 2020; it was raised to £40,000 by the amendment regulations that extended the regime to the social rented sector, in force from 1 November 2025, so any page still quoting £30,000 is describing the old law. The regulation also expressly permits more than one penalty for a continuing failure, so the cap is per breach, not per landlord: a portfolio landlord with several non-compliant properties, or one who ignores a penalty and carries on, faces multiples of it.</p>

<p>Keep this regime separate from licensing penalties, because they are routinely mixed up. A civil penalty of up to £40,000 per offence is also available under section 249A of the Housing Act 2004 for licensing offences, such as running an unlicensed HMO. The identical caps make the confusion easier, but they are different regimes with different triggers, and a landlord running an unlicensed HMO with no EICR can face both: a licensing penalty for the missing licence and electrical safety penalties for the missing report. Our page on <a href="/blog/property-types-and-specialist-tax/hmo-selective-licensing-compliance-housing-act-2004-landlord-licensing-mechanics">HMO and selective licensing mechanics</a> covers the licensing side.</p>

<p>Two further costs sit behind the headline penalty. The authority can arrange remedial action itself, with the tenant's consent, and recover its costs from you, which means paying for the electrical work anyway plus the authority's expenses. And none of it is tax-deductible, which the next section explains.</p>

<h2>The tax treatment: fee, remedials, penalties</h2>

<p>This is the layer a price-comparison site cannot give you, and it changes the real cost of every figure above.</p>

<p><strong>The report fee is revenue-deductible.</strong> Rental profits are computed using the trading income rules that <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2005/5/section/272">ITTOIA 2005 section 272</a> applies to property businesses, including the wholly-and-exclusively test. A compulsory regulatory cost of letting the property, which is exactly what an EICR is, passes that test comfortably, so the fee comes off your rental income in the year you incur it. A £200 report costs a higher-rate taxpayer £120 after relief. To see what a deductible expense does to your own liability, run the numbers through our <a href="/calculators/rental-income-tax-calculator">rental income tax calculator</a>. The same logic applies to a company landlord, where the fee is deductible against corporation tax, and the reasoning mirrors licence fees, on which our page covering <a href="/blog/property-types-and-specialist-tax/hmo-licensing-fees-tax-deductible-uk-landlords">whether HMO licensing fees are tax-deductible</a> sets the precedent.</p>

<p><strong>A pre-letting EICR is normally deductible too.</strong> A report obtained before your first tenant moves in, which regulation 3 requires, does not lose relief just because the rental business has not started. The pre-trading rules treat qualifying expenses incurred in the seven years before the business begins as incurred on day one, provided they would have been deductible had the business been running.</p>

<p><strong>Remedial work splits between revenue and capital.</strong> The dividing line is repair versus improvement. Like-for-like work, replacing damaged sockets, correcting earthing, renewing faulty sections of wiring, or swapping an old consumer unit for its modern equivalent, is a repair to the entirety that is the property, and deductible as a revenue expense even where the new part is technically better because standards have moved on. Work that materially upgrades the installation beyond its previous character is different: a first-time full rewire that substantially enhances a property, most obviously as part of a wider refurbishment, can be capital. Capital treatment is not relief lost, it is relief moved: the spend becomes enhancement expenditure within <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1992/12/section/38">TCGA 1992 section 38(1)(b)</a>, added to your base cost and reducing the capital gain when you sell. The practical discipline is to keep the remedial invoice itemised, because a single bill can contain both revenue repairs and capital improvement, and an itemised invoice lets you claim each line correctly.</p>

<p><strong>Penalties get no relief.</strong> A regulation 11 penalty is not deductible against rental profits. HMRC's Business Income Manual at <a href="https://www.gov.uk/hmrc-internal-manuals/business-income-manual/bim38515">BIM38515</a> states the long-standing position that penalties for infractions of the law are not allowable: a fine punishes you, it does not earn your rent, and it fails the wholly-and-exclusively test. The asymmetry is the whole compliance argument in one line: the £200 report is fully deductible and the £40,000 penalty is fully non-deductible.</p>

<p>If your remedial bill is large, or a rewire is tangled up with a refurbishment, the revenue-capital split is worth an hour of a property tax specialist's time before you file, because claiming a capital rewire as a repair is a common enquiry point and the correct split is usually defensible with good invoices.</p>

<h2>Budgeting the five-year cycle: two worked examples</h2>

<p><strong>Priya, portfolio landlord, six terraced houses.</strong> Priya's EICRs fall due in different years because the properties were bought at different times. Rather than let renewal dates ambush her, she puts each property on a rolling budget line: six reports at around £200 each is £1,200 per five-year cycle, £240 a year across the portfolio, £40 per property per year. She adds a remedial contingency of £150 per property per cycle, since her stock is 1900s terraces where a C2 or two per inspection is realistic, taking the electrical line to roughly £420 a year. All of it is revenue-deductible, so as a higher-rate taxpayer her after-tax cost is about £250 a year for portfolio-wide electrical compliance. She also books each EICR two months before expiry, which keeps her out of urgency pricing and leaves the 28-day remedial window comfortably inside the old report's validity. Figures from the verified table above; the contingency is an illustrative planning figure.</p>

<p><strong>Tom, accidental landlord, one-bed flat.</strong> Tom inherited a flat and is letting it for the first time, so regulation 3 requires a report before his tenant occupies. His report costs £150 and comes back unsatisfactory with two C2s: a cracked socket in the kitchen and an ageing consumer unit without residual current protection. The electrician quotes £600 for the consumer unit swap and the socket, completes the work within the 28-day window, and issues written confirmation for £60. Total outlay £810, every pound of it revenue, the report as a compulsory regulatory cost and the works as like-for-like repairs, and all deductible against his first year of rental income under the pre-trading rules even though the spend landed before the tenancy started. The one-line contrast: had the flat instead needed a first-time full rewire as part of a gut refurbishment before letting, that element could have been capital, relieved against his eventual gain under TCGA 1992 section 38(1)(b) rather than against this year's rent. Prices are illustrative within the verified ranges above.</p>

<h2>Where this leaves you</h2>

<p>Price the report at £120 to £350 for a normal single let, more for an HMO, budget a remedial contingency scaled to the age of your installation, and book ahead of the renewal date rather than into it. Claim the fee and the like-for-like remedials against your rental income, itemise anything that smells like improvement, and never plan around the penalty, because it is the one number on this page that comes with no tax relief.
