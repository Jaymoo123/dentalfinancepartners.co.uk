  - question: "What is mezzanine finance in property development?"
    answer: "Mezzanine finance is subordinated, second-charge debt that sits between senior development debt and the developer's own equity. It tops up leverage when senior debt alone will not cover enough of the cost, commonly stretching total borrowing to around 85% to 90% of project cost. Because the mezzanine lender ranks behind the senior lender and is repaid only after the senior facility, it carries a much higher coupon and often a fee stack and sometimes a profit share. It is the tool for preserving your equity across more schemes at the price of a higher blended cost of capital."
  - question: "What is the difference between mezzanine and JV finance?"
    answer: "Mezzanine is debt: you pay a coupon and fees and repay the capital, but you keep all of the profit and all of the control. JV finance is equity: a partner funds the gap in return for a share of the profit, not interest, so you give up a slice of the upside and usually some control, but you carry no debt-service coupon during the build and the partner shares the downside. In short, mezzanine costs yield but keeps ownership; a JV costs ownership but shares risk. The right answer depends on the margin, your appetite for gearing, and how much of the scheme you want to keep."
  - question: "How much of the cost can mezzanine finance cover?"
    answer: "Mezzanine is sized by reference to the whole stack rather than as a standalone loan. Senior debt typically covers up to around 65% to 70% of total cost; mezzanine then tops the combined debt up to roughly 85% to 90% of cost, and sometimes higher on a strong scheme with an experienced developer. So on a scheme where senior debt reaches 65% and you hold 15% equity, mezzanine of around 20% of cost closes the gap. Every lender sizes it against loan to gross development value as well as loan to cost, so the finished-scheme valuation caps how far it stretches.   - question: "Why is mezzanine finance so expensive?"
    answer: "Price follows risk and priority. The mezzanine lender ranks behind the senior lender on the security and is repaid only after the senior facility is cleared, so if the scheme underperforms the mezzanine capital is the first meaningful money at risk after your own equity. That subordination, plus the higher leverage it enables, means the coupon is well above the senior rate and there is usually an arrangement fee, an exit fee, and sometimes a profit share or equity kicker on top. The all-in cost of the mezzanine slice is what matters, not the headline coupon, and it should be modelled against the marginal profit the extra leverage lets you earn."
  - question: "How does a property development joint venture work?"
    answer: "In a development JV, one party brings the money and the other brings the scheme, the expertise and the delivery. The money partner funds the equity gap the developer cannot cover; the developer sources the site, secures planning, runs the build and manages the sale. Profit is split by agreement once the senior and any mezzanine debt is repaid, commonly on something approaching an equal share for the funded gap, though the split reflects who contributes what. The terms are set out in a JV agreement or shareholders' agreement covering profit split, decision rights, what happens on overrun, and the exit. It is equity, so the partner shares the downside as well as the upside."
  - question: "What is a deed of priority or intercreditor agreement?"
    answer: "It is the document that ranks the lenders against each other on the same security. When a scheme has both senior debt and mezzanine, the senior lender takes a first charge and the mezzanine lender a second charge, and the deed of priority (or intercreditor agreement) records who is repaid first, how much the senior debt can grow to, and what each lender can and cannot do on a default. The senior lender must consent to the mezzanine going in behind it, which is why you cannot simply bolt mezzanine on without the senior lender at the table. It protects the priority the senior lender priced its facility around."
  - question: "Is mezzanine finance interest tax deductible?"
    answer: "For a genuine development trade, mezzanine interest is a cost of the trade, so it is deductible against the development profit rather than restricted like an individual landlord's residential finance costs. In a company, finance costs fall under the loan relationship rules in CTA 2009 and are relieved as they accrue, subject to anti-avoidance and any interest-restriction rules for large groups. Interest is often capitalised into work in progress and relieved when the units are sold. The Section 24 residential finance-cost restriction, which limits an individual landlord to a 20% tax reducer, does not touch a build-to-sell trade. Treatment turns on trading versus investment status, so confirm your position before you draw the facility."
  - question: "How is a JV profit share taxed?"
    answer: "It depends entirely on how the JV is structured. A profit share paid as a distribution from a JV company is a dividend in the recipient's hands, not a deductible cost to the company, which is very different from interest. A profit share routed through a partnership or LLP is taxed as each member's share of the trading profit. A preferred return dressed up as a coupon may be recharacterised depending on the terms. Because the label on the payment does not decide the tax, and the wrong structure can turn a deductible cost into a non-deductible distribution, the JV structure should be modelled for tax before the agreement is signed, not after."
  - question: "Do I give up control with a JV partner?"
    answer: "Usually yes, to some degree. Because a JV partner takes equity and shares the risk, they will expect a say over the decisions that affect their money: the budget, material variations, the sales strategy, and often a veto over further borrowing or a sale below a floor price. How much control you cede is a matter of negotiation and is set out in the JV or shareholders' agreement. Mezzanine, by contrast, is debt, so the mezzanine lender takes security and covenants but does not own a share of the profit or the day-to-day decisions, though it will monitor the scheme closely. If keeping control matters more than keeping yield, that points towards debt over equity."
  - question: "Do I need senior debt in place before arranging mezzanine?"
    answer: "In practice, yes. Mezzanine is defined by its position behind the senior facility, so the senior terms have to be known before the mezzanine can be sized and priced, and the senior lender has to consent to a second charge ranking behind it through a deed of priority. Some lenders offer a combined senior-and-mezzanine (stretch senior) facility from one house, which removes the intercreditor negotiation and can be quicker, at a blended price. Either way the mezzanine layer cannot be settled in isolation; it is always underwritten as part of the whole stack against loan to cost and loan to gross development value."
  - question: "What is a profit share or equity kicker on mezzanine debt?"
    answer: "Some mezzanine lenders take part of their return as a share of the scheme's profit on top of, or instead of, part of the coupon. This is often called a profit share or an equity kicker. It aligns the lender with the outcome and can lower the cash coupon during the build, but it means a strong scheme pays the lender more than a plain-interest facility would. It also blurs the line between debt and equity for tax, because a payment tied to profit can be treated differently from interest. Model the total cost of the mezzanine, coupon plus fees plus any profit share, against the profit the extra leverage actually unlocks."

<p>Most property developments do not fund from a single source. Senior debt covers the bulk of the cost, the developer puts in some cash, and there is almost always a gap between the two. Mezzanine finance and joint venture (JV) equity are the two ways to fill that gap, and they are the most expensive and most consequential layers of the whole structure. One is debt that costs yield; the other is equity that costs ownership. Choosing between them, and pricing each correctly, decides how much of the profit you keep and how much risk you carry.</p>
<p>This guide sets out where each layer sits in the capital stack, how far mezzanine stretches your leverage, how a JV profit share differs from a coupon, and the cost-versus-dilution-versus-control trade-off with a worked example. It then covers the deed of priority the senior lender will insist on, and how each layer is taxed. It is education, not a financial promotion: there is no rate table, no lender recommendation and no invitation to borrow. If you are weighing these structures, the tax treatment of the finance is part of the sum, and that is where we can help.</p>

<h2>Where mezzanine and JV finance sit in the capital stack</h2>
<p>Think of the money in a development as a stack, ordered by who gets repaid first if the scheme is wound up. At the bottom, first in the queue and cheapest, is <strong>senior debt</strong>: a first charge over the site, typically funding up to around 65% to 70% of total project cost and capped against a percentage of gross development value. At the top, last in the queue and most exposed, is the <strong>developer's own equity</strong>, which absorbs the first losses and earns the residual profit once everyone else is paid.</p>
<p>The gap between senior debt and the equity you can or want to commit is where mezzanine and JV finance live. Mezzanine slots in above the senior debt and below your equity as subordinated debt. JV equity sits alongside or in place of your own equity, at the top of the stack, sharing the same last-in-line risk you carry. That position in the queue is the single fact that explains everything else about how each is priced and taxed: the further up the stack, the greater the risk, the higher the required return, and the more the provider will want in exchange.</p>
<p>Because both layers are underwritten as part of the whole structure, neither can be settled in isolation. A lender or partner assesses the combined loan to cost, the loan to gross development value, your track record and the exit before agreeing where they will sit and what they will charge.</p>

<h2>What mezzanine finance is and how far it stretches leverage</h2>
<p>Mezzanine finance is subordinated, second-charge debt that tops up the senior facility. Where senior debt reaches around 65% to 70% of cost, mezzanine commonly stretches the combined borrowing to roughly 85% to 90% of total cost, and occasionally higher on a strong scheme run by an experienced developer. The practical effect is that it shrinks the cash you have to put in, so the same equity can support more schemes at once.</p>
<p>That leverage is not free. The mezzanine lender ranks behind the senior lender on the security and is repaid only after the senior debt is cleared, so its capital is the first meaningful money at risk after your own. To compensate for that subordination, the coupon sits well above the senior rate, and there is usually an arrangement fee, an exit fee, and sometimes a profit share or equity kicker on top. The number that matters is the all-in cost of the mezzanine slice, not the headline coupon, and it should be weighed against the marginal profit the extra leverage lets you earn. Pricing moves with the market and is deal-specific; the figures here are indicative ranges as at July 2026, and you should verify current terms with a lender or broker.</p>
<p>Some lenders package the senior and mezzanine layers into a single "stretch senior" facility from one house. That removes the intercreditor negotiation and can be faster, at a blended price. It is the same idea, delivered without a second lender at the table. Our <a href="/blog/property-finance/development-finance-guide">guide to development finance</a> covers how the senior facility itself is sized and drawn.</p>

<h2>What JV finance is: equity for a profit share</h2>
<p>Joint venture finance is not a loan. A JV partner funds the equity gap in return for a share of the profit, once the senior and any mezzanine debt is repaid. There is no coupon to service during the build and no capital to repay on a fixed date; the partner is paid out of the profit at the end, and shares the loss if there is one. In its cleanest form, one party brings the money and the other brings the scheme, the planning, the delivery and the sale.</p>
<p>Because the partner takes equity and carries the downside, they expect a meaningful slice of the upside and usually a say in the decisions that affect their money: the budget, material variations, the sales strategy, and often a veto over further borrowing or a sale below a floor price. The commercial terms live in a JV agreement or shareholders' agreement covering the profit split, decision rights, what happens on a cost overrun, and how and when each party exits. A JV can be run through a special purpose company, a partnership or an LLP, and that choice drives the tax, as the later section explains.</p>

<h2>Mezzanine versus JV: cost, dilution and control</h2>
<p>The choice between the two comes down to three levers: cost, dilution and control. Mezzanine costs yield (a coupon and fees) but keeps all the profit and all the control. A JV costs ownership (a share of the profit) but carries no debt-service coupon and shares the downside. A worked example makes the trade concrete.</p>
<p>Take a residential scheme with a total project cost of <strong>£2.5m</strong> and an expected profit of <strong>£500k</strong> on completion. Senior debt covers 65% of cost (£1.625m). You have committed 15% of cost as equity (£375k). That leaves a <strong>£500k gap</strong>, 20% of cost, and two ways to fill it.</p>

<p>On a scheme that performs to plan, mezzanine is the cheaper way to fill the gap: you pay roughly £130k for the leverage and keep about £370k of the profit, against giving away £250k to a JV partner. That is the case for debt. The case for equity is what happens when the scheme does not perform. If costs overrun or sales soften and the profit is thinner, the mezzanine still has to be repaid ahead of your equity, so the pain lands on you, whereas the JV partner's return falls with the profit and the loss is shared. Mezzanine is cheaper and keeps control; a JV is dearer on a good outcome but spreads the risk on a bad one. <h2>The deed of priority: why the senior lender must consent</h2>
<p>You cannot bolt mezzanine onto a scheme without the senior lender's agreement, because the mezzanine ranks against the same security. When both are present, the senior lender takes a first charge and the mezzanine lender a second charge, and a <strong>deed of priority</strong> (or intercreditor agreement) records who is repaid first, how far the senior debt can grow, and what each lender can and cannot do if the borrower defaults. The senior lender priced its facility around being first in the queue, so it must consent to anything sitting behind it.</p>
<p>This is why mezzanine cannot be arranged in isolation from the senior facility, and why a combined stretch-senior facility from one lender can be simpler: there is no second lender to negotiate priority with. For a JV, the equivalent document is the JV or shareholders' agreement, which governs the relationship between the equity partners rather than the ranking of charges. Getting these documents right is a legal exercise, and the security and priority terms interact with the tax analysis of the payments that flow up the stack.</p>

<h2>How mezzanine and JV finance are taxed</h2>
<p>The tax treatment of the gap-funding layer is where the debt-versus-equity choice bites hardest, and it turns first on whether your activity is a development <strong>trade</strong> or an <strong>investment</strong>. That distinction decides the treatment of every finance cost, and property already sits on the wrong side of it for many people who assume they are investors. Our guides on <a href="/blog/property-types-and-specialist-tax/property-development-tax-trading-vs-investment-income">the trading-versus-investment line for developers</a> and <a href="/blog/property-types-and-specialist-tax/are-you-a-property-investor-or-developer">whether you are a property investor or a developer</a> set out the test in full; the summary below is deliberately short, because the detailed decision lives on those pages.</p>
<p><strong>Mezzanine interest.</strong> For a genuine development trade, mezzanine interest is a cost of the trade. In a company it falls under the loan relationship rules in CTA 2009 and is relieved as it accrues, subject to anti-avoidance and, for larger groups, the corporate interest restriction. Interest is frequently capitalised into work in progress and relieved when the units are sold rather than expensed year by year. The Section 24 residential finance-cost restriction that limits an individual landlord to a 20% basic-rate tax reducer does not touch a build-to-sell trade, because that restriction applies to a residential property rental business, not to a development trade.</p>
<p><strong>JV profit share.</strong> Equity is taxed by its structure, not its label. A profit share paid as a distribution from a JV company is a dividend in the recipient's hands and is <em>not</em> a deductible cost to the company, which is the opposite of interest. A profit share routed through a partnership or LLP is taxed as each member's share of the trading profit. A preferred return dressed up to look like a coupon may be recharacterised depending on the terms. Because the wrong structure can turn what you assumed was a deductible cost into a non-deductible distribution, and because a profit-linked mezzanine kicker can blur the debt-equity line, the structure should be modelled for tax before the agreement is signed. Our <a href="/blog/property-finance/financing-a-property-development-tax">guide to the tax treatment of financing a development</a> works through interest, capitalisation and the profit-share point in detail.</p>

<h2>Scope and the regulated boundary</h2>
<p>Mezzanine and JV finance for a genuine property development are business-purpose arrangements. They sit outside the regulated-mortgage and consumer-credit perimeters, so the activity is unregulated, and this page describes how the products work rather than offering or arranging any of them. That distinction matters, because the financial promotion of qualifying credit is restricted under FSMA 2000 section 21, and nothing here is an invitation or inducement to borrow.</p>
<p>Two situations fall outside this guide entirely. First, any borrowing secured on your own home is a regulated mortgage contract, whatever it funds, and you should speak to an FCA-authorised mortgage adviser rather than treat it as development finance. Second, this is not tax, legal or financial advice on your specific scheme; the numbers are illustrative ranges, and pricing, leverage and the tax analysis all depend on the facts. Verify current finance terms with a lender or broker, and take advice on the structure before you commit.</p>

Whether the gap is filled with mezzanine debt or JV equity changes not only the headline cost but whether the payment up the stack is a deductible finance cost or a non-deductible distribution, whether interest is capitalised into work in progress, and how the profit split is charged in each partner's hands. Those choices are far cheaper to get right before the agreements are signed than to unpick afterwards.</p>
