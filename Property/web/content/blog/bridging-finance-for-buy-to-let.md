<p>Bridging finance for buy to let exists to solve one specific problem: the property you want to let cannot be mortgaged yet. A standard term buy-to-let (BTL) mortgage lender values a property as it stands on the day of purchase. If it is uninhabitable, below the minimum energy standard, on a short lease or otherwise outside a term lender's box, that lender declines. A bridge steps in for a matter of months to fund the purchase and the works, after which the property becomes lettable and mortgageable and you refinance onto the term BTL mortgage you wanted all along. That refinance is what repays the bridge. This is the bridge-to-let route, and understanding it as a two-stage sequence, rather than as a loan in isolation, is the whole point of this guide.</p>

<p>This page is educational. It explains how bridge-to-let works, what it costs as a range, and how the interest is taxed. It is not an invitation to enter into a credit agreement, and it does not arrange, quote or compare finance. Where a reader's situation is a regulated consumer product rather than investment lending, we say so and route them away. If you are weighing a bridge-to-let deal, the finance mechanics and the tax treatment of the interest are two halves of the same sum, and the tax half is where we can help.</p>

<h2>What bridging for buy to let actually is</h2>
<p>A bridging loan is short-term, secured, interest-first borrowing. It is typically taken for a term of a few months up to around 12 to 18 months, secured by a first (or sometimes second) charge over property, and priced by the month rather than the year. In a buy-to-let context, the bridge is almost never the end state. It is the means to an end, and the end is a term BTL mortgage on a finished, lettable property.</p>
<p>Contrast that with a term buy-to-let mortgage, which is long-term (often 5 to 25 years), priced annually, and underwritten primarily on the rent the property produces. A term BTL lender needs a property that is habitable and lettable now and rent it can stress-test. A bridge does not need any of that. It underwrites the security and, above all, the <strong>exit</strong>: the credible route by which the bridge will be repaid. In bridge-to-let, the exit is the BTL remortgage. Everything about the deal is designed to make that remortgage possible.</p>
<p>For the wider picture of how bridging works across every use case, first versus second charge, open versus closed, and rolled versus serviced interest, see our <a href="/blog/property-finance/bridging-loans-guide">bridging loans guide</a>. This page stays narrowly on the buy-to-let use case.</p>

<h2>The test that defines this product: unmortgageable now, mortgageable after works</h2>
<p>The single question that decides whether bridge-to-let is the right tool is this: <strong>can a term BTL lender mortgage this property today, exactly as it is?</strong> If yes, you do not need a bridge, you need a purchase mortgage. If no, a bridge can buy you the time and the funds to change that answer to yes. So the entire strategy turns on why a term lender would decline the property in its current state.</p>
<p>The common reasons a property is unmortgageable to a term BTL lender are:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>No functioning kitchen or bathroom.</strong> Most mainstream BTL lenders require a property to be habitable, which means a working kitchen and at least one working bathroom. A stripped-out shell fails this on sight.</li>
<li><strong>Serious disrepair or structural issues.</strong> Damp, subsidence, an unsafe roof or defective wiring can each make a property unmortgageable until remedied.</li>
<li><strong>A short lease.</strong> A lease below roughly 70 years (lender-dependent) narrows the market sharply and can make a flat unmortgageable on term products.</li>
<li><strong>Below the minimum energy efficiency standard.</strong> A property that cannot legally be let because its EPC is below the required rating is, in practice, not lettable and so not attractive to a term lender.</li>
<li><strong>Non-standard construction or a difficult location</strong> (for example a flat above commercial premises), which some term lenders exclude but a specialist can accept once other issues are fixed.</li>
</ul>
<p>The defining logic of bridge-to-let is that these are <em>fixable</em> conditions. Fit a kitchen and bathroom, remedy the disrepair, extend the lease or improve the EPC, and the same property that a term lender declined on Monday meets its criteria a few months later. The bridge funds the interval in between. If the blocker cannot be fixed within a short bridge term (a very short lease that will take years to extend, for instance), bridge-to-let is the wrong tool.</p>

<h2>Bridge-to-let as a two-stage sequence</h2>
<p>It helps to see the deal as two loans that hand over to each other:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Stage one, the bridge.</strong> You buy the unmortgageable property with a bridge (plus your own deposit and, usually, your own cash for the works). Interest is often rolled up, meaning it is added to the balance rather than paid monthly, so the property does not need to produce income during the works. You complete the works that make the property lettable and mortgageable.</li>
<li><strong>Stage two, the term BTL exit.</strong> With the property finished, lettable and (ideally) let or ready to let, you apply for a term buy-to-let mortgage. The new lender values the finished property and stress-tests the rent. Its advance is used to redeem the bridge in full: the capital, the rolled interest and the exit fee. Any surplus, where the finished value supports a larger loan than the bridge, is returned to you as recycled capital for the next project.</li>
</ul>
<p>Some lenders sell this as a single packaged bridge-to-let product with a pre-agreed term exit. Others treat the two stages as separate applications, sometimes with two different lenders. The mechanics are the same in both cases. The important discipline is to line up the exit before you draw the bridge, not after, because a bridge with no viable exit is the classic way this strategy goes wrong. For the term BTL side of the sequence, our wave guide to <a href="/blog/property-finance/buy-to-let-mortgages-guide">buy-to-let mortgages</a> covers how term lenders underwrite the finished property, and the <a href="/blog/landlord-tax-essentials/deposit-buy-to-let-2026-mortgage-requirements">buy-to-let deposit and LTV requirements</a> page covers what the exit lender will expect you to put in.</p>

<h2>What it costs and how much you can borrow</h2>
<p>Bridging is priced by the month, and the true cost of a bridge-to-let deal is the sum of both stages. The figures below are indicative <strong>ranges as at July 2026</strong> and are not quotes. Bridging pricing moves with the Bank of England <a href="https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/monetary-policy/the-interest-rate-bank-rate">base rate</a> and is set deal by deal, so verify current pricing with a lender or broker before you rely on any number.</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Monthly bridge rate:</strong> roughly 0.55% to 1% per month for a first-charge investment bridge, with the rate driven by loan to value, the strength of the exit, the quality of the security and your experience.</li>
<li><strong>Arrangement fee:</strong> commonly around 1% to 2% of the loan, usually added to the balance.</li>
<li><strong>Valuation, legal and admin fees,</strong> and often an <strong>exit fee</strong> on redemption.</li>
<li><strong>Bridge loan to value:</strong> typically up to around 70% to 75% of the purchase price or day-one value (whichever the lender uses), lower for weaker security.</li>
<li><strong>Term BTL exit loan to value:</strong> commonly up to around 75% of the finished value, with better pricing at lower bands.</li>
</ul>
<p>Because bridge interest is often rolled up, the effective cost over a short term is higher than the headline monthly rate makes it look, and every rolled month erodes the equity you will release on refinance. For the mechanics of gross versus net loan and how the fee stack builds, see our <a href="/blog/property-finance/bridging-loan-rates">bridging loan rates</a> page. To sketch the cost of a specific bridge, the <a href="/calculators/bridging-loan-calculator">bridging loan calculator</a> is a starting point, and the <a href="/calculators/buy-to-let-rental-stress-test-calculator">buy-to-let rental stress test calculator</a> lets you check whether the finished rent will actually support the exit mortgage.</p>

<h2>A worked bridge-to-let example</h2>
<p>Consider an investor buying a tired top-floor flat that has been stripped back: no kitchen, no working bathroom, and on paper a gross yield under 6% at the price it would fetch if finished. No mainstream term BTL lender will mortgage it in that state. The numbers, illustrative only and rounded, run like this:</p>
<table>
<tr><th>Stage</th><th>Figure</th></tr>
<tr><td>Purchase price (uninhabitable flat)</td><td>£180,000</td></tr>
<tr><td>Bridge advance (approx. 70% of price)</td><td>£126,000</td></tr>
<tr><td>Investor deposit into the bridge</td><td>£54,000</td></tr>
<tr><td>Refurbishment cost (kitchen, bathroom, redecoration), funded from own cash</td><td>£18,000</td></tr>
<tr><td>Bridge term</td><td>~6 months</td></tr>
<tr><td>Finished value once lettable</td><td>£215,000</td></tr>
<tr><td>Achievable monthly rent</td><td>~£1,100</td></tr>
<tr><td>Term BTL exit at 75% of finished value</td><td>£161,250</td></tr>
</table>
<p>At the exit, the £161,250 term BTL mortgage redeems the bridge. The bridge to clear is the original £126,000 plus roughly six months of rolled interest and fees (say £8,000 to £10,000 depending on the rate and fee stack), so around £134,000 to £136,000. The exit advance covers that in full and returns roughly £25,000 of capital to the investor, offsetting most of the works spend. The property is now a standard let on a term mortgage, and the bridge has done its single job and disappeared. The deal only works because the finished value (£215,000) genuinely exceeds the price paid plus the works, and because the rent supports the exit lender's coverage test. Change either of those and the picture changes entirely.</p>

<h2>The exit is everything: day-one value versus the six-month rule</h2>
<p>In bridge-to-let, the exit is not an afterthought, it is the deal. The bridge only ever repays from the term BTL mortgage (or, failing that, a sale), so the exit must be viable before you commit. Two lender conventions decide whether your planned exit will actually complete.</p>
<p>The first is the <strong>interest coverage ratio (ICR) stress test</strong>. A term BTL lender does not simply lend a percentage of value. It requires the rent to cover the mortgage interest by a set margin at a stressed interest rate. If the finished rent is too thin relative to the loan you want, the exit shrinks or fails regardless of the property's value. That is why the achievable rent, not just the finished valuation, governs how much of the bridge you can refinance away. Modelling the ICR on the finished rent is the single most useful thing you can do before drawing the bridge.</p>
<p>The second is the <strong>six-month rule</strong>. Many BTL lenders will only remortgage against the price you paid, not an uplifted value, if you have owned the property for less than six months. For bridge-to-let this is a trap, because your whole plan often relies on refinancing against the higher finished value. Two points soften it. Some lenders will lend on the current day-one value where you have genuinely added value through works, and there are specialist refurbishment-to-let products built for exactly this. But you must confirm your intended exit lender's day-one-value stance before you buy, not discover it afterwards. The tax and timing angle of remortgaging a BTL property is covered in our guide to <a href="/blog/section-24-and-tax-relief/buy-to-let-refinancing-when-does-it-make-sense">buy-to-let refinancing and when it makes sense</a>, and the <a href="/blog/section-24-and-tax-relief/section-24-remortgaging-btl-property-tax-implications">tax implications of remortgaging a BTL property</a> page covers what happens when you pull capital out.</p>

<h2>The consumer buy-to-let fence: when this guide does not apply</h2>
<p>This guide is about <strong>investment</strong> buy-to-let, which is business-purpose lending and sits outside the regulated mortgage perimeter. Not every property someone calls a buy-to-let is that. Three situations tip a deal into regulated or consumer territory, and if any describes you, this page does not cover your situation.</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>A bridge secured on your own home.</strong> If the security is your main residence, the loan is a regulated mortgage contract, whatever you intend to do with the money. That is outside scope here.</li>
<li><strong>An accidental buy-to-let.</strong> A property you previously lived in and are now letting, rather than one bought as an investment, can be a regulated or consumer buy-to-let.</li>
<li><strong>A family buy-to-let.</strong> Letting to a close relative can bring the loan within the consumer buy-to-let regime.</li>
</ul>
<p>The boundary between regulated and unregulated lending is set by legislation and FCA guidance, not by what a product is marketed as. The definition of a regulated mortgage contract and the buy-to-let treatment are in the FCA's <a href="https://www.handbook.fca.org.uk/handbook/PERG/4/">Perimeter Guidance on regulated mortgage activities (PERG 4)</a>, and the restriction on promoting credit is in <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2000/8/section/21">section 21 of the Financial Services and Markets Act 2000</a>. If your situation is a regulated or consumer buy-to-let, or the security is your home, speak to an FCA-authorised mortgage adviser. We do not arrange finance for either regulated or unregulated deals, and nothing here is a financial promotion of credit.</p>

<h2>How the interest is taxed: the part most bridge-to-let plans get wrong</h2>
<p>The bridging interest and fees are a real cost, and how much of that cost you get back through tax relief depends entirely on how you hold the property. This is where a bridge-to-let deal that looks marginal on the finance can either work or fail once tax is in the sum.</p>
<p><strong>Individual landlord, residential buy-to-let.</strong> Finance costs, including bridging interest, are caught by the Section 24 finance-cost restriction. You do not deduct the interest from your rental profit. Instead you get a basic-rate tax reducer at 20% of the finance costs. For a higher-rate taxpayer, that is materially less relief than a full deduction, and it is the single biggest reason individuals reconsider how they hold BTL property. The mechanics are set out in our guides to <a href="/blog/section-24-and-tax-relief/finance-costs-section-24-complete-guide">finance costs and Section 24</a> and <a href="/blog/section-24-and-tax-relief/section-24-tax-relief-complete-guide">Section 24 tax relief</a>, and the HMRC position is in the <a href="https://www.gov.uk/hmrc-internal-manuals/property-income-manual/pim2054">Property Income Manual (PIM2054)</a>.</p>
<p><strong>Company or SPV.</strong> A limited company or special purpose vehicle deducts the interest in full against its rental profit under the loan relationship rules. Section 24 does not apply to companies. This is one reason so much bridge-to-let is now done through an SPV, though incorporation carries its own costs and consequences and is a decision to model, not a default.</p>
<p><strong>Arrangement and broker fees.</strong> The incidental costs of obtaining loan finance can generally be relieved, but through the same route as the interest: within Section 24 for an individual, and within the loan relationship rules for a company. The capital-versus-revenue line matters here too. Our page on <a href="/blog/section-24-and-tax-relief/mortgage-arrangement-fees-deductible-landlord">whether mortgage arrangement fees are deductible</a> covers the detail.</p>
<p>Because the answer swings so hard on structure and property type, we have a dedicated guide to <a href="/blog/property-finance/is-bridging-loan-interest-tax-deductible">whether bridging loan interest is tax deductible</a>, which splits the treatment by individual versus company and residential versus commercial. If you already have a term BTL mortgage in place, the interest-relief position is the same, and our <a href="/blog/landlord-tax-essentials/btl-mortgage">buy-to-let mortgage tax</a> guide covers it from the term-loan side.</p>

<h2>When bridge-to-let is the wrong tool</h2>
<p>Bridge-to-let is a strategy for below-standard stock bought below its finished value, where the value you add plus the discount you captured comfortably exceeds the bridging cost. It is not a general-purpose way to buy an ordinary lettable house, because on a property a term lender would happily mortgage today, the bridge is pure extra cost. It also fails when the exit is fragile: if the deal only stacks up on an optimistic finished valuation, or if the rent barely clears the exit lender's coverage test, then a low valuation or a rate move at refinance can leave you holding a short-term loan you cannot repay except by selling under pressure. The refurbishment version of this strategy, including the buy-refurbish-refinance approach, is covered in our <a href="/blog/property-finance/bridging-finance-for-refurbishment">bridging finance for refurbishment</a> guide, which is the natural sibling to this page. The discipline that separates a good bridge-to-let deal from a bad one is simple to state and easy to skip: line up and stress-test the exit before you draw the bridge.</p>
