<p>Extending the lease on a flat with about 85 years unexpired, worth around £350,000 with a £100-a-year ground rent, typically costs £6,000 to £8,000 in statutory premium, plus roughly £2,600 to £4,400 in professional fees. That is the honest shape of the answer in 2026: a premium driven almost entirely by the unexpired term, the flat's value and the ground rent, and a fee stack on top that most leaseholders underestimate because it includes the freeholder's costs as well as their own.</p>

<p>Two things make the number hard to pin down from a web calculator. First, the 80-year boundary: once the unexpired term falls to 80 years or below, an extra valuation component called marriage value applies, and the premium can roughly double. Second, and this catches out almost everyone researching in 2026, many online calculators and guides now assume marriage value has been abolished. It has not. The Leasehold and Freehold Reform Act 2024 abolishes it, but that provision is not in force, and a claim made today still pays it in full.</p>

<p>This page decomposes the premium into its components, works the 80-year cliff with numbers, prices every line of the fee stack including the freeholder's costs you are legally obliged to cover, and then adds the layer cost guides never reach: what the spend does to your tax position. If you are weighing an extension against buying the freehold outright, that decision has its own page, our <a href="/blog/property-types-and-specialist-tax/lease-extension-vs-freehold-purchase">lease extension vs freehold purchase guide</a>; this one is about what the extension route costs.</p>

<h2>What a lease extension costs in 2026: the all-in answer</h2>

<p>A statutory lease extension on a flat under Chapter II of the Leasehold Reform, Housing and Urban Development Act 1993 buys you a specific product: your existing unexpired term plus 90 years, at a peppercorn (nil) ground rent for the entire new term. On a lease with 84 years left, you come out with 174 years and no ground rent. The government's own <a href="https://www.gov.uk/leasehold-property/extending-changing-or-ending-a-lease">leasehold guidance</a> confirms the 90-year figure for flats.</p>

<p>The all-in cost has two layers:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>The statutory premium</strong>, calculated under Schedule 13 to the 1993 Act. This is the price of the new lease, and it ranges from a few thousand pounds on a long lease with a small ground rent to tens of thousands on a short lease in an expensive area.</li>
<li><strong>The professional fee stack</strong>, typically £2,600 to £4,400. This covers your own solicitor and valuer, the freeholder's solicitor and valuer (which you pay under section 60 of the 1993 Act), and Land Registry fees.</li>
</ul>

<p>One eligibility point that changed recently and in your favour: you no longer need to have owned the flat for two years before claiming. The two-year rule was abolished with effect from 31 January 2025 by <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2025/57/made">SI 2025/57</a>, commencing section 27 of the Leasehold and Freehold Reform Act 2024. A buyer can serve the extension notice on completion day. That abolition is genuinely in force; much of the rest of the 2024 Act, as you will see, is not.</p>

<h2>The statutory premium decomposed: term, reversion and marriage value</h2>

<p>The premium is not a negotiated market price. It is a statutory valuation under <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1993/28/schedule/13">Schedule 13 to the 1993 Act</a>, built from up to three components. Understanding them tells you what actually drives your number, and why two flats on the same street can face premiums thousands of pounds apart.</p>

<p><strong>1. The term.</strong> The freeholder currently has the right to receive your ground rent for the rest of the unexpired term. The new lease reduces that rent to a peppercorn, so you compensate the freeholder for the income stream they lose. A fixed £100-a-year ground rent capitalises to a modest sum; an escalating rent that doubles every ten years capitalises to a serious one. This is why ground rent review clauses matter so much to extension pricing.</p>

<p><strong>2. The reversion.</strong> At the end of your existing lease, the flat would revert to the freeholder. By adding 90 years you push that day nine decades further away, so you compensate the freeholder for the delay. The value of the flat is discounted back over the unexpired term at the deferment rate, which since the Sportelli decision has stood at 5% for flats. Because of compound discounting, the reversion is tiny on a 120-year lease and grows steadily as the term shortens: £300,000 deferred 84 years at 5% is worth around £5,000 today, but deferred 60 years it is worth around £16,000.</p>

<p><strong>3. Marriage value, only below 80 years.</strong> When a short lease and the freeholder's interest are "married" by the grant of a new long lease, the combined value is greater than the sum of the parts, because short leases are worth disproportionately less than long ones. Schedule 13 gives the freeholder 50% of that uplift, but paragraph 4(2A) sets marriage value at nil where the unexpired term exceeds 80 years at the valuation date. Above 80 years, this component simply does not exist. At 80 years or below, it exists in full, and it is usually the largest single component.</p>

<p>The deferment rate itself is one of the things the 2024 Act changes: it hands the Secretary of State power to prescribe the rates, and the consultation on those rates closes on 23 September 2026. Until secondary legislation lands, the post-Sportelli 5% remains the working rate, and nobody can tell you whether prescribed rates will push premiums down or up.</p>

<h2>The 80-year cliff: what crossing it actually costs</h2>

<p>The cliff is best seen with numbers. Take Priya, who owns a one-bedroom flat worth about £300,000 with the benefit of a long lease, paying £150 a year in fixed ground rent. The figures below are illustrative ranges built from the Schedule 13 components at the 5% deferment rate, in line with published market examples; her actual premium would come from a specialist valuation.</p>

<p><strong>With 84 years unexpired</strong>, the premium has two components:</p>

<ul>
<li>Term (capitalised ground rent): around £2,000</li>
<li>Reversion (£300,000 deferred 84 years at 5%): around £4,500 to £5,500</li>
<li>Marriage value: nil, because the term exceeds 80 years</li>
<li><strong>Illustrative premium: roughly £6,500 to £7,500</strong></li>
</ul>

<p><strong>The same flat at 79 years unexpired</strong> looks very different:</p>

<ul>
<li>Term: still around £2,000</li>
<li>Reversion: around £6,000 to £7,000, slightly higher because the reversion is nearer</li>
<li>Marriage value: the uplift created by the new lease might run to £10,000 to £14,000, of which the freeholder takes 50%, so £5,000 to £7,000</li>
<li><strong>Illustrative premium: roughly £13,000 to £16,000</strong></li>
</ul>

<p>Five years of lease, roughly double the premium. The gradient continues below 79: relativity (the ratio of short-lease value to long-lease value) falls faster as the term shortens, so marriage value grows faster than the term shrinks.</p>

<p>One timing point worth more than any negotiation tactic: the valuation date is the date your section 42 notice is given, not the date the extension completes. If your lease has 80 years and 3 months left, serving the notice now fixes the no-marriage-value basis even though the claim will take months to complete. Drifting past the boundary while you gather quotes is the most expensive form of procrastination in leasehold, and it is entirely avoidable.</p>

<h2>"But I read marriage value was abolished": what is actually in force</h2>

<p>You may well have read that the Leasehold and Freehold Reform Act 2024 abolished marriage value, and that reading is understandable, because the abolition is genuinely on the statute book. What the coverage tends to omit is commencement. The 2024 Act's valuation reforms only take effect when the government makes commencement regulations, and it has not done so. Only three commencement instruments exist under the Act, and none of them touches the valuation provisions. On legislation.gov.uk, the omission of Schedule 13 by section 36(5) of the 2024 Act sits under "changes yet to be applied": a prospective repeal, not a live one. Schedule 13, marriage value included, remains the operative valuation law for every claim made today.</p>

<p>The same applies to the 990-year term. The 2024 Act legislates a fresh 990-year lease in place of the plus-90 extension, but that provision is not in force either; an extension completing in 2026 is your term plus 90 years. And the £250 ground rent cap you may have seen reported belongs to a draft Bill announced in the May 2026 King's Speech, not to any Act at all.</p>

<p>The safe way to hold all of this in your head: the 2024 Act abolishes marriage value, but that provision is not yet in force, and a claim made today still pays it. For the full provision-by-provision ledger of what is and is not commenced, see our companion page on <a href="/blog/property-types-and-specialist-tax/leasehold-reform-act-2024-what-is-in-force">what the Leasehold and Freehold Reform Act 2024 actually has in force</a>.</p>

<h2>The professional fee stack, including the freeholder's costs you pay</h2>

<p>The premium is only the headline. A statutory claim involves four professionals, and you pay for all of them. Under <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1993/28/section/60">section 60 of the 1993 Act</a>, the leaseholder is liable for the freeholder's reasonable costs of investigating the right to a new lease, valuing the flat for the premium, and granting the new lease. The 2024 Act repeals section 60, but that repeal too is prospective, listed as a change yet to be applied; on any claim made today, the liability stands.</p>

<h2>The tax treatment: not deductible, but never wasted</h2>

<p>Here is the layer the consumer cost guides do not reach. Everything above, premium and fee stack alike, is capital expenditure. That has three consequences, one disappointing, two useful.</p>

<p><strong>No deduction against rental income.</strong> If you let the flat, you cannot set the premium or the professional fees against your rents in the year you pay them. The dividing line is the one HMRC draws in its property income guidance: recurring ground rent you pay out is a revenue expense and deductible (see <a href="https://www.gov.uk/hmrc-internal-manuals/property-income-manual/pim2205">PIM2205</a>, and our page on <a href="/blog/property-types-and-specialist-tax/ground-rent-rules-uk">ground rent rules</a> for the ground-rent side), but the one-off cost of acquiring a longer lease is capital. No amount of creative categorisation changes that.</p>

<p><strong>It enters your CGT base cost.</strong> The premium and the associated fees are expenditure on your lease, and they count in the capital gains computation when you eventually sell, alongside what you originally paid for the flat. HMRC's Capital Gains Manual at <a href="https://www.gov.uk/hmrc-internal-manuals/capital-gains-manual/cg71141">CG71141</a> covers allowable expenditure on lease disposals, including premiums paid and enhancement expenditure. For leases with more than 50 years to run at disposal, which after an extension yours will comfortably be, the expenditure counts in full; the wasting-asset restrictions only bite on sub-50-year leases. Note the extension itself costs you nothing in CGT: you are paying money out, not receiving it, so there is no disposal by you at that point.</p>

<p><strong>Company-held flats get the corporation tax version.</strong> Take Marcus, whose SPV holds a £450,000 flat with 72 years unexpired. His illustrative premium, with marriage value now firmly in play, lands in the low-to-mid £40,000s, plus around £3,500 of fees. The company gets no corporation tax deduction against its rental profits for any of it, but the full spend goes into the company's base cost for the flat, reducing the chargeable gain on a future sale. And because the premium exceeds £40,000, SDLT enters the picture, including the 5% surcharge that applies to company purchasers; our <a href="/blog/property-types-and-specialist-tax/lease-extensions-in-the-uk-surrender-and-regrant">surrender and regrant guide</a> works that mechanic in full.</p>

<p><strong>Keep the paperwork for decades.</strong> The completion statement, the section 42 notice, the valuation report and every fee invoice are the evidence for a base-cost claim you might not make until the 2040s or 2050s. File them with the lease and treat them as permanent records. A £15,000 premium you cannot evidence at disposal is, at residential CGT rates, potentially £3,600 of tax paid for want of a PDF.</p>

<h2>Houses, waiting for reform, and the SDLT question</h2>

<p><strong>Houses run on a different statute.</strong> A leasehold house falls under the Leasehold Reform Act 1967, not the 1993 Act. The 1967 Act extension adds 50 years at a modern ground rent, a materially worse product than the flat regime's 90 years at a peppercorn, which is why most qualifying house leaseholders skip the extension entirely and buy the freehold under the 1967 Act, with the price assessed under its section 9 valuation. If you own a leasehold house, price enfranchisement first.</p>

<p><strong>Should you wait for the reforms?</strong> The tempting logic runs: the 2024 Act abolishes marriage value, so a sub-80-year leaseholder who waits saves thousands. The problem is that the saving is unknowable and the cost of waiting is not. Commencement of the valuation provisions needs secondary legislation that does not exist yet; the rates consultation closes on 23 September 2026, and 2027-28 is the earliest realistic window, with credible commentary putting it later. The prescribed deferment and capitalisation rates could move premiums in either direction, including upward for some leaseholders. Meanwhile your lease shortens every month, the reversion component grows, and a lease near the 80-year line can cross into marriage-value territory while you wait for a reform that may not arrive in time. A leaseholder at 95 years can afford to watch and wait. A leaseholder at 82 years is gambling a certain, avoidable cost against an uncertain, unquantifiable saving.</p>

<p><strong>SDLT in one paragraph.</strong> A statutory lease extension is treated for SDLT as a surrender of your old lease and the grant of a new one, and where the premium reaches £40,000 or more a land transaction return and SDLT can follow, including the 5% additional dwellings surcharge for owners of other residential property and for companies. Most owner-occupier extensions with premiums in the single thousands are untouched. We keep the mechanics out of this page deliberately: the full treatment is in our <a href="/blog/property-types-and-specialist-tax/lease-extensions-in-the-uk-surrender-and-regrant">surrender and regrant guide</a>, the rates comparison is in our <a href="/blog/landlord-tax-essentials/sdlt-leasehold-extension-vs-fresh-purchase">SDLT on lease extensions vs fresh purchases page</a>, and you can sanity-check a figure with our <a href="/calculators/stamp-duty-calculator">stamp duty calculator</a>.</p>

<h2>Getting to a real number</h2>

<p>The ranges on this page will put you within sight of your cost, but only two documents turn sight into certainty: a specialist valuation of your own flat, which fixes the realistic premium range and your negotiating position, and itemised fee quotes for the stack. If your lease is anywhere between 80 and 85 years, treat the valuation as urgent rather than routine, because the section 42 notice date is what freezes the marriage-value position, not the completion date. And before you pay, have a property tax specialist or your accountant confirm how the spend sits in your records: capital not revenue, into the base cost, completion statement filed where you will find it in twenty years. The premium buys you 90 years; ten minutes of record-keeping makes sure the tax system gives some of it back when you sell.</p>
