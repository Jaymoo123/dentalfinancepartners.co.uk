The company declares a dividend equal to the property's market value and settles it by transferring the property. For company law it is an ordinary distribution and must come out of accumulated realised profits under section 830 of the Companies Act 2006. For tax it is treated as a dividend received by the shareholder, valued at the market value of what they received, not at the property's book cost."

    answer: "Yes, and it is often cleaner than a distribution. The director pays the company market value in cash, the company banks the proceeds, and no dividend arises so there is no dividend tax.   - question: "Can I sell my company property to myself cheaply?"
    answer: "No. Selling at an undervalue does not work. The discount you gave yourself is treated as a distribution to you and taxed as a dividend on that shortfall. Directors also owe duties to the company and its creditors, and a sale at an undervalue can be unwound if the company later fails."

    answer: "Not in the same way as on the way in. Section 53 of the Finance Act 2003, which substitutes market value as the consideration for stamp duty, applies where the purchaser is a company.     answer: "Yes, for a distribution in specie. Section 830 of the Companies Act 2006 allows a distribution only out of accumulated realised profits less accumulated realised losses. Many property companies hold a valuable asset but modest reserves because the value sits in an unrealised uplift. If that is your position, a sale to the director is usually the workable route instead."

    answer: "The lender controls the timing. Personal buy-to-let borrowing brings back the section 24 finance cost restriction, which is often the reason the property went into a company in the first place. <p>Almost everything written about property and limited companies assumes you are moving in one direction, from your own name into a company. The reverse journey is a real and much less documented problem: the company already owns the flat or the house, and you want it back in your personal name. Directors reach this point for ordinary reasons, and rarely for a clever tax reason.</p>
<p>The short answer is that it can be done, in two ways, and that neither is a free reversal. A transfer out is a taxable event on both sides of the transaction at once. The company is treated as having disposed of the property at full market value and pays corporation tax on the gain before the property goes anywhere, and you are taxed personally on the value you receive. On a mortgage-free property worth £280,000 the combined bill in the worked example below is £123,305, roughly 44 per cent of the property's value, without a penny of cash changing hands.</p>
<p>One boundary first, because it decides whether you are on the right page. This is about extracting a single property while the company stays open and carries on. If your actual plan is to shut the company down, that is a different decision with different tax treatment, and it belongs in our guide to <a href="/blog/incorporation-and-company-structures/how-to-close-a-property-limited-company">closing a property limited company</a>.</p>

<h2>Why a director wants the property back in their own name</h2>
<p>The reasons cluster into four groups, and none of them is a tax trick.</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Retirement or winding down.</strong> A landlord who is stopping wants to move a portfolio out one asset at a time rather than in a single event, often to control which tax year each transfer lands in.</li>
<li><strong>Occupation.</strong> Somebody wants to live in the property, or house a family member in it. A company cannot sensibly own a home occupied by a director or a connected person without running into the Annual Tax on Enveloped Dwellings, the benefit-in-kind charge on living accommodation, and mortgage terms that prohibit it.</li>
<li><strong>Succession and divorce.</strong> Splitting a portfolio between children, or between separating spouses, is often simpler when specific properties sit in specific personal names rather than behind shares in a jointly held company.</li>
<li><strong>Lender and insurance friction.</strong> Company buy-to-let borrowing is a narrower market with a smaller lender panel and personal guarantees. Some landlords with a single property conclude the company wrapper costs more in accountancy, filing and finance than the tax it saves.</li>
</ul>
<p>What almost never appears on that list is "to save tax", and that is the honest position. If the company's base cost is well below today's value, the corporation tax on the deemed disposal alone can make the transfer out the most expensive thing you do this year.</p>

<h2>Distribution in specie: the mechanism</h2>
<p>A distribution in specie is a dividend that the company satisfies with an asset instead of cash. The company declares a dividend equal to the property's market value, and settles that dividend by transferring the property itself. It is not a special company law procedure, it is an ordinary distribution that happens to be paid in bricks.</p>
<p>Because it is an ordinary distribution, it is subject to the ordinary distribution rules. <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2006/46/section/830" rel="nofollow">Section 830 of the Companies Act 2006</a> allows a company to make a distribution "only out of profits available for the purpose", defined as accumulated realised profits less accumulated realised losses. That is the first practical gate, and it stops more transfers than the tax does.</p>
<p>The problem is that a property company's value usually sits in an <em>unrealised</em> uplift. A company that bought a flat for £190,000 which is now worth £280,000 has not realised that £90,000. Unless it has built up retained rental profits, its distributable reserves may be a fraction of the market value of the asset it is trying to hand over. A distribution that exceeds available profits is unlawful, and the shareholder who knew or ought to have known can be required to repay it. If the reserves are not there, the distribution-in-specie route is closed and the sale route below is the alternative.</p>
<p>The mechanics, assuming reserves are adequate, are short: a RICS valuation, a board minute recording the directors' decision, a shareholder resolution declaring the dividend in specie of that named property, and a conveyancer to transfer the legal title and register it, following the same conveyancing process set out in our guide to <a href="/blog/incorporation-and-company-structures/transfer-property-to-limited-company-conveyancing">transferring property to a limited company</a>. If a lender holds a charge, that conversation happens before anything else.</p>

<h2>What tax do I pay taking a property out of a company?</h2>
<p>Three layers can apply, and the order matters because the first one happens inside the company before you receive anything.</p>
<h3>Layer one: corporation tax in the company</h3>
<p>The company is disposing of the property to a connected person, so for corporation tax purposes it is treated as disposing at open market value regardless of what it actually receives. The chargeable gain is market value less the company's base cost and allowable costs, and it goes into the company's total taxable profits for the period.</p>
<p>Corporation tax is charged at 19 per cent where profits are below £50,000 and 25 per cent above £250,000, with marginal relief tapering between the two. A single property gain can easily push an otherwise small company from the 19 per cent band deep into marginal relief territory, which means the effective rate on the gain itself is higher than the headline small profits rate. Companies do keep indexation allowance on base costs incurred up to December 2017, where the relief is frozen, which individuals lost long ago.</p>
<h3>Layer two: income tax on you</h3>
<p>A distribution in specie is a dividend in your hands, valued at the market value of the property you received. It is taxed at the 2026/27 dividend rates of 10.75 per cent basic, 35.75 per cent higher and 39.35 per cent additional, after the £500 dividend allowance. The basic and higher rates each rose by two percentage points from 6 April 2026.</p>
<p>This is the single biggest practical cost, and the reason is bracket compression. A £280,000 dividend is not spread over the years it took the property to appreciate. It all lands in one tax year, so most of it is taxed at the additional rate, and because adjusted net income sails past £125,140 the personal allowance is withdrawn entirely, adding tax on income that would otherwise have been covered. Model the extraction cost with our <a href="/calculators/property-company-extraction-calculator">property company extraction calculator</a> before choosing a route.</p>
<h3>Layer three: stamp duty land tax on the way out</h3>
<p>Stamp duty is charged on chargeable consideration, and on a genuine dividend in specie of an unencumbered property there is none. Nothing is given in return, so there is normally no charge and no return to file.</p>
<p>Two points guard that conclusion. First, if you take on the company's outstanding mortgage, <a href="https://www.gov.uk/hmrc-internal-manuals/stamp-duty-land-tax-manual/sdltm04040" rel="nofollow">paragraph 8 of Schedule 4 to the Finance Act 2003</a> treats the assumption of liability for existing debt as chargeable consideration, and HMRC's manual is explicit that this covers being released from a covenant or agreeing to indemnify. A £150,000 mortgage taken on personally is a £150,000 acquisition for stamp duty, with the higher rates for additional dwellings usually in point. Second, the market-value substitution rule that catches transfers <em>into</em> a company does not mirror on the way out: <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2003/14/section/53" rel="nofollow">section 53 of the Finance Act 2003</a> applies only "where the purchaser is a company", and on a transfer out the purchaser is an individual. That asymmetry is genuinely favourable, and it is the one place where the reverse journey is cheaper than the forward one.</p>

<h2>Can a company sell property to a director instead?</h2>
<p>Yes, and where distributable reserves are short it is usually the only route. The director buys the property from the company at market value and pays for it in cash. The company banks the proceeds, no dividend is declared, and no dividend tax arises on the transfer.</p>
<p>The corporation tax position is identical. The company still has a disposal at market value and still pays tax on the gain, because the price is genuine market value in the first place. What changes is everything else:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>You need the cash.</strong> Either savings or personal buy-to-let borrowing, which puts you back inside the section 24 finance cost restriction on personal landlord interest.</li>
<li><strong>Stamp duty applies to the whole price.</strong> There is real consideration now, so the full residential rates apply, plus the 5 per cent additional dwellings surcharge if this is not replacing your only home.</li>
<li><strong>The cash is still stuck in the company.</strong> You have converted a property inside the company into money inside the company. Getting that money to you later is another dividend, or a liquidation, and the tax is deferred rather than avoided.</li>
<li><strong>Company law formalities.</strong> A transaction with a director above the statutory threshold is a substantial property transaction requiring shareholder approval, and the price must be defensible.</li>
</ul>
<p>Selling to yourself at a discount does not solve anything. The company is still taxed on market value because you are connected, and the discount is treated as a distribution to you and taxed as one.</p>

<h2>Worked example: extracting a £280,000 mortgage-free flat</h2>
<p>A company bought a rental flat in November 2018 for £190,000. It is now worth £280,000 on a RICS valuation and is owned outright, no mortgage. The company makes £12,000 a year of rental profit and has enough retained reserves to cover the distribution. The sole director and shareholder takes a £12,570 salary and no other dividends.</p>
<p><strong>Step one, inside the company.</strong> The disposal is deemed to be at market value.</p>

<p>Without the transfer, £12,000 of profit would have been taxed at 19 per cent, or £2,280. So the corporation tax caused by the transfer is £21,000, an effective 23.3 per cent on the gain. Note that the company pays this in cash, out of rental income and reserves, even though it received nothing for the property.</p>
<p><strong>Step two, on the director.</strong> The £280,000 dividend in specie lands in a single tax year. Adjusted net income is £292,570, above £125,140, so the personal allowance is fully withdrawn and the salary becomes taxable.</p>

<p><strong>Step three, stamp duty.</strong> There is no mortgage, no cash and no assumed debt, so there is no chargeable consideration and no stamp duty. The purchaser is an individual, so section 53 does not substitute market value.</p>
<p><strong>The total.</strong> £21,000 of corporation tax plus £102,305 of personal tax is <strong>£123,305</strong>, or 44 per cent of the property's value, payable in cash within roughly a year of a transaction that generated no cash at all. That last point is what catches directors out. The tax is real money on a date certain, and the only asset that produced it is now a house you cannot spend.</p>
<p>For contrast, had the director bought the flat for £280,000 in cash instead, the company would still pay the same £21,000 of corporation tax, there would be no dividend tax, but stamp duty at the additional-dwellings rates on £280,000 would be £18,000, and £280,000 of the director's own money would be locked into a company that then has to distribute it at some point anyway.</p>

<h2>When taking it out beats an open-market sale, and when it does not</h2>
<p>The genuine advantages are narrow but real. You avoid estate agency fees, marketing, viewings and chain risk. You keep the property. You control the completion date precisely, which is what makes tax-year timing possible. And if the intention is for a family member to live there, no third-party sale achieves that.</p>
<p>What it does not do is save tax. Compare it honestly with just selling the flat on the open market for £280,000. The company pays the same corporation tax on the same gain either way. On a sale, the company ends up with roughly £256,000 of cash after tax and fees, which it can distribute over several tax years, keeping much more of it in the basic and higher rate dividend bands instead of forcing the whole amount into the additional rate in one go. The distribution-in-specie route pays for keeping the asset by concentrating the income tax.</p>
<p>The route is therefore right when you want the property more than you want the tax efficiency, and wrong when you were told it was a way to unwind an incorporation cheaply. There is no reverse of section 162 incorporation relief. If moving in was a mistake, moving out does not undo it, it charges you for the round trip. Our guide on <a href="/blog/incorporation-and-company-structures/how-to-transfer-property-into-limited-company-uk">transferring property into a limited company</a> covers the forward journey, which is worth reading if you are still deciding on direction rather than reversing a decision already made.</p>

<h2>If your real plan is to close the company</h2>
<p>Where a director wants all the properties out and the company gone, a distribution in specie by a live company is often the wrong tool. A formal members' voluntary liquidation distributes the company's assets through a licensed insolvency practitioner, and the distribution can be treated as capital rather than income, which means capital gains tax rates instead of dividend rates. For a large single distribution that difference is substantial. Above £25,000 of total distributions the informal strike-off route cannot deliver capital treatment at all, and the whole amount becomes an income distribution.</p>
<p>That analysis, including how the capital-versus-income comparison actually works out and where Business Asset Disposal Relief does and does not help, sits in our guide to a <a href="/blog/incorporation-and-company-structures/mvl-members-voluntary-liquidation-property-company-cgt-vs-income-treatment">members' voluntary liquidation for a property company</a>. If the company is staying open, that page is not your route. If it is closing, it is the first thing to read. Either way, the wider structuring context is set out in our <a href="/spv-company">property SPV and company structures hub</a>.</p>

<h2>The order to do things in</h2>
<ol>
<li>Get a written RICS valuation. Everything downstream depends on it and a portal estimate will not survive scrutiny.</li>
<li>Check the company's distributable reserves against that valuation. If they fall short, the distribution route is out and you are looking at a sale.</li>
<li>Speak to the lender if there is a charge. Consent, redemption or personal refinance has to be settled before anything is minuted.</li>
<li>Model the corporation tax and the personal tax together, in the specific tax year, including the personal allowance taper and the marginal relief band.</li>
<li>Consider splitting across tax years if more than one property is involved.</li>
<li>Only then minute the board decision, pass the shareholder resolution and instruct the conveyancer.</li>
</ol>
<p>The most common failure is doing step six first and discovering steps two and four afterwards, by which time the transfer has happened, the tax is fixed, and the money to pay it is inside a building.</p>
