Spousal pre-gifting (transferring half to a spouse first, then both giving to the child) effectively gives two £3,000 exemptions on the disposal, saving £540 to £720 of tax.     answer: "A gift of property to an individual (other than a spouse) is a Potentially Exempt Transfer for inheritance tax. Death within seven years brings the gift back into the estate at its value at the date of gift, with taper relief reducing the IHT charge from year 3 onwards, but only on any part of the gift above the nil-rate band. The combination of full CGT now plus PET-then-out-of-estate is a common tax cost of lifetime gifting. If you keep living in the property or keep a share of the rent, the gift is a gift with reservation and the seven-year clock does not start while the benefit continues."

Stamp duty land tax for the buyer is charged on what they actually pay plus any mortgage they take over, not on market value (the SDLT market value rule bites on transfers to connected companies, not to family members). The 'sell low' strategy does not work for CGT purposes."

    answer: "A pure gift of property with no consideration (no mortgage to assume, no cash to the donor) attracts no SDLT, regardless of value. Where the donee assumes an outstanding mortgage on the property, the mortgage assumed is deemed consideration and SDLT is calculated on that amount, plus the 5% additional dwellings surcharge if the donee already owns property. So gifting an unencumbered £600,000 property is SDLT-free, but gifting the same property with a £400,000 mortgage to a child who already owns a home triggers SDLT on £400,000 of deemed consideration including the 5% surcharge (£30,000 of SDLT at 2026-27 rates). Where only a share is gifted, only the matching share of the mortgage counts."
  - question: "Can I gift partial ownership and use multiple annual exempt amounts over several years?"
    answer: "Yes, in principle. Each disposal is a separate event for CGT, so gifting 20% to a child this tax year and another 20% next year uses each year's annual exempt amount. However, the linked transactions rule in TCGA 1992 s19 can apply to a series of gifts to connected people within six years. It does not merge the gifts into one, but it can change how each slice is valued, so a 20% share may be valued as a straight fifth of the whole rather than at a discount for being a minority share. Staging works most cleanly where there is a genuine reason for it (e.g., the child reaching majority, financial need at specific points) and is more vulnerable to challenge where it is done purely for CGT advantage."

Where the whole gain is covered by PPR, losses or the annual exempt amount, a UK resident does not need to file the 60-day return. Non-UK residents must file for every UK property disposal, even where no tax is due. Late filing attracts a £100 fixed penalty, then £10 a day from day 91, then 5% of the tax due (or £300 if greater) at 6 and 12 months."

    answer: "You can, usually through a bare trust because a child cannot hold the legal title. The CGT position is the same as a gift to an adult: a disposal at market value. Under the settlements rules (ITTOIA 2005 s629) rent from property a parent has given to their own minor child is taxed on the parent until the child turns 18, apart from a £100 a year de minimis.     answer: "Yes, in some cases. Transferring property into the FIC is itself a disposal at market value for CGT, with no holdover: s165 does not apply to shares in an investment company and s260 does not apply because there is no chargeable lifetime transfer. The IHT benefit comes from gifting growth shares, which are potentially exempt transfers, so the seven-year clock starts on the share gift, not on forming the company.     answer: "It changes the filing, not the basic rule. A non-UK resident giving UK property must file the 60-day return for every UK property disposal, even where no tax is due. Where the recipient lives abroad, the giver's CGT is worked out in the same way, and the recipient will be within non-resident CGT when they later sell the UK property. If a non-resident recipient takes over a mortgage, the 2% non-resident SDLT surcharge can apply on top of the standard rates and the 5% surcharge."

    answer: "Capital gains tax works the same way across the UK, so the market value rule, the reliefs and the 60-day return apply in Scotland and Wales too. <p>Gifting property to family members triggers capital gains tax in most cases, even though no money changes hands. The market value rule in TCGA 1992 s17, with the connected persons rules in s18 and s286, imposes market value as the deemed consideration for the disposal, so the donor pays CGT on the gain over their original base cost. This catches many families by surprise. This guide covers when CGT is due and who pays it, the four main reliefs available, the interaction with inheritance tax and SDLT, gifting a home you want to keep living in, gifting a share of a property, and a series of worked examples for the most common scenarios (parent to adult child, spousal transfers, lifetime trust gifts, a let flat with a mortgage). For the full multi-tax picture of a gift (CGT, SDLT on any assumed mortgage, the IHT 7-year clock and reservation-of-benefit traps), see our guide to <a href="/blog/capital-gains-tax/gifting-property-and-deed-of-gift-tax-implications">gifting property and deed of gift tax implications</a>.</p>

<h2>The core rule: market value disposal between connected persons</h2>

<p>Under TCGA 1992 s17 and s18, a gift, or any disposal between connected persons (defined in s286), is treated as taking place at market value, regardless of the actual consideration. Connected persons include:</p>

<ul>
<li>Spouse or civil partner (but these get separate s58 no-gain-no-loss treatment, see below)</li>
<li>Parents, grandparents, and other ascendants</li>
<li>Children, grandchildren, and other descendants</li>
<li>Siblings</li>
<li>In-laws (spouses or civil partners of the above)</li>
<li>Companies under common control with the individual or their relatives</li>
<li>Business partners, and their spouses and relatives (except for disposals of partnership assets under bona fide commercial arrangements)</li>
</ul>

<p>The "I sold it for £1" strategy does not work. Whatever the legal documents say, HMRC computes CGT on market value at the date of disposal. A gift to someone outside this list, such as an unmarried partner or a friend, is still a gift rather than an arm's-length bargain, so market value applies to it as well.</p>

<h2>Who pays CGT on a gifted property, and when</h2>

<p>The person making the gift pays. The disposal is theirs, so the CGT is the giver's bill, not the recipient's. The recipient pays nothing on receiving the property; they simply take its market value at the date of the gift as their base cost for the future.</p>

<p>Where CGT is due, the giver files the HMRC CGT on UK property return and pays the tax within 60 days of completion of the transfer. Because a gift brings in no sale proceeds, the giver usually funds the tax from savings or other resources. That cash cost is the single most common reason families rethink a gift of a buy-to-let, and it is why the order of steps (spouse transfer first, timing within a tax year, whether a mortgage moves) matters before the deed is signed.</p>

<p>There are two cases where a gift of property normally produces no CGT to pay: a gift to your spouse or civil partner, and a gift of a home that was your only or main residence for the whole time you owned it. Both are covered under the reliefs below.</p>

<h2>Calculating the gain on a property gift</h2>

<p>The CGT computation is identical to a regular sale:</p>

<tr><td><strong>Market value at date of gift</strong></td><td>£X</td></tr>
<tr><td>Less original purchase price</td><td>(£Y)</td></tr>
<tr><td>Less qualifying improvement costs (capital, not revenue)</td><td>(£Z)</td></tr>
<tr><td>Less SDLT and legal fees paid on original purchase</td><td>(£A)</td></tr>
<tr><td>Less legal fees and the valuation fee on the gift transfer</td><td>(£B)</td></tr>
<tr><td>Less Principal Private Residence relief (if applicable)</td><td>(£C)</td></tr>
<tr><td><strong>Chargeable gain</strong></td><td>£G</td></tr>
<tr><td>Less £3,000 annual exempt amount (2026-27)</td><td>(£3,000)</td></tr>
<tr><td><strong>Taxable gain</strong></td><td>£T</td></tr>
<tr><td>Tax at 18% (basic rate band remaining) or 24% (higher rate)</td><td>£CGT</td></tr>
</tbody>
</table>

<p>Mortgage interest and early repayment charges are never deductible from the gain, and nor is a mortgage the recipient takes over: the gain is measured on market value either way.</p>

<p>Where CGT is due, report and pay within 60 days of completion of the gift through the <a href="https://www.gov.uk/report-and-pay-your-capital-gains-tax">HMRC CGT on UK property service</a>. If PPR, losses or the annual exempt amount cover the whole gain, a UK resident has no 60-day return to file. A non-UK resident must file the return for every UK property disposal, even where no tax is due.</p>

<p><strong>How the property is valued:</strong> market value means the price the property would fetch on the open market at the date of the gift. HMRC can and does challenge valuations, so use a RICS-qualified surveyor and keep a written report dated to the gift. For a let property the valuation is of the property with the tenant in place.</p>

<h2>The four main reliefs</h2>

<h3>1. Spouse / civil partner: TCGA 1992 s58 (no gain, no loss)</h3>

<p>Transfers between spouses or civil partners living together are treated as taking place for a consideration that produces neither gain nor loss. The recipient takes the transferor's base cost and acquisition date. No CGT arises. No annual exempt amount is used. This is the most powerful and most common CGT planning route in family situations.</p>

<p><strong>Practical use:</strong> a higher rate husband owns a BTL with a £100,000 latent gain. Before selling, he transfers 50% to his wife under s58 (no CGT triggered). On subsequent sale to a third party, he pays 24% on his half and she pays 18% on hers (until her basic rate band is filled), and both use a £3,000 annual exempt amount. Even if her whole basic rate band is unused, only £37,700 of her £47,000 taxable gain is taxed at 18%, so the bill falls from £23,280 (24% of £97,000) to £20,298 (£11,280 on his half plus £9,018 on hers), a saving of about £2,982. The saving is smaller if her basic rate band is already partly used.</p>

<p>The transfer must be a genuine, documented transfer of beneficial ownership. Use a Deed of Trust and (where the legal title is to be changed) update Land Registry. Married couples who only file Form 17 without an underlying Deed of Trust risk HMRC challenging the substance, because Form 17 only declares an unequal ownership that already exists; it does not create one. If the receiving spouse takes on a share of the mortgage, that share of the debt counts as consideration for SDLT.</p>

<h3>2. Principal Private Residence relief: TCGA 1992 s222 to s224</h3>

<p>If the property was your main residence at any point, PPR exempts the gain attributable to the periods of actual occupation plus the final 9 months of ownership (36 months where you or your spouse or civil partner is disabled or a long-term care home resident and neither of you has another home that could qualify). The formula is:</p>

<p>PPR relief = Total gain × (months of occupation + 9) / total months of ownership</p>

<p>Letting Relief, post the April 2020 reforms, is only available where you occupied the property as your only or main residence in shared occupancy with the tenant (e.g., taking in lodgers). Most ordinary BTL scenarios no longer qualify for Letting Relief.</p>

<p>A married couple can have only one main residence between them for PPR. Gifting your own home outright, having lived in it throughout, normally leaves no CGT to pay; the tax issue then becomes inheritance tax, particularly if you plan to stay living there (see the section on gifting your home below).</p>

<p>See our <a href="/blog/capital-gains-tax/ppr-relief-calculation-former-home-step-by-step">PPR calculation walkthrough</a> for the mechanics.</p>

<h3>3. Section 165 holdover relief: gifts of business assets</h3>

<p>Section 165 TCGA 1992 lets the donor and donee jointly elect to defer the gain on a gift of qualifying business assets. The donor pays no CGT now. The donee takes the donor's base cost (so they inherit the latent gain and pay it on their eventual disposal).</p>

<p>Qualifying assets include:</p>
<ul>
<li>Assets used in a trade carried on by the donor</li>
<li>Shares and securities in an unquoted trading company</li>
<li>Assets used in a partnership in which the donor was a partner</li>
<li>Certain agricultural property</li>
</ul>

<p>Critically, residential BTL property held as investment does NOT qualify. The activity must be a trade. A short-stay accommodation business with significant services (cleaning, breakfast, concierge) might qualify; a passive long-term let does not. Since the furnished holiday lettings regime ended on 6 April 2025 there is no special treatment for holiday lets: serviced accommodation counts only if the services are extensive enough to make it a genuine trade on the facts. Shares in a property investment company, including a Family Investment Company, do not qualify either.</p>

<h3>4. Section 260 holdover relief: gifts to relevant property trusts</h3>

<p>Section 260 TCGA 1992 provides holdover relief on gifts that are immediately chargeable to inheritance tax. The most common case is a gift into a discretionary trust (which is a chargeable lifetime transfer for IHT). The donor and trustees jointly elect to hold over the gain. The relief is not available if the trust is settlor-interested, meaning the settlor or their spouse can benefit; in that case CGT is due immediately at market value.</p>

<p>The catch: if the gift exceeds the donor's nil-rate band (£325,000), IHT is immediately due on the excess at 20% (the lifetime rate). For high-value gifts this is often the wrong route. For gifts of modest value with significant latent gains it can be useful.</p>

<p>The trust then holds the property, with potential ten-yearly anniversary IHT charges (up to 6%) and exit charges when the property leaves the trust. Trust structures need careful long-term planning.</p>

<h2>Worked example 1: parent gifts BTL to adult child</h2>

<p>Margaret bought a flat in 2009 for £180,000 (SDLT £1,800, legal fees £1,200). She has held it as a BTL throughout. Current market value £350,000. She gifts it to her son David in 2026-27. Margaret is a higher rate taxpayer.</p>

<table>
<tbody>
<tr><td>Market value at gift</td><td>£350,000</td></tr>
<tr><td>Less original cost + SDLT + legal fees</td><td>(£183,000)</td></tr>
<tr><td>Less legal fees on the gift (£800)</td><td>(£800)</td></tr>
<tr><td>Chargeable gain</td><td>£166,200</td></tr>
<tr><td>Less £3,000 annual exempt amount</td><td>(£3,000)</td></tr>
<tr><td>Taxable gain</td><td>£163,200</td></tr>
<tr><td>CGT at 24% (higher rate, BTL is investment so no s165)</td><td><strong>£39,168</strong></td></tr>
</tbody>
</table>

<p>Margaret must report and pay this within 60 days of completion of the transfer through the HMRC CGT on UK property service. She receives no money, so the £39,168 is real cash she needs to find from other resources.</p>

<p>If she dies within 7 years of the gift, the £350,000 value at gift comes back into her estate for IHT. Taper relief then reduces the tax (not the value of the gift) for deaths between years 3 and 7, and only on any part of the gift above her available nil-rate band. CGT paid is not refunded on death.</p>

<h2>Worked example 2: same gift but split via spouse first</h2>

<p>Margaret transfers 50% of the flat to her husband Tom before gifting to David. Tom's only income is a small pension within his personal allowance, so his whole £37,700 basic rate band is available. Tom and Margaret then each gift their 50% share to David in the same tax year.</p>

<tr><td>Tom's half: gain £83,100 (taking Margaret's base cost), less £3,000 AEA, taxable £80,100</td><td></td></tr>

<p>A saving of £2,982 versus the single-donor gift. The saving shrinks if Tom's income already uses part of his basic rate band. Further savings are possible where the gifts are spread across two tax years, using a second annual exempt amount for each spouse. Tom's half must be genuinely his, with no arrangement obliging him to pass it on.</p>

<h2>Worked example 3: gift of a former main residence</h2>

<p>Tony bought a house in 2014 for £200,000, lived in it for 5 years, then let it out for 7 years. Gifts to his daughter in 2026 at market value £400,000.</p>

<ul>
<li>Total ownership period: 12 years = 144 months</li>
<li>Period of actual occupation: 60 months</li>
<li>Final 9 months always exempt</li>
<li>Total PPR-eligible months: 69 / 144 = 47.9%</li>
<li>Gross gain: £200,000</li>
<li>PPR-exempt portion: £200,000 × 69/144 = £95,833</li>
<li>Chargeable gain: £104,167</li>
<li>Less £3,000 AEA: £101,167 taxable at 24% higher rate</li>
<li>CGT: £24,280</li>
</ul>

<p>PPR has reduced the CGT from £47,280 (24% of £197,000) to £24,280, a saving of £23,000.</p>

<h2>Worked example 4: gift to discretionary trust using s260 holdover</h2>

<p>Sarah gifts a BTL flat worth £300,000 (base cost £150,000, latent gain £150,000) into a discretionary trust for her three minor grandchildren in 2026-27. Sarah has not made any prior chargeable transfers in the last 7 years, and neither she nor her husband can benefit from the trust.</p>

<ul>
<li>For CGT: s260 holdover applies because the gift is a chargeable lifetime transfer for IHT and the trust is not settlor-interested. No CGT now; the trust takes the £150,000 base cost.</li>
<li>For IHT: gift value £300,000, within the £325,000 nil-rate band, so no immediate IHT charge.</li>
<li>For SDLT: assuming no mortgage assumed by the trust, no SDLT charge.</li>
<li>Future: the trust will face ten-yearly anniversary IHT charges (currently 6% maximum) and an exit charge when the property is distributed to a grandchild.</li>
</ul>

<p>Sarah has deferred about £35,280 of CGT (24% of the £147,000 that would have been taxable after her annual exempt amount) by using s260. The cost is the long-term trust IHT regime and the loss of access to the property's value during her lifetime. Suitable for some family situations, not others.</p>

<h2>Worked example 5: gifting a let flat with a mortgage to an adult child</h2>

<p>Helen, a higher rate taxpayer, bought a flat in 2012 for £160,000 and paid £3,500 in SDLT and legal fees. It has been let throughout. In 2026-27 she gifts it to her daughter Amy, who already owns her own home. A RICS valuation with the tenant in place gives a market value of £280,000. The outstanding mortgage is £100,000, and Amy takes it over with the lender's agreement. Legal and valuation fees on the gift come to £1,700.</p>

<table>
<tbody>
<tr><td>Market value at gift (tenanted)</td><td>£280,000</td></tr>
<tr><td>Less original cost + SDLT + legal fees</td><td>(£163,500)</td></tr>
<tr><td>Less legal and valuation fees on the gift</td><td>(£1,700)</td></tr>
<tr><td>Chargeable gain</td><td>£114,800</td></tr>
<tr><td>Less £3,000 annual exempt amount</td><td>(£3,000)</td></tr>
<tr><td>Taxable gain</td><td>£111,800</td></tr>
<tr><td>Helen's CGT at 24%, due within 60 days of completion</td><td><strong>£26,832</strong></td></tr>
<tr><td>Amy's SDLT on the £100,000 debt she takes over (0% standard rate plus 5% surcharge, as she already owns a home)</td><td><strong>£5,000</strong></td></tr>
</tbody>
</table>

<p>Three points come out of this. First, the mortgage does not reduce Helen's gain: CGT is measured on market value regardless of the debt. Second, Amy's SDLT exists only because the mortgage moves; if Helen paid off the £100,000 first, there would be no SDLT, but she would need that cash on top of the £26,832 of CGT. Third, from the date of the gift the rent is Amy's income, taxed on her self assessment (see the donee's position below).</p>

<p>For inheritance tax the gift is a potentially exempt transfer, so it leaves Helen's estate if she survives seven years, provided she takes no share of the rent afterwards.</p>

<h2>SDLT on property gifts</h2>

<p>Pure gift of unencumbered property with no cash or other consideration: zero SDLT.</p>

<p>Gift of property where the donee assumes an outstanding mortgage: SDLT on the mortgage value treated as consideration (FA 2003 Sch 4 para 8), capped at the property's market value. The 5% additional dwellings surcharge (raised from 3% on 31 October 2024) applies if the donee already owns another dwelling. So gifting a £600,000 property with a £400,000 mortgage to a child who owns their main home triggers SDLT of £30,000 at 2026-27 rates (£10,000 of standard SDLT on £400,000 plus the 5% surcharge of £20,000).</p>

<p>Where a share is gifted, only the share of the mortgage the recipient becomes liable for counts. Gifting a half share of a property with a £200,000 mortgage, where the recipient takes on half the debt, puts £100,000 in the SDLT calculation. In Scotland and Wales the same principle applies under LBTT and LTT, with their own bands and surcharges.</p>

<p>This often makes "redeem the mortgage before gifting" the right answer to avoid the SDLT charge entirely, even if it requires the donor to find the cash.</p>

<h2>IHT and the seven-year clock</h2>

<p>A gift to an individual (other than a spouse) is a Potentially Exempt Transfer for inheritance tax. If the donor survives seven years, the gift falls fully outside the estate. Death within seven years brings the gift value (at date of gift) back into the estate:</p>

<ul>
<li>0 to 3 years: full 40% IHT charge on the gift value above the nil-rate band</li>
<li>3 to 4 years: 80% of the IHT charge (effective 32%)</li>
<li>4 to 5 years: 60% (effective 24%)</li>
<li>5 to 6 years: 40% (effective 16%)</li>
<li>6 to 7 years: 20% (effective 8%)</li>
<li>7+ years: 0%</li>
</ul>

<p>This is "taper relief" on the IHT itself, not on the gift value. It only operates above the nil-rate band. CGT paid at the time of the gift is not credited against any subsequent IHT charge, and paying CGT does not take the gift outside the seven-year rule: the two taxes are separate. The nil-rate band is £325,000 per person, frozen until 5 April 2031. The £3,000 IHT annual exemption (with one year's carry-back) can reduce the value of the gift that counts.</p>

<h2>Gifting your home and carrying on living there (or renting it back)</h2>

<p>Many parents want to give the family home to their children while staying put. For CGT this is usually straightforward: if the house has been your only or main home throughout, PPR normally covers the gain. The problem is inheritance tax. Under FA 1986 s102, a gift where you keep living in the property, or keep any other benefit from it, is a gift with reservation of benefit. The house is treated as still in your estate at its value on the date of your death, and the seven-year clock does not start while you keep the benefit.</p>

<p>There are three ways out, and each is narrow:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Pay a full market rent.</strong> You pay your children an open-market rent for continuing to live there, reviewed periodically and actually paid, with records. Your children declare that rent as their income. A token or informal rent brings the reservation back.</li>
<li><strong>Move out completely.</strong> You leave and do not return other than for ordinary family visits.</li>
<li><strong>Gift a share and both live there.</strong> Under FA 1986 s102B(4), you gift an undivided share (for example half), the recipient genuinely lives there as their home too, and you take no benefit from them connected with the gift. It fails if the child does not really live there.</li>
</ul>

<p>Where an arrangement escapes the reservation rules but you still enjoy the property, the pre-owned assets income tax charge (FA 2004 Sch 15) can apply instead. The same rules catch a landlord who gifts a rental but keeps a share of the rent. Our guide to <a href="/blog/landlord-tax-essentials/iht-gifts-with-reservation-of-benefit-property">gifts with reservation of benefit on property</a> works through each route.</p>

<p>Where you want to keep living in the home or keep some of the rent, Property Tax Partners offers <a href="/for/gifting-property-to-family">specialist help with a family property gift</a>, setting the CGT, the stamp duty and the seven-year position side by side before the deed is signed.</p>

<h2>Gifting a share or half of a property</h2>

<p>You do not have to give the whole property. A gift of a share (half to a son, a third to each of three children, a half share to a sister or a parent) is a disposal of that share at market value, so the gain is the share's portion of market value less the same portion of your costs.</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>To your spouse or civil partner:</strong> no CGT under s58, and no SDLT unless they take on a share of the mortgage.</li>
<li><strong>To a child, parent, sibling or anyone else:</strong> CGT on the gain in that share, after your annual exempt amount. If the property has been your only or main home throughout, PPR usually covers it.</li>
<li><strong>Joint tenants:</strong> a joint tenant has no separate share to give. The joint tenancy has to be severed into a tenancy in common first.</li>
<li><strong>Adding a child to the deeds of your home:</strong> for IHT this is only effective while you both live there if your child genuinely lives there as their home and you take no benefit from them connected with the gift (the s102B(4) route above).</li>
<li><strong>Rental income:</strong> the rent follows the beneficial shares, so the recipient is taxed on their share from the date of the gift (or the parent is, where the child is under 18).</li>
</ul>

<p>When the recipient later sells their share, their base cost is the market value of the share at the date of the gift. They only get PPR on their share for the periods it was their own main residence.</p>

<h2>The donee's tax position after the gift</h2>

<p>The donee takes the property at the gifted market value as their CGT base cost (unless a holdover election applied, in which case they take the donor's lower base cost). From the date of gift, they receive any rental income on their own self assessment, file SA105 (or are pulled into MTD for ITSA if their qualifying income exceeds the threshold: £50,000 from April 2026, £30,000 from April 2027 and £20,000 from April 2028), and stand in the donor's shoes for future capital improvement records and tax history.</p>

<p>The donee does not pay income tax on receiving the property itself. They will pay income tax on rental profits going forward (subject to Section 24 if held personally), and CGT on any future disposal calculated against their new base cost. Where the donee is the donor's own child under 18, rental income is taxed on the parent until the child turns 18 (ITTOIA 2005 s629). Our guide to <a href="/blog/incorporation-and-company-structures/gifting-property-to-minor-children-bare-trust-mechanics-tax-traps">gifting property to minor children</a> covers the bare trust mechanics.</p>

<h2>Selling a property that was gifted to you</h2>

<p>If you were gifted a property and now sell it, your gain is worked out from the market value at the date of the gift, not from what the giver originally paid. The exception is where a holdover election was made (s165 or s260): then you take over the giver's original base cost and the deferred gain comes to you on sale.</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>If you lived in it:</strong> PPR covers the gain for the periods it was your only or main home, plus the final 9 months.</li>
<li><strong>If you let it or kept it empty:</strong> the gain since the gift is taxable at 18% or 24% after your annual exempt amount, reported and paid within 60 days of completion where tax is due.</li>
<li><strong>If the giver had inherited it:</strong> that only changes the giver's CGT, which was measured from the probate value. Your base cost is still the market value at the date of the gift.</li>
</ul>

<p>Keep the giver's valuation and transfer documents: they set your base cost, and without them the figure is open to challenge when you sell.</p>

<h2>Family Investment Companies as an alternative</h2>

<p>For families with substantial residential property holdings, a Family Investment Company can be a more controlled lifetime planning structure than direct gifting. The founder retains voting shares and (initially) growth shares. As children reach adulthood, growth shares are gifted to them; future capital appreciation accrues to them, freezing the founder's estate for IHT purposes. The seven-year clock runs from each share gift, not from forming the company.</p>

<p>Transferring property INTO the FIC is itself a disposal at market value for CGT (s17), so the same gift-tax problem arises at the founding moment, and neither s165 nor s260 holdover is available on it. Section 162 incorporation relief may roll over the gain if the property is part of a genuine business transferred as a whole in exchange for shares; HMRC looks for evidence of real business activity, with Ramsay v HMRC [2013] setting the threshold. For transfers on or after 6 April 2026, s162 relief has to be claimed; it is no longer automatic.</p>

<h2>How to reduce CGT when gifting property</h2>

<p>There is no way to make an ordinary buy-to-let gift free of CGT by calling it a gift or selling it for £1. What does reduce the bill:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Transfer to a spouse first.</strong> A s58 transfer before the family gift brings in a second annual exempt amount and, where available, a second basic rate band (worked example 2).</li>
<li><strong>Count every main residence period.</strong> Any time the property was your main home, plus the final 9 months, comes off the gain (worked example 3).</li>
<li><strong>Claim every allowable cost.</strong> Purchase costs, capital improvements, and the legal and valuation fees on the gift all reduce the gain.</li>
<li><strong>Choose the tax year.</strong> A year with lower income leaves more of the gain at 18%, and spreading gifts of shares across tax years uses more than one annual exempt amount.</li>
<li><strong>Use capital losses.</strong> Losses on other disposals reduce the gain.</li>
<li><strong>Consider the trust route.</strong> s260 holdover defers the gain on a gift into a non-settlor-interested trust, at the price of the trust IHT regime (worked example 4).</li>
<li><strong>Weigh holding until death.</strong> Death is not a CGT disposal, and heirs take the property at probate value. The trade-off is that the whole value then sits in the estate for inheritance tax at 40% above the available nil-rate bands.</li>
</ul>

<h2>Common mistakes when gifting property</h2>

<ul>
<li><strong>Assuming no tax applies because no money changes hands.</strong> CGT at market value still applies under s17.</li>
<li><strong>Skipping the 60-day CGT return.</strong> Where CGT is due on the gift, the return and the payment are both due within 60 days of completion. Non-UK residents must file even where no tax is due.</li>
<li><strong>Using an unrealistic valuation.</strong> A figure without a dated RICS report is the easiest part of the return for HMRC to challenge.</li>
<li><strong>Assuming s165 holdover applies to BTL.</strong> It almost never does. Investment activity is excluded.</li>
<li><strong>Ignoring SDLT on assumed mortgages.</strong> Gifting an encumbered property without redeeming the mortgage puts the debt taken over into the SDLT calculation at residential rates, including the 5% surcharge where the recipient already owns a home.</li>
<li><strong>Forgetting the seven-year IHT clock.</strong> Older donors should consider whether to insure the IHT exposure during the seven-year window.</li>
<li><strong>Gifting the home and carrying on living there rent-free.</strong> Unless the narrow shared-occupation route applies, the gift stays in your estate for IHT.</li>
<li><strong>Gifting without a Deed of Trust on partial ownership.</strong> Where you keep legal title but want beneficial ownership to shift, a written Deed of Trust is essential evidence.</li>
<li><strong>Misjudging the donee's tax position.</strong> A higher rate donee inheriting rental income may face more tax than the donor was paying.</li>
</ul>
