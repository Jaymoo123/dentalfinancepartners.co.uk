<p>Closing a limited company sounds like one decision. It is really three: whether you are pausing or finishing, what is left inside the company when you do it, and how the money comes out. Get the third one wrong and a routine £13 filing turns a capital distribution into a dividend taxed at up to 39.35 per cent.</p>
<p>Three routes exist: leave the company dormant, apply to strike it off on form DS01, or put it through a members' voluntary liquidation. Which one is open to you is settled almost entirely by what is left inside the company on the day you decide, and the threshold that separates the cheap route from the formal one is £25,000. Everything below works outward from that number, through the DS01 mechanics, to the property that has to be out of the company before it is dissolved.</p>
<p>Two things sit deliberately elsewhere. The detailed tax analysis of a liquidation distribution, including where business asset disposal relief does and does not reach, is on the <a href="/blog/incorporation-and-company-structures/mvl-members-voluntary-liquidation-property-company-cgt-vs-income-treatment">members' voluntary liquidation guide</a>. If you are structuring rather than unwinding, the <a href="/spv-company">property SPV hub</a> is the better starting point.</p>

<h2>Reasons for dissolving a company</h2>
<p>Most property companies are closed for one of five reasons, and the reason usually points at the route:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>The portfolio has been sold.</strong> The single most common case. The vehicle has done its job, the cash is in the bank, and the only question left is how it comes out.</li>
<li><strong>Retirement or a change of plan.</strong> The landlord is winding down rather than reinvesting, so there is no reason to keep a vehicle alive.</li>
<li><strong>The arithmetic that justified the company no longer works.</strong> Section 24 mortgage interest restriction made the corporate route attractive for leveraged higher-rate landlords, but a low-gearing or single-property portfolio can end up paying corporation tax plus dividend tax for no net benefit. Our <a href="/blog/incorporation-and-company-structures/limited-company-vs-personal-ownership-tax-comparison-2026">limited company versus personal ownership comparison</a> sets out where the crossover sits.</li>
<li><strong>The company is dormant and not worth the filings.</strong> An empty company still needs a confirmation statement every year. If you are certain you will not buy again through it, closing is cheaper than maintaining it.</li>
<li><strong>Moving back to personal ownership.</strong> Taking the properties out into personal names and then dissolving the shell. Note that a transfer out is a disposal at market value for the company and usually a stamp duty land tax event for the recipient, so this is a tax decision before it is a closure decision.</li>
</ul>
<p>Solvency is the dividing line that matters. Everything on this page assumes the company can pay its debts in full. A company that cannot is an insolvency matter, and the route is a creditors' voluntary liquidation through a licensed insolvency practitioner, not a strike-off.</p>

<h2>The three ways to close (or pause) a property company</h2>
<p>The route is decided by two facts: whether anything of value is left in the company, and whether you ever want to use the vehicle again.</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Dormant.</strong> The company stops trading but stays on the register. It still files a confirmation statement and dormant accounts each year. This is a pause, not a closure, and it only makes sense if there are no leftover assets to deal with and you might buy again through the same company.</li>
<li><strong>Voluntary strike-off (form DS01).</strong> The company is removed from the register and ceases to exist. Available only where there are no assets or liabilities left, and where total distributions made in anticipation of dissolution are £25,000 or less.</li>
<li><strong>Members' voluntary liquidation (MVL).</strong> A formal solvent wind-up run by a licensed insolvency practitioner. Needed where more than £25,000 is being distributed, or where the shareholders want formal capital treatment regardless of value.</li>
</ul>
<p>The £25,000 figure is not an accounting convention. It comes from <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2010/8/section/1030A" rel="nofollow">CTA 2010 s.1030A</a>, and it is the single most important number on this page.</p>

<h2>Can I just make my SPV dormant instead?</h2>
<p>Yes, if what you actually want is to stop, not to finish. Dormancy is genuinely useful for a landlord between purchases: the company keeps its incorporation date, its bank account history and its relationship with lenders, all of which have some value when you come back to the market. Filing dormant accounts is cheap and quick.</p>
<p>But be honest about what it is. A dormant company is still on the register, still has directors with statutory duties, and still has an annual filing obligation that never ends. Miss it and you collect late filing penalties on a company that earns nothing. Dormancy is a pause with a small recurring cost, not a closure.</p>
<p>One practical constraint: a company cannot be dormant and hold a let property. Rent received is a transaction, and a company that receives rent is trading for accounts purposes. Dormancy is only available once the properties are gone. The filing mechanics, what counts as a significant accounting transaction, and how to file are covered in our <a href="/blog/property-accountant-services/file-dormant-accounts-a-complete-guide">guide to filing dormant accounts</a>.</p>

<h2>DS01 voluntary strike-off: eligibility, cost and timeline</h2>
<p>Voluntary strike-off is the cheap route, and for a single-property SPV that has sold up and settled its debts, it is usually the right one.</p>
<h3>Eligibility conditions</h3>
<p>The company must not, in the three months before the application:</p>
<ul>
<li>have traded or otherwise carried on business;</li>
<li>have changed its name;</li>
<li>have disposed for value of property or rights it held for disposal in the normal course of business (selling the investment property itself does not usually breach this, but the three-month clock still runs from when trading stopped);</li>
<li>have engaged in any activity other than what is necessary to make the strike-off application, settle affairs or meet a statutory requirement.</li>
</ul>
<p>It also must not be in liquidation, subject to a creditor agreement, or the subject of insolvency proceedings. In practice the three-month rule means you stop, wait a quarter, then apply.</p>
<h3>Cost</h3>
<p>The Companies House fee is <strong>£13 online or £18 on paper</strong>. That is the whole mandatory government cost of closing a company this way. You will normally also pay for final accounts and the closing corporation tax return, and you must clear any outstanding filing penalties first.</p>
<h3>Timeline</h3>
<p>Around three to four months. Companies House checks the application, publishes a first notice in the Gazette, allows an objection period of at least two months, then publishes a second notice and dissolves the company.</p>
<h3>Who must be told</h3>
<p>Within seven days of filing, you must send a copy of the application to every member, creditor, employee, and any director who did not sign the form. Skipping this is an offence and is also the fastest way to attract an objection.</p>
<h3>Objections</h3>
<p>Any creditor can object during the notice period, and HMRC does so routinely where returns are outstanding, tax is unpaid, or a VAT or PAYE registration is still live. An objection suspends the application. There is no way round it other than fixing the underlying problem and reapplying, so file everything and close the registrations before you send the DS01, not after.</p>
<p>A word on the "just stop filing and let them strike it off" approach: it works, eventually, but the path runs through late filing penalties, a compulsory strike-off notice and, for persistent failure, director disqualification proceedings. It costs more than £13 and leaves a record. Do it properly.</p>

<h2>The £25,000 line: CTA 2010 s.1030A and s.1030B</h2>
<p>This is where most closures go wrong.</p>
<p>Under CTA 2010 s.1030A, a distribution made by a company in anticipation of its dissolution is <strong>not</strong> treated as an income distribution, and therefore gets capital treatment in the shareholder's hands, provided two things hold. Condition A is that the company intends to secure payment of every sum due to it and to satisfy its debts and liabilities. Condition B is that the total amount distributed in respect of shares does not exceed <strong>£25,000</strong>.</p>
<p>If Condition B fails, <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2010/8/section/1030B" rel="nofollow">s.1030B</a> bites, and it bites hard: the <strong>whole</strong> distribution is treated as an income distribution, not just the excess over £25,000. There is no marginal relief, no tapering and no apportionment. Distribute £26,000 and all £26,000 is a dividend.</p>
<aside><p><strong>The £1,000 mistake.</strong> A shareholder distributing £26,000 on a strike-off is taxed on the full £26,000 as dividend income. The same shareholder distributing £25,000 is taxed on a capital gain, after the annual exempt amount. The £1,000 difference in cash can swing several thousand pounds of tax. If your reserves are anywhere near the line, get the numbers checked before you file the DS01.</p></aside>
<p>Note also that £25,000 is a total across all distributions in anticipation of dissolution, not a per-shareholder allowance. Two shareholders taking £15,000 each have distributed £30,000 and have failed Condition B.</p>

<h2>What happens to the property when I close the company?</h2>
<p>Every property must be out of the company before it is dissolved. There are two ways out:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Sell it.</strong> An open-market sale, mortgage redeemed, proceeds banked. The company pays corporation tax on the chargeable gain in its final period.</li>
<li><strong>Transfer it out.</strong> A transfer to a shareholder or connected party is a disposal at market value for corporation tax whatever price is documented, and it will normally trigger stamp duty land tax for the recipient where consideration (including a mortgage taken over) is given. This is a different exercise from closing the company and is worth planning separately.</li>
</ul>
<p>If a property is still registered to the company on the day it is dissolved, it passes to the Crown as <strong>bona vacantia</strong> under the Companies Act 2006. So does any cash left in the company bank account. Getting it back means applying to restore the company to the register, and then dealing with the Treasury Solicitor or the relevant Crown body over the asset itself. It is slow, it is expensive, and it is entirely avoidable.</p>
<p>The practical checklist before filing anything: properties sold or transferred, mortgages redeemed and charges satisfied at Companies House, bank account emptied and closed, VAT deregistered, PAYE scheme closed, creditors paid.</p>

<h2>When you need an MVL instead of a DS01</h2>
<p>Use a members' voluntary liquidation where more than £25,000 is coming out, or where there are assets that need a liquidator to distribute properly. An MVL is a formal solvent wind-up conducted by a licensed insolvency practitioner, who takes control of the company, realises the assets, settles the liabilities and distributes the surplus to shareholders.</p>
<p>The tax point in one paragraph: a distribution in an MVL is a capital distribution, taxed as a capital gain rather than as dividend income, with no £25,000 ceiling. For a higher or additional-rate director that is usually materially cheaper than income treatment, which is why the insolvency practitioner's fee is worth paying above the threshold. Business asset disposal relief can reduce the capital gains tax rate to 18 per cent from 6 April 2026, but only where the shares meet the trading company test, and a pure buy-to-let SPV holding investment property is very unlikely to meet it. Do not assume BADR on a rental SPV.</p>
<p>The full analysis, including the trading company test, the anti-phoenixing targeted anti-avoidance rule and how the numbers compare at different reserve levels, is on our dedicated <a href="/blog/incorporation-and-company-structures/mvl-members-voluntary-liquidation-property-company-cgt-vs-income-treatment">MVL capital versus income treatment guide</a>.</p>
<p>Insolvency practitioner fees for a straightforward single-asset MVL commonly sit in the region of £2,000 to £4,000. Treat that as a framing figure for the route decision, not a quote.</p>

<h2>Closing a limited company: the tax and admin sequence</h2>
<p>Independent of the route, the same closing tasks have to be done, and in roughly this order:</p>
<ol>
<li><strong>Sell or transfer out the properties.</strong> Redeem the mortgages and file the satisfaction of charge at Companies House.</li>
<li><strong>Deregister for VAT</strong> if the company was registered (an opted-to-tax commercial property, for instance). Submit the final VAT return.</li>
<li><strong>Close the PAYE scheme</strong> if there was one, with a final full payment submission marked as the final submission.</li>
<li><strong>Settle the creditors</strong>, including any director's loan owed by the company and any outstanding penalties.</li>
<li><strong>Prepare final accounts</strong> for the shortened final period and file the final corporation tax return with HMRC. Corporation tax on the final period's profits and gains runs at 19 or 25 per cent with marginal relief between the limits.</li>
<li><strong>Pay the corporation tax</strong>, then distribute what is left.</li>
<li><strong>Empty and close the company bank account</strong>, then file the DS01 or appoint the insolvency practitioner.</li>
</ol>
<p>If you would rather draw the reserves down over a couple of years before closing, rather than in one final distribution, the extraction options and their tax cost are set out in our guide to <a href="/blog/incorporation-and-company-structures/extracting-money-from-property-limited-company">extracting money from a property limited company</a>. Planning the drawdown early is often what keeps a closure under the £25,000 line.</p>

<h2>Worked example: two SPVs, two routes</h2>
<p><strong>SPV A, one flat, sold up.</strong> A single-property SPV sold its flat, redeemed the mortgage and paid the corporation tax on the gain. £18,000 sits in the bank and there are no creditors. It stopped trading in March, so by July the three-month condition is met. The directors file a DS01 online for £13, notify the two shareholders, and the company is dissolved about three and a half months later. The £18,000 distributed in anticipation of dissolution is under the £25,000 ceiling, so Condition B of s.1030A is satisfied and the shareholders treat it as capital, using their annual exempt amounts against the gain. Total cost of closing: £13 plus the accountant's final accounts fee.</p>
<p><strong>SPV B, four properties, sold up.</strong> A portfolio SPV sold its four properties over eighteen months and has £120,000 left after corporation tax. A DS01 would be a disaster here: the whole £120,000 would fall under s.1030B and be taxed as an income distribution, at 35.75 or 39.35 per cent for higher and additional-rate shareholders. Instead the shareholders appoint a licensed insolvency practitioner for an MVL. The distribution keeps capital treatment, and the practitioner's fee is a small fraction of the tax saved. Whether business asset disposal relief is available depends on the trading company test, which a rental-holding SPV will not usually meet.</p>
<aside><p>The route decision turns on one number: what is left in the company after corporation tax. If you are within a few thousand pounds of £25,000, or you are not sure whether a director's loan balance counts toward it, that is the point to get the closure modelled rather than filed. Use the enquiry form below with your reserve figure and property position.</p></aside>

<h2>Common mistakes when closing a property company</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Filing the DS01 with a property still in the company.</strong> The title passes to the Crown on dissolution. Restoration is far more expensive than doing it in the right order.</li>
<li><strong>Treating £25,000 as per shareholder.</strong> It is a total across all distributions in anticipation of dissolution.</li>
<li><strong>Distributing first, checking later.</strong> Once the money is out and the total exceeds the ceiling, s.1030B applies to all of it. You cannot unwind it by paying some back.</li>
<li><strong>Leaving the VAT or PAYE registration open.</strong> This is the most common trigger for an HMRC objection to the strike-off.</li>
<li><strong>Forgetting the director's loan account.</strong> If the company owes the director, that is a creditor to settle. If the director owes the company, writing it off on closure has its own tax consequences.</li>
<li><strong>Assuming BADR applies.</strong> A buy-to-let SPV is an investment company, not a trading one, and the relief is unlikely to be available.</li>
</ul>

<h2>Which route should you take?</h2>
<p>Work through it in this order. Do you want the vehicle again in the next couple of years? If yes, and the properties are gone, make it dormant. If no, is anything still owned by the company? If yes, sell or transfer it out first. Once the company holds only cash, is that cash £25,000 or less? If yes, file a DS01 for £13. If no, appoint a licensed insolvency practitioner for an MVL. Everything else on this page is detail hanging off those four questions.</p>

HMRC's treatment of distributions in a winding up is set out in its <a href="https://www.gov.uk/hmrc-internal-manuals/company-taxation-manual" rel="nofollow">Company Taxation Manual</a>.</p>
