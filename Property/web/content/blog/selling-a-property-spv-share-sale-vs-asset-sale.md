  - question: "What does selling an SPV actually mean?"
    answer: "It means one of two very different transactions. Either the shareholders sell their shares, so the buyer takes ownership of the company with its properties, mortgages, history and tax position intact, or the company sells the properties and keeps the sale proceeds as cash inside the corporate shell. The first is a share sale and is taxed on you personally. The second is an asset sale and is taxed on the company first, with a second tax charge when you take the money out. The words are used loosely in the market, so agree which one you mean before you negotiate a price."
  - question: "Is a share sale or an asset sale better for the seller?"
    answer: "A share sale usually wins on tax because it is taxed once, at 18 or 24 per cent capital gains tax on the gain over what you paid for the shares. An asset sale is taxed twice: corporation tax at 19 or 25 per cent on the company's gain, then dividend tax or a liquidation to move the cash to you. The share-sale advantage is not free, because the buyer will discount their offer for the latent gain they inherit. Where a buyer refuses to buy shares at all, an asset sale at full market value can still net more than a share sale at a heavy discount."
  - question: "What is a property-rich company?"
    answer: "A company whose value comes predominantly from UK land. The phrase matters most for non-UK residents, who are taxed on indirect disposals of UK property: selling shares in a company deriving at least 75 per cent of its value from UK land, where the seller holds at least a 25 per cent interest, is within the UK charge and needs a 60-day return. For a UK-resident shareholder selling a UK SPV the label carries no special charge, but buyers and their lawyers use it as shorthand for the diligence they will run on the company's property titles, leases and mortgages."
  - question: "What discount will a buyer want for latent capital gains?"
    answer: "There is no fixed rule and it is purely a negotiation. The starting point is the corporation tax the buyer would eventually pay on the gain built into the property at your original base cost, so on a £150,000 latent gain at the 25 per cent main rate the theoretical figure is £37,500. Buyers rarely get the full amount and sellers rarely give nothing. A common landing zone is somewhere between a third and a half of the latent tax, adjusted for how long the buyer expects to hold and whether they ever intend to sell the property at all."
  - question: "What stamp duty is payable on an SPV share sale?"
    answer: "0.5 per cent of the consideration paid for the shares. It is collected as Stamp Duty Reserve Tax on electronic transfers, or as Stamp Duty on a paper stock transfer form, where the charge applies only if the consideration exceeds £1,000. The higher 1.5 per cent rate applies only to transfers into clearance services and depositary receipt schemes, so it is irrelevant to a private SPV sale. On a £231,250 share price the buyer's stamp duty is about £1,156, against tens of thousands of pounds of SDLT on an equivalent direct property purchase."
  - question: "Do property developers set up a company and then sell it claiming SSE?"
    answer: "The substantial shareholding exemption only helps where the seller is itself a company, not an individual, and where the company being sold is a trading company. A genuine development company carrying out and selling developments can be trading, so a corporate parent selling a development subsidiary may be in scope. A buy-to-let SPV that simply owns and lets property is an investment company, so the exemption does not apply however the shares are held. Individual shareholders never get SSE at all, because it is a corporation tax exemption on a company's gain."
  - question: "Can I claim Business Asset Disposal Relief on selling my SPV shares?"
    answer: "Almost certainly not. BADR requires the company to be a trading company, and passive letting of residential or commercial property is an investment activity, not a trade. Holiday lets lost their special status when the furnished holiday lettings regime was abolished in April 2025, and there is no replacement deeming rule. Where a company genuinely trades, for example a development or serviced-accommodation business with substantial services, BADR can apply at 18 per cent from 6 April 2026, but the trading test is fact-specific and should be tested with advice long before you market the company."
  - question: "Is this the same as selling my house to my own limited company?"
    answer: "No, and the two are frequently confused. Transferring a property you own personally into a company you control is a purchase by the company at market value: it triggers SDLT for the company including the surcharge, and a capital gains disposal for you. That is an incorporation question. This page is about the opposite end of the life cycle, where an existing SPV and its shareholders exit by selling either the shares or the properties to a third party. If you are moving property in rather than out, read our <a href=\"/blog/incorporation-and-company-structures/how-to-transfer-property-into-limited-company-uk\">guide to transferring property into a limited company</a> instead."
  - question: "What happens to the company's mortgage on a share sale?"
    answer: "The mortgage stays where it is, because the borrower is the company and the company has not changed. What does change is who controls it, and almost every buy-to-let facility contains a change-of-control clause requiring lender consent before the shares move. Directors' personal guarantees also need to be released and replaced by the incoming shareholders, which the lender must agree to. Expect the lender to underwrite the buyer as if it were a new application, and start that conversation early because refusal of consent can kill a share sale outright."
  - question: "Do I need a solicitor for an SPV share sale?"
    answer: "Yes. A share sale runs on a share purchase agreement with warranties, disclosure and often an indemnity or price retention for tax risk, and there is no standard form to fall back on. The buyer will run legal, tax and property due diligence on the company's whole history, not just the properties, because they are inheriting every past liability. You also need Companies House filings, stock transfer forms, board and shareholder resolutions, and lender consents. Budget for materially higher legal fees than a straightforward property conveyance."
  - question: "How long does an SPV share sale take?"
    answer: "Plan for two to four months from heads of terms to completion, longer if there are multiple properties, several shareholders or a lender whose consent is needed. Due diligence on the company is the slow part: buyers examine the corporation tax returns, VAT position, accounts, leases, deposits, licensing and any historical activity that predates the property portfolio. An asset sale runs closer to a normal property transaction, so if speed matters to you it is a genuine argument in the asset-sale column rather than a purely tax question."
  - question: "Can I sell just some of the shares in my SPV?"
    answer: "Yes. Selling a partial stake is common where one shareholder exits, or where a seller keeps a minority holding to share in future growth. The tax treatment follows the same rules on the slice sold, with the base cost apportioned across the shareholding, and stamp duty at 0.5 per cent on the consideration for those shares. What changes is the governance: a shareholders' agreement covering decision making, dividend policy, deadlock, drag-along and tag-along rights becomes essential, because a minority holding in a private property company is otherwise very hard to sell on."
  - question: "What does a listing for a property SPV for sale actually mean?"
    answer: "Two quite different things are marketed under that phrase. One is a live SPV with properties, mortgages and a trading history inside it, which is the share sale this page is about, and the price will reflect the net asset value less a latent-gain discount. The other is a ready-made or shelf company with a property SIC code and no assets, sold cheaply as a formation shortcut. If you are the seller, say which you are offering in the first line of the listing, because a buyer expecting an empty shell will not price the diligence a real portfolio company needs."
  - question: "What if the SPV holds cash as well as property?"
    answer: "Surplus cash inflates the price the buyer must fund and attracts the same 0.5 per cent stamp duty as the rest of the consideration, so most sellers extract it before completion rather than selling it. How you strip cash out, and how far that reduces or increases the buyer's discount, is a planning exercise in itself and needs to respect the timing and the tax treatment of whichever extraction route you use. Read the pre-sale extraction guide before you declare a dividend or repay a director's loan in the run-up to a sale."

<p>Two people can say "I am selling my SPV" and mean opposite transactions. In one, the shareholders sell their shares and the buyer takes the company whole, with its properties, mortgages, tax history and original base costs. In the other, the company sells the properties, banks the cash and the shareholders then face the separate problem of getting that cash out. The tax bills differ by tens of thousands of pounds on a single flat, and the route is usually settled in the first conversation with a buyer, long before anyone models it.</p>
<p>Nothing about the building changes between those two deals. The same house, the same tenant, the same rent, the same completion date, and a tax bill that can differ by more than £70,000 because of what the buyer's money technically bought. Sell the shares and you are taxed once, on a personal gain, while the company's own tax position simply carries on under new ownership. Sell the property and the company is taxed on its gain first, and then you are taxed again on getting the proceeds out of a company that now holds nothing but cash. The rest of this page prices both routes on the same £400,000 property, including the discount a buyer will ask for when they are the one inheriting your base cost.</p>

<h2>Share sale: capital gains tax on the shares, not the property</h2>
<p>When you sell shares, you are disposing of a personal asset. The gain is your sale proceeds less what the shares cost you, which for most SPVs is the subscription price plus any later share capital you put in. It is not the gain on the property, and the property's own history is irrelevant to your personal computation.</p>
<p>The rate is the general capital gains tax rate for individuals, not a property rate, because shares are shares even when the company owns houses. In practice this makes no difference to the number for 2026/27: gains on non-residential assets were aligned to the same 18 per cent basic-rate and 24 per cent higher-rate bands as residential property gains from 30 October 2024, per the <a href="https://www.gov.uk/capital-gains-tax/rates" rel="nofollow">gov.uk capital gains tax rates</a>. The £3,000 annual exempt amount applies. Get the mechanism right even though the rate lands in the same place, because it drives everything else: the company has made no disposal, so it pays no corporation tax, and the property keeps the base cost it always had. That single fact is what creates the buyer's problem in the next section.</p>
<p>Timing is simpler too. There is no 60-day property return on a share sale by a UK resident, because you have not disposed of UK land. The gain goes in your self assessment return for the tax year of completion. Non-UK residents are a different case: an indirect disposal of a property-rich company can fall inside the UK charge and does require a 60-day return, so check residence before assuming the deadline.</p>

<h2>Why Business Asset Disposal Relief rarely helps here</h2>
<p>Sellers often arrive expecting an 18 per cent BADR rate on their shares. For a buy-to-let SPV, plan on not getting it. BADR requires a disposal of shares in a trading company, and letting property is an investment activity rather than a trade. HMRC's position on passive letting has never been in doubt, and the abolition of the furnished holiday lettings regime in April 2025 removed the one statutory route that treated some letting businesses as trading.</p>
<p>Where the company genuinely trades, a development business, or serviced accommodation with services extensive enough to amount to a trade on first principles, BADR can be in point at 18 per cent from 6 April 2026 (up from 14 per cent for 2025/26 and 10 per cent before that). That is a fact-specific test with no bright line, and it is decided by what the company actually did over the qualifying period, not by how the sale is structured. If there is a serious BADR argument, test it with advice before you market the company, not after you have signed heads of terms.</p>

<h2>The buyer's problem: inherited latent gains</h2>
<p>Because the company makes no disposal on a share sale, it keeps the base cost it has always had. If the company bought for £250,000 and the shares change hands when the property is worth £400,000, the buyer now owns a company holding a £400,000 property with a £250,000 base cost. The moment they sell it, corporation tax falls due on the whole £150,000 gain, including all the growth that happened on your watch and that you have already been paid for in the share price.</p>
<p>That is the latent gain, and buyers price it in by discounting their offer. How much of it they extract is a pure negotiation, influenced by how long they intend to hold, whether they ever intend to sell rather than refinance, and how competitive the process is. Extracting surplus cash from the company before completion is the main lever sellers have for shrinking the gap, and it has its own timing and tax traps, which our <a href="/blog/incorporation-and-company-structures/pre-sale-extraction-strip-cash-before-spv-share-sale-vs-buyer-discount">pre-sale extraction guide</a> covers in full.</p>

<h2>Stamp duty on shares: 0.5 per cent against full SDLT</h2>
<p>This is the part that funds the negotiation. A buyer of shares pays 0.5 per cent of the consideration, charged as Stamp Duty Reserve Tax on an electronic transfer or as Stamp Duty on a paper stock transfer form, per <a href="https://www.gov.uk/tax-buy-shares" rel="nofollow">gov.uk guidance on tax when you buy shares</a>. On a paper stock transfer form the charge only bites where the consideration exceeds £1,000, which will never be in doubt on a real SPV sale. The 1.5 per cent rate exists but applies only to transfers into clearance services or depositary receipt schemes, so it has nothing to do with a private share sale.</p>
<p>Compare that with what the same buyer pays to acquire the property directly. A company buying residential property pays the standard <a href="https://www.gov.uk/stamp-duty-land-tax" rel="nofollow">SDLT bands</a> plus the 5 per cent additional-dwellings surcharge on the whole price, and above £500,000 a non-natural person faces the 17 per cent flat rate unless a relief such as property rental business relief applies. On a £400,000 house the SDLT is roughly £30,000. On a share purchase at £231,250 the stamp duty is about £1,156.</p>

A seller who concedes the whole £37,500 of latent corporation tax has given away more than the buyer gained, and has paid the buyer to take a tax charge they may never crystallise.</p>

<h2>Asset sale: corporation tax, then a second layer to get the cash out</h2>
<p>On an asset sale, the company sells the property and pays <a href="https://www.gov.uk/corporation-tax-rates" rel="nofollow">corporation tax</a> on its own gain: 19 per cent where profits are below £50,000, 25 per cent above £250,000, with marginal relief between the two. On a £150,000 gain in a company with no other profits, the main rate applies to most of it. The company is then a cash box, and the cash still belongs to the company rather than to you.</p>
<p>Getting it out is the second layer. A dividend is taxed at 2026/27 rates of 10.75, 35.75 and 39.35 per cent above the £500 dividend allowance, which for most sellers of an appreciated portfolio means the additional rate on the bulk of it. Capital treatment is available on winding up, but only within limits. Under <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2010/4/section/1030A" rel="nofollow">CTA 2010 s.1030A</a>, distributions made in anticipation of dissolution are capital only where the total does not exceed £25,000, and <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2010/4/section/1030B" rel="nofollow">s.1030B</a> makes this all or nothing: exceed the cap and the whole amount becomes an income distribution, not just the excess. Above £25,000 of reserves, capital treatment needs a formal members' voluntary liquidation with a licensed insolvency practitioner.</p>
<p>The comparison between capital and income treatment on that final distribution, and how BADR interacts with it, is set out on our <a href="/blog/incorporation-and-company-structures/mvl-members-voluntary-liquidation-property-company-cgt-vs-income-treatment">MVL capital versus income guide</a>. The route choice itself, dormancy against strike-off against liquidation, is covered in <a href="/blog/incorporation-and-company-structures/how-to-close-a-property-limited-company">how to close a property limited company</a>. Both are the natural next step if your asset sale ends with an empty company you no longer want.</p>

<h2>An honest reality check on the substantial shareholding exemption</h2>
<p>SSE can exempt a company's gain on selling shares in a trading subsidiary, and it is genuinely valuable in the right structure. It is not available on a normal SPV sale, for two reasons: it exempts a gain made by a company, so an individual selling their own shares can never use it, and it requires the company being sold to be a trading company, which a buy-to-let SPV that simply owns and lets property is not. The narrow cases where it does apply, such as a corporate group selling a genuine development subsidiary, and the full eligibility test including the 10 per cent shareholding and 12-month holding requirements, are set out in our <a href="/blog/incorporation-and-company-structures/substantial-shareholding-exemption-property-companies">substantial shareholding exemption for property companies</a> guide.</p>

<h2>Why buyers prefer assets and sellers prefer shares</h2>
<p>The tension is structural, not a matter of negotiating style. On an asset purchase the buyer gets a base cost equal to what they paid, so future growth is taxed from today's value rather than from your 2021 purchase price. They also buy the property and nothing else: no historical corporation tax position, no VAT history, no old leases or deposit-protection failures, no directors' past decisions. On a share purchase they get a discount and a stamp duty saving, but they are buying the company's entire past along with the roof.</p>
<p>That is why the price gap between the two routes is never just the latent tax. It reflects the base-cost step-up, the risk transfer, the warranty and indemnity package, and the higher legal cost of a share deal. It also explains a common outcome: an institutional or first-time buyer will simply refuse shares, and the choice is then between an asset sale at full value and no sale at all.</p>

<h2>Worked example: a single-property SPV worth £400,000</h2>
<p>The company bought a house for £250,000 five years ago, funded by £100,000 of subscribed share capital and a £150,000 interest-only mortgage. The property is now worth £400,000. There are no other assets or liabilities, and the shareholder is an additional-rate taxpayer with the annual exempt amount available.</p>
<p><strong>Route A: share sale.</strong> The company's net asset value at market value is £400,000 less the £150,000 mortgage, so £250,000. The latent gain is £150,000, carrying £37,500 of corporation tax at the 25 per cent main rate. The buyer asks for that £37,500, the seller points at the buyer's £28,844 SDLT saving, and they settle at half, an £18,750 discount. The shares change hands at £231,250.</p>

<p>Two figures move the answer. If the buyer will only pay a heavily discounted share price, say the full £37,500, the shares change hands at £212,500, the tax is £26,280 and the net falls to £186,220, barely £1,100 ahead of the asset route. If your share base cost is low, because the company was capitalised with £100 of shares and a director's loan rather than £100,000 of share capital, the share-sale gain and the tax on it are both much larger, and the loan repayment comes out of the price tax-free instead. Model your own base cost before assuming either column.</p>

<h2>Practical steps and where the time goes</h2>
<p>A share sale is a corporate transaction, not a conveyance. Expect a share purchase agreement with warranties and disclosure, tax indemnities or a retention against unknown liabilities, buyer due diligence across the company's accounts, tax returns, leases and licensing, stock transfer forms and Companies House filings, and lender consent under the change-of-control clause in the mortgage, together with the release and replacement of any directors' personal guarantees. Two to four months is a realistic run, and the lender's consent is the item most likely to derail it. Both sides need their own solicitor and the legal cost is materially above a property sale.</p>
<p>If the exercise leaves you concluding that there is no buyer for the shares and no property you want to keep, the question becomes a closing question rather than a sale question. Our guide to <a href="/blog/incorporation-and-company-structures/how-to-close-a-property-limited-company">closing a property limited company</a> sets out the routes, and the wider structuring context sits on our <a href="/spv-company">property SPV hub</a>.</p>
