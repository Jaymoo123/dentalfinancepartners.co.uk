<p>A property chain is only as strong as its weakest link. When a buyer several steps below you pulls out or a sale slips its completion date, the ripple runs up the chain and can sink a purchase you were days from finishing. For an investor, that can mean losing a deposit, forfeiting an agreed deal or missing the asset entirely. A chain-break bridging loan exists to hold the deal together: it releases the value locked in the property you are selling so the onward purchase completes now, and it is repaid when the delayed sale finally goes through.</p>

<p>This guide is written for property investors, developers and business owners, and it covers the <strong>investment chain-break only</strong>. There is a hard line running through this topic that decides whether the product is even lawful for us to explain the way we do, and we draw it before anything else.</p>

<h2>The line that comes first: own-home chains are regulated</h2>

<p>If the chain you are trying to break involves the home you live in, or a home you intend to live in, a bridge secured on that property is a <strong>regulated mortgage contract</strong>. Under Article 61 of the <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2001/544/article/61" rel="nofollow">Financial Services and Markets Act 2000 (Regulated Activities) Order 2001</a>, a loan secured by a first legal charge on a property at least 40 percent of which is used as a dwelling by the borrower or a close relative is regulated. The <a href="https://www.handbook.fca.org.uk/handbook/PERG/4/" rel="nofollow">FCA's PERG 4 guidance</a> sets out that boundary in detail.</p>

<p>A regulated bridging loan can only be arranged, advised on and promoted by a firm the Financial Conduct Authority has authorised for that activity, and it comes wrapped in the consumer protections of the Mortgage Conduct of Business rules. This page does not cover regulated own-home bridging, does not advise on it and does not invite anyone to enter into it. <strong>If your chain break involves your main residence, this product is outside the scope of this guide and you should speak to an FCA-authorised mortgage adviser.</strong> You can check any firm's permissions on the <a href="https://register.fca.org.uk/" rel="nofollow">FCA's Financial Services Register</a> before you deal with them.</p>

<p>The same caution applies to a buy-to-let that is itself a regulated consumer product, for example a property let to a close family member or the accidental let of a former home. Those can fall inside the consumer perimeter and are not covered here. Everything below concerns a genuine <strong>investment or business chain</strong>, which sits outside the regulated-mortgage and consumer-credit perimeters because its purpose is a property business, not a place to live.</p>

<h2>How a chain break happens to investors, not just homeowners</h2>

<p>It is easy to think of chains as a residential problem, but investors sit in them constantly. Consider the common patterns:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Portfolio churn.</strong> You are selling one or two investment units to fund the deposit or completion on a larger acquisition. The proceeds are earmarked, so if a sale slips, the purchase has no funding.</li>
<li><strong>A 1031-style rotation.</strong> You have agreed to buy an onward asset conditional on releasing capital from an existing one, and the timing has to line up to the day.</li>
<li><strong>A developer selling completed units.</strong> Sales of finished stock are funding the land purchase or deposit on the next site, and one buyer's delay stalls the whole reinvestment.</li>
<li><strong>Auction pressure above the chain.</strong> You have won an onward lot with a fixed completion date, and the sale meant to fund it has not exchanged.</li>
</ul>

<p>In each case the problem is timing, not solvency. The value exists; it is simply trapped in a property that has not yet turned into cash. A term mortgage cannot solve this, because it takes many weeks to arrange and the completion you are trying to save is often days away. That mismatch, cash locked in a slow sale against a fast completion deadline, is the exact gap a chain-break bridge is designed to close.</p>

<h2>Where the security sits</h2>

<p>A chain-break bridge does not have to be secured against the property that is stuck. There are two common structures:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>First charge on the delayed-sale property.</strong> You bridge against the asset you are selling. When the sale completes, the proceeds redeem the bridge directly. This is clean where that property is unencumbered or lightly mortgaged, but it can complicate the sale to your own buyer if their lender queries the charge.</li>
<li><strong>Charge on a different portfolio asset.</strong> Many investors instead raise a first charge on an unencumbered investment property elsewhere, or a second charge behind a low-balance mortgage, and leave the property being sold entirely clean. The funds complete the onward purchase; the eventual sale still provides the cash to repay.</li>
</ul>

<p>The choice is practical. Securing against a separate asset keeps the stalled sale simple for its own buyer and can be quicker to execute, but it needs sufficient equity in that second property. Either way, the lender is underwriting one thing above all others, the exit, and it wants to see enough equity and a credible route to repayment.</p>

<h2>What it costs and how much you can borrow</h2>

<p>Chain-break bridging is priced like any other short-term first-charge investment bridge. Rates and fees move with the base rate and with lender appetite, so the figures below are indicative ranges <strong>as at July 2026</strong> and you should verify current pricing with a lender or an FCA-authorised broker before relying on any number.</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Monthly interest:</strong> broadly 0.55 percent to 1 percent or more per month on a first-charge investment bridge, driven by the loan-to-value, the strength and certainty of the exit, the security quality and your experience. Second charges price higher.</li>
<li><strong>Loan-to-value:</strong> typically up to around 70 to 75 percent of value on a first charge, with more available where you offer additional security across the portfolio.</li>
<li><strong>Fees on top:</strong> an arrangement fee of roughly 1 to 2 percent, plus valuation, legal costs (yours and often the lender's) and sometimes an exit fee. These come off the top, so the cash you receive is less than the headline loan.</li>
</ul>

<p>Because interest is quoted monthly and charged only for the weeks you hold the loan, a chain-break bridge that runs for six weeks costs a fraction of one held for six months. The single biggest lever on your total cost is therefore how quickly the delayed sale completes. <h2>The exit is everything: open versus closed</h2>

<p>Lenders underwrite the exit before they underwrite the borrower, and in a chain break the exit is the sale that has been delayed. Its certainty determines both whether you get the loan and what it costs.</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Closed bridge.</strong> You already have an exchanged contract on the property being sold, so there is a legally committed buyer and a known completion date. The lender knows exactly when and how it is repaid, so a closed bridge is the cheaper, easier facility. If your chain break is caused by a slow but exchanged sale, this is your position.</li>
<li><strong>Open bridge.</strong> The sale is agreed but not exchanged, or the property is only being marketed. There is an intention to repay but no fixed date, so the lender carries more risk and prices for it. Many chain breaks start here, because the whole problem is that the sale has not firmed up.</li>
</ul>

<p>A prudent investor holds a <strong>fallback exit</strong> as well. If the delayed sale collapses entirely, the ability to refinance the security onto a term buy-to-let or commercial mortgage, or to sell a different asset, is what stops a timing problem from becoming an equity problem. If part of your fallback is a refinance, it is worth understanding when a remortgage genuinely stacks up: our guide on <a href="/blog/section-24-and-tax-relief/buy-to-let-refinancing-when-does-it-make-sense">buy-to-let refinancing and when it makes sense</a> covers the after-tax maths.</p>

<h2>Worked example: a portfolio sale that slipped</h2>

<p>An investor, holding through a limited company, has agreed to buy an onward investment property for £460,000 and has a completion date fixed for early September. The deposit and completion funds were to come from the sale of a let flat in the portfolio at £240,000, on which the company owes £55,000. Contracts on the flat were due to exchange in August, but the buyer's own purchaser withdrew, pushing exchange back by several weeks. The onward completion cannot wait.</p>

<p>Rather than lose the £460,000 deal, the investor raises a first-charge chain-break bridge. The lender advances 75 percent of the flat's £240,000 value, £180,000, and after redeeming the existing £55,000 mortgage, arrangement fee of around 1.5 percent and legal and valuation costs, the net funds released come to roughly £119,000. Combined with cash reserves, that is enough to complete the onward purchase on time.</p>

<p>Interest at, say, 0.85 percent per month is rolled up rather than serviced, so nothing is paid monthly. When the flat sale finally exchanges and completes seven weeks later at £240,000, the proceeds clear the £180,000 bridge, the rolled interest and the exit fee in full. The chain held, the onward asset was secured, and the total finance cost was a defined, short-term expense against keeping a £460,000 acquisition alive. Every figure here is illustrative and specific to this scenario; your own pricing, LTV and fees will differ and should be confirmed with a lender or broker.</p>

<h2>The risks worth naming</h2>

<p>A chain-break bridge is a genuine tool, but it is not free of danger, and honesty about the downside is part of using it well:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>The exit fails.</strong> If the delayed sale collapses outright, your repayment route vanishes. You then rely on the fallback, and until it lands, rolled interest keeps compounding.</li>
<li><strong>Rolled interest compounds.</strong> Not servicing the loan is convenient, but the balance grows each month. A short hold is cheap; a bridge that overruns because a re-marketed sale drags can become expensive quickly.</li>
<li><strong>Default rates are steep.</strong> If you pass the agreed term without repaying, many facilities switch to a materially higher default rate. Read the small print on term, extensions and default before you draw.</li>
<li><strong>Personal guarantees.</strong> Where a company or SPV borrows, directors are commonly asked for a personal guarantee, so liability can reach beyond the security property.</li>
</ul>

<p>None of these is a reason to avoid a chain-break bridge; they are reasons to size the loan conservatively, keep a fallback exit and treat the exit strategy, not the monthly rate, as the part that deserves the most scrutiny. For the wider mechanics of how bridging works, open versus closed, first versus second charge, rolled versus serviced interest, see the <a href="/blog/property-finance/bridging-loans-guide">complete bridging loans guide</a>. <h2>The tax angle in brief</h2>

<p>For an investment purpose, the interest on a chain-break bridge is generally an allowable finance cost, but how the relief reaches you depends on structure and property type, and this is where the numbers can shift meaningfully.</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Individual landlord, residential property:</strong> finance costs are not a deduction. They give a basic-rate tax reducer under Section 24, set at 20 percent for 2026/27 and rising to 22 percent from 6 April 2027 in England, Wales and Northern Ireland.</li>
<li><strong>Individual, commercial property:</strong> Section 24 does not apply, so interest is deducted in full against rental profit.</li>
<li><strong>Company or SPV:</strong> finance costs are deducted in full under the loan-relationship rules before corporation tax, with no Section 24 restriction.</li>
</ul>

<p>Rolled and retained interest, and the arrangement and incidental costs of the finance, have their own timing and deductibility rules. Because the same £1 of bridging interest can be worth very different amounts of relief depending on how you hold the property, this is worth getting right rather than assuming. Our dedicated guide, <a href="/blog/property-finance/is-bridging-loan-interest-tax-deductible">is bridging loan interest tax deductible</a>, sets out each case, and the underlying restriction is explained in our <a href="/blog/section-24-and-tax-relief/finance-costs-section-24-complete-guide">Section 24 finance costs guide</a>. HMRC's own position on the residential finance-cost restriction sits in the <a href="https://www.gov.uk/guidance/changes-to-tax-relief-for-residential-landlords-how-its-worked-out-including-worked-examples" rel="nofollow">Property Income Manual and gov.uk guidance</a>.</p>

<h2>A note on how this product is promoted</h2>

<p>Everything above is educational. Business-purpose bridging is not a regulated activity, but its promotion is still restricted: under <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2000/8/section/21" rel="nofollow">section 21 of the Financial Services and Markets Act 2000</a>, an invitation to enter into a credit agreement must be made or approved by an FCA-authorised person. That is why this guide explains how the product works and stops there. It does not compare live rates, invite an application or arrange finance. Market standards for the sector are maintained by the <a href="https://bdla.co.uk/" rel="nofollow">Bridging and Development Lenders Association</a>, and the boundary between promotion and information is set out in <a href="https://www.handbook.fca.org.uk/handbook/PERG/8/" rel="nofollow">FCA PERG 8</a>.</p>
