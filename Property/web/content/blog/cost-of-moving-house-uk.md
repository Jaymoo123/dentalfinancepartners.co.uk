<p>Moving house in the UK costs you about <strong>£13,275</strong> if you are selling and buying at the England average price of £293,262. That total covers both ends of your chain. On the way out you pay the agent, your solicitor and the certificate you need before you can market, and on the way in you pay stamp duty, a survey and the fee to register your new home. It is the number to budget against, and the table below shows you every line in it.</p>

<p>Then this page does the part no other moving-cost guide does. Your moving bill is not one number, it is three. Some of what you spend comes off a tax bill now. Some of it is worth nothing today and a lot in twenty years, and the rest is simply gone. Working out which line sits in which bucket takes you two minutes, and it is worth more than haggling £50 off a removals quote.</p>

<h2>What does it cost to move house in the UK?</h2>

<p>Here is your whole bill at a glance, assuming a high street agent, a mortgage, and a sale and a purchase at the England average.</p>

<li><strong>Total, selling and buying:</strong> about £13,275 on a £293,262 home.</li>
<li><strong>Estate agent:</strong> £4,164 at the 1.42% average fee including VAT, and it is negotiable.</li>
<li><strong>Stamp duty:</strong> £4,663 on the purchase, or nothing if you are a first-time buyer at this price.</li>
<li><strong>Solicitors, both ends:</strong> about £1,750, and more if either property is leasehold.</li>
<li><strong>Survey:</strong> about £650 for a full building survey, and the best value line on your list.</li>
<li><strong>Land Registry fee:</strong> £150, fixed by law, and the only line nobody can negotiate.</li>
<li><strong>Removals:</strong> about £550, driven by distance, volume and how much packing you hand over.</li>
</ul>

<h2>Which moving costs cut your tax bill, and which are just spent?</h2>

<p>Every moving-cost guide lists stamp duty as a cost and stops there, which is the wrong place to stop. Your £13,275 does three different things to your tax position, and which one depends on the line you are looking at.</p>

<p>On the worked bill above, that is <strong>£4,944</strong> in your first bucket, <strong>£6,513</strong> in the second and <strong>£1,818</strong> in the third. Three points follow, and they are the ones people get wrong.</p>

<p><strong>The deductible list is closed.</strong> The rules name the costs you can set against a gain. They are fees to a surveyor, valuer, auctioneer, accountant, agent or legal adviser, and the costs of transfer including stamp duty. They are advertising, and any valuation you need to work the gain out. If your cost is not on that list it does not count, however unavoidable it felt. Removals is the classic: it feels like a cost of selling and it gets you no relief at all.</p>

<p><strong>Which way your advertising points matters.</strong> Money you spend advertising the home you are selling is a selling cost, so it comes off your gain now. Money you spend advertising for a property to buy attaches to the new home instead, and sits there until you sell that one. Same activity, opposite ends of your chain, opposite tax treatment.</p>

<p><strong>If you cannot reclaim VAT, use the VAT-inclusive figure.</strong> You are not VAT registered on your own home, so the number that comes off your gain is the agent's fee including VAT, not the fee before it. Every figure on this page is stated including VAT for that reason.</p>

<h2>What does it cost to sell: the agent, the solicitor and the EPC</h2>

<p>The agent is your largest selling cost and your only negotiable one of any size. The average fee in 2026 is 1.42% including VAT on the HomeOwners Alliance figures, with sole agency deals running from 1.2% to 1.8% including VAT. On your £293,262 sale, 1.42% is £4,164 and 1.2% is £3,519, so the negotiation is worth £645 in one conversation. No law caps what an agent may charge in England and Wales, which is exactly why the range is so wide.</p>

<p>Aim for 1.2% including VAT on a sole agency deal, and get three quotes before you sign. If one agent came in cheaper, say so to the one you actually want. Push on the tie-in and the notice period, not only the rate. A low fee locked into a twenty week tie-in can cost you more than a higher fee you can walk away from. Our guides on <a href="/blog/capital-gains-tax/how-much-do-estate-agents-charge-to-sell-a-house">what estate agents charge to sell a house</a> and <a href="/blog/capital-gains-tax/cheapest-estate-agent-fees-uk">the cheapest estate agent fees in the UK</a> take the rate apart. So do our pages on <a href="/blog/capital-gains-tax/online-estate-agents-uk">online estate agents</a> and <a href="/blog/capital-gains-tax/estate-agent-contract-tie-in-periods">tie-in periods</a>. London runs on its own numbers, in <a href="/blog/capital-gains-tax/average-london-estate-agent-fees">average London estate agent fees</a>.</p>

<p>Before you sign, the agent has to tell you what the fee is or how it will be worked out. They also have to tell you when you have to pay it, and what else you might be charged for. If they did not, the agreement is much harder for them to enforce. Watch for the words sole selling rights. That is not the same as sole agency, and it can leave you paying commission on a buyer you found yourself.</p>

<p>Your solicitor on the sale costs about £700, and leasehold adds to it. The freeholder or managing agent charges for the information pack and for answering enquiries, and your solicitor cannot control that.</p>

<p>The Energy Performance Certificate is the cheapest line on your bill and the only one that is a legal condition of marketing. You must have a valid EPC available free of charge at the earliest opportunity. At the latest you need it by the time someone who asks for details in writing gets them, or views the house, whichever comes first. Your buyer must be given one free of charge too. A domestic EPC costs £35 to £120 for most homes, and you can see the detail in <a href="/blog/landlord-tax-essentials/epc-certificate-cost-uk">what an EPC certificate costs</a> and <a href="/blog/landlord-tax-essentials/energy-performance-certificates-epc">how EPCs work</a>. If you already hold one that has not expired, you do not need another.</p>

<p>For the selling side in full, see our <a href="/cost-of-selling-a-property">cost of selling a property</a> guide.</p>

<h2>What does it cost to buy: stamp duty and the Land Registry fee</h2>

<p>Stamp duty is the biggest single number on your buying side. At £293,262, replacing your main home, it comes to <strong>£4,663</strong>. Work out your own with our <a href="/calculators/stamp-duty-calculator">stamp duty calculator</a>. The figure moves in steps, so a few thousand pounds on the price can shift it more than you expect. If you are buying an additional property rather than replacing your main home, a surcharge applies on top. Our guide to <a href="/blog/landlord-tax-essentials/sdlt-buy-to-let-rates-surcharge-guide-2025">buy-to-let stamp duty and the surcharge</a> covers that case.</p>

<p>The Land Registry fee is the only line on your whole moving bill fixed by law rather than by a market. A fee order that took effect on 9 December 2024 sets it, and every firm charges you the same amount because they have to. The fee goes by the price you pay.</p>

<table>
<tr><th>Price you pay</th><th>Fee, whole title lodged online</th><th>Fee by post, or for part of a title or a new lease</th></tr>
<tr><td>£0 to £80,000</td><td>£20</td><td>£45</td></tr>
<tr><td>£80,001 to £100,000</td><td>£40</td><td>£95</td></tr>
<tr><td>£100,001 to £200,000</td><td>£100</td><td>£230</td></tr>
<tr><td>£200,001 to £500,000</td><td>£150</td><td>£330</td></tr>
<tr><td>£500,001 to £1,000,000</td><td>£295</td><td>£655</td></tr>
<tr><td>£1,000,001 and over</td><td>£500</td><td>£1,105</td></tr>
</table>

<p>Two details that other sites get wrong. The reduced fee is not a discount for filing online. It is a discount for lodging a transfer or surrender of a <strong>whole</strong> registered title through the portal or gateway. Buy part of a title, such as a plot split off a larger one, and you pay the full fee. So does a new lease, even lodged electronically. On an ordinary house purchase you get the reduced column, which is why the mistake is so easy to publish and so rarely spotted. Land that has never been registered goes on a separate reduced scale for first registration.</p>

<p>Two smaller rules are worth knowing: the price is rounded down to the nearest whole pound before the band is read, and the fee is charged on the VAT-inclusive amount. The figures above are the Land Registration Fee Order 2024 as restated by <a href="https://www.gov.uk/guidance/hm-land-registry-registration-services-fees" rel="nofollow noopener" target="_blank">HM Land Registry's own fees guidance</a>.</p>

<h2>Do you need a survey, and what do the searches cost?</h2>

<p>Nothing forces you to buy a survey on a normal purchase, and it is still the best value line on your bill. A full building survey costs about £650, and shorter reports cost less and tell you less. Your lender's £150 valuation is not a survey and it is not for you. It tells the lender the house is worth lending against, and it will not find you a failing roof.</p>

<p>Your solicitor on the purchase costs about £1,050, and that side carries more work. Searches, your lender's own requirements, the title checks and, on leasehold, a management pack nobody can control the price of. Ask for your quote split into the firm's own fee and the payments it makes for you. The second half is where a cheap-looking quote quietly catches up with an expensive one.</p>

<p>Both of those, and the survey, sit in your second bucket. They are worth nothing to you this year, but they come off your gain on the day you sell that home, so keep the invoices.</p>

<h2>How much do removals cost, and what drives the price?</h2>

<p>The one published estimate we can point you to is £550, from the HomeOwners Alliance 2026 figures. Treat it as a starting point, not an average. We could not find a second published source with a stated method, and we are not going to invent a range and dress it up as one.</p>

<p>What actually moves your quote is distance, how much you own, access at both ends, and whether you pay the firm to pack. Stairs, a long carry from the van, a piano and a completion date in the last week of the month all push it up. Get three quotes from a video walk-through or a home visit rather than a web form, because a firm that has not seen your loft is guessing. Book early for a Friday, which is the day most chains complete.</p>

<p>None of it comes off any tax bill of yours. Removals, storage between two dates, the final clean and the new carpets all sit in the third bucket, and there is no version of the rules where they do not.</p>

<h2>What changes if you are a first-time buyer, or buying without selling?</h2>

<p>Buying without a sale takes your whole selling side off the bill. That is £4,944 gone, leaving about <strong>£8,331</strong>. Selling without buying works the other way and leaves you at roughly £5,534 once removals and redirection are counted in.</p>

<p>First-time buyer relief then does the heavy lifting. It gives you 0% on the first £300,000 and 5% on the slice between £300,000 and £500,000, and it disappears entirely above £500,000. At £293,262 that means no stamp duty at all, so take the £4,663 off and you are at about <strong>£3,668</strong> all in. Our guide to <a href="/blog/property-types-and-specialist-tax/first-time-buyer-relief-understanding-tax-credits-and-deductions">first-time buyer relief</a> covers who qualifies, and the trap of buying with someone who has owned before.</p>

<p>If a house builder has offered to take your old home in part exchange, your arithmetic changes again. The offer usually sits below what an agent would get you, and you pay that gap for certainty and speed. Our guide to <a href="/blog/capital-gains-tax/part-exchange-house-uk">part exchange on a house</a> puts numbers on that trade.</p>

<h2>What are the hidden costs of moving?</h2>

<p>The lines nobody budgets for, in rough order of how much damage they do to you.</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Mortgage arrangement fee, about £1,000.</strong> Adding it to your loan feels free and is not, because you then pay interest on it for the whole term.</li>
<li><strong>Early repayment charge.</strong> Leave a fixed deal before it ends and this can dwarf everything else on the page. Check your figure before you list.</li>
<li><strong>Overlap.</strong> Holding two places for a few weeks means two lots of council tax, utilities and insurance at once.</li>
<li><strong>Insurance from exchange.</strong> As the buyer you normally carry the risk from exchange, not completion, so your cover has to start early.</li>
<li><strong>Storage.</strong> Cheap by the week and expensive by the quarter, and the quarter is what chains tend to need.</li>
<li><strong>Homebuyer protection insurance, about £78.</strong> It pays your wasted costs back if the other side pulls out.</li>
<li><strong>Mail redirection, £39.50.</strong> The one everybody forgets and the cheapest thing to fix.</li>
</ul>

<p>All of these sit in your third bucket. None of them reduces your tax bill at either end. Restructuring your borrowing does not help either, because interest is excluded in terms.</p>

<h2>Do you pay Capital Gains Tax when you move house?</h2>

<p>Usually not. The relief for your main home normally covers the whole gain on a property you lived in throughout. Most people move without paying a penny of capital gains tax. It becomes live in three situations: you let the home out for part of the time you owned it, it was a second home rather than your main one, or the grounds are unusually large.</p>

<p>Where your gain is taxable, the first bucket finally earns its keep. Your £4,944 of selling costs comes straight off that gain before any tax is worked out, and that is real money rather than a rounding item. Two dates matter here and they are not the same date. The tax year your sale falls into is fixed by the day you exchange contracts. The deadline to report and pay runs from completion, and where tax is due you get 60 days. So a late March exchange with a May completion sits in the earlier tax year.</p>

<p>The mechanics live elsewhere on this site rather than here. Start with our <a href="/blog/capital-gains-tax/capital-gains-tax-property-complete-guide-uk">complete guide to capital gains tax on property</a>. Then go to <a href="/blog/capital-gains-tax/capital-gains-tax-second-home-sale">selling a second home</a> or <a href="/blog/capital-gains-tax/tax-sell-rental-property-uk">selling a rental property</a> if either describes the home you are leaving.</p>

<h2>What should you keep, and for how long?</h2>

<p>Your second bucket is the reason this section exists. Those costs do nothing for you today and everything on the day you sell, and that day may be twenty years out. Nobody remembers a 2026 survey fee in 2046, and without the invoice you cannot claim it.</p>

<p>Keep your completion statement, the stamp duty return and its receipt, the Land Registry fee, the survey invoice and both solicitors' bills. Add every improvement receipt as you go, because a new kitchen that genuinely improves the property belongs in the same pile. Scan the lot, put it somewhere that survives a laptop dying, and keep it for as long as you own the home. Our page on <a href="/blog/capital-gains-tax/cgt-record-keeping-property-sales-what-to-save-how-long">what to save and how long to keep it</a> lists the whole set.</p>

<p>Your selling side matters for less time. Once the tax year of the sale is settled and any enquiry window has closed, those papers have done their job.</p>

It gives you an itemised breakdown for your price and your situation, whether you are selling only, buying and selling, or just working out whether a move is affordable yet. It takes about 60 seconds.</p>
