  - question: "What is refurbishment bridging finance?"
    answer: "It is a short-term, interest-first loan that funds the purchase and improvement of a property that a mainstream mortgage will not accept in its current state. Because the security is unlettable or unsellable on day one, a term lender declines it, so an investor uses a bridge to buy, complete the works, then repay by refinancing or selling. The loan is secured on the property (usually a first charge) and typically runs six to eighteen months, long enough to finish the works and arrange the exit."
  - question: "What is the difference between light and heavy refurbishment?"
    answer: "Lenders draw the line at whether the works touch the structure or the planning use. Light refurbishment is cosmetic: a new kitchen and bathroom, rewiring within existing runs, flooring, decoration and a garden tidy, with no structural change and no building control notice. Heavy refurbishment moves or removes load-bearing elements, re-roofs, extends, or changes the use or number of units, and needs building control sign-off and sometimes planning permission. Heavy work carries more risk, so it usually attracts a higher rate, tighter monitoring and stage-released funds."
  - question: "How do staged drawdowns work?"
    answer: "For heavier schemes a lender rarely hands over the whole works budget up front. Instead it agrees a schedule of works with costed stages (for example strip-out, first fix, second fix, completion) and releases each tranche in arrears once that stage is inspected and signed off, often by a monitoring surveyor. This protects the lender against money being drawn and not spent, and it means you fund each stage first and are reimbursed after certification. Light refurbishments are sometimes advanced as a single retained sum released on completion instead."
  - question: "What is BRRR and how does the refinance work?"
    answer: "BRRR stands for buy, refurbish, rent, refinance. You buy a tired property with a bridge, improve it so it revalues higher, let it, then refinance onto a term buy-to-let mortgage at a percentage of the new, higher value. The refinance redeems the bridge and can release some of the cash you put in, which you recycle into the next project. It rarely returns every pound: most lenders lend against the lower of price and value for a period, and costs eat into the release."
  - question: "Does heavy refurbishment need planning or building control?"
    answer: "Often both, and the two are separate. Building control approval concerns the safety and standard of the construction (structure, fire, insulation, drainage) and is needed for most structural work, extensions and conversions. Planning permission concerns whether you may make the change at all; many extensions and loft conversions fall under permitted development and do not need a full application, but that depends on the property, any Article 4 direction and whether it is listed or in a conservation area. Confirm both with the local authority before you commit, because they affect the exit."
  - question: "Can I pull my money back out after refurbishing?"
    answer: "Sometimes, partly. If the works add enough value, a refinance at, say, 75% of the new valuation can repay the bridge and return a chunk of your deposit and works cost. But you should not assume a full return. Many term lenders apply a six-month rule and lend against the price you paid rather than the higher value until you have owned it for six months, and refinance, bridge and purchase costs all reduce the release. Model it on the lower of price and value first, then treat any day-one-value uplift as a bonus."
  - question: "Is a property flip taxed as trading?"
    answer: "Usually yes. If you buy, refurbish and sell with the intention of making a profit on the sale, HMRC generally treats that as a trade, so the profit is income (subject to income tax and Class 4 National Insurance, or corporation tax in a company) rather than a capital gain. If instead you refurbish and keep the property to let, you hold an investment asset. The distinction changes how the interest, the profit and any allowances are treated, and it is decided on the facts. Our trading-versus-investment guide covers the tests."

For an individual holding a residential rental after the works, the Section 24 restriction limits relief to a basic-rate 20% tax reducer. A company holding the let property deducts finance costs in full under the loan-relationship rules, and commercial property is not caught by Section 24.     answer: "Most run six to eighteen months. A light cosmetic refurbishment might need only three to six months of works plus time to let and refinance, so a nine-month term is common. A heavy structural scheme with building control stages, possible planning and a longer build can need twelve to fifteen months or more. Because interest is often rolled or retained rather than paid monthly, a longer term costs more, so you size the term to a realistic works-plus-exit timeline with some contingency, not the shortest plan that could slip."

    answer: "A schedule of works is a costed, staged plan of the refurbishment: what will be done, in what order and at what cost per stage. <p>Refurbishment bridging is one product name covering two very different jobs. Repainting, a new kitchen and a bathroom is not the same risk as taking out a load-bearing wall, re-roofing and adding an extension, and lenders price, monitor and fund the two accordingly. The property address barely matters. What defines your bridge is the schedule of works: whether it is cosmetic or structural, whether it needs building control or planning, and how convincing the exit is once the dust settles.</p>
<p>This guide is written for the investor or developer buying an unmortgageable property to improve and either let or sell, most often through the buy, refurbish, rent, refinance approach known as BRRR. It is educational. It explains how the product works, what drives the cost, and how the interest is taxed, and it links up to the property tax guides that own that decision. It does not arrange finance, invite an application or promote any lender, and it excludes bridging secured on your own home, which is a regulated product handled elsewhere.</p>

<h2>What refurbishment bridging finance is, and why a mortgage will not do</h2>
<p>A term mortgage lender needs security it can value and, if things go wrong, sell or let today. A property with no working kitchen or bathroom, or one mid-conversion, fails that test: it is unlettable and unsellable in its current state, so a surveyor cannot support a standard valuation and the mortgage is declined. That is the gap a refurbishment bridge fills. It is a short-term, secured, interest-first loan that funds the purchase (and usually the works) of a property precisely because it is not yet mortgageable, on the understanding that the works will make it so.</p>
<p>The logic is the exit, not the entry. A bridge is only as sound as the plan to repay it, and for refurbishment that plan is almost always one of two things: refinance the finished property onto a term buy-to-let mortgage, or sell it. The lender underwrites the works and the exit as much as the borrower. For a plain-English grounding in how any bridge works (first versus second charge, open versus closed, rolled versus serviced interest), start with our <a href="/blog/property-finance/bridging-loans-guide">bridging loans guide</a>, then come back here for the refurbishment-specific mechanics.</p>

<h2>Light versus heavy refurbishment: the line that defines your loan</h2>
<p>This is the distinction that decides everything else, and it is the one thing worth getting right before you speak to anyone. Lenders split refurbishment into two bands.</p>
<p><strong>Light (cosmetic) refurbishment</strong> does not touch the structure or the planning use. Think a new kitchen and bathroom, replastering and decoration, flooring, rewiring within existing routes, a boiler swap, tidying the garden. There is no structural alteration and no building control notice. Because the works are predictable and the property is habitable soon, the risk is lower, the rate is usually keener, and the funds are often advanced more simply.</p>
<p><strong>Heavy (structural) refurbishment</strong> changes the building. Removing or moving load-bearing walls, re-roofing, underpinning, extending, converting a house into flats or into a house in multiple occupation, or any change of use. This work needs <a href="https://www.gov.uk/building-regulations-approval" rel="nofollow noopener" target="_blank">building control approval</a> and may need planning permission, although many extensions and loft conversions fall under <a href="https://www.planningportal.co.uk/permission/common-projects" rel="nofollow noopener" target="_blank">permitted development rights</a> rather than a full application. Confirm the position with the local authority first, because whether you can make the change, and evidence that you did it lawfully, directly affects whether the exit lender will lend. If the scheme changes the number of units or the use, it is really development, and you may be reading the wrong guide: see our <a href="/blog/property-finance/bridging-finance-for-hmo-conversions">bridging for HMO conversions</a> page for the planning and licensing angle.</p>
<p>Where a project sits on this line drives the interest rate, the day-one advance, how the works money is released, whether a monitoring surveyor is appointed, and the term. Two identical-looking houses on the same street can attract quite different bridges depending on what you intend to do inside them.</p>

<h2>Stage-payment drawdowns and the schedule of works</h2>
<p>On anything beyond cosmetic, a lender rarely hands over the whole works budget at completion. Instead it agrees a <strong>schedule of works</strong>, a costed plan broken into stages, and releases each tranche in arrears once that stage is inspected. On heavier schemes a monitoring surveyor (a quantity surveyor acting for the lender) certifies each stage before the next payment is made. So the sequence is: you fund a stage, it is inspected and signed off, then you are reimbursed and the next stage releases.</p>
<p>Two consequences follow for cash flow. First, you need working capital to reach the first certification, because the money follows the work, not the other way round. Second, an over-optimistic budget or timeline surfaces early, at the schedule stage, which is the cheapest possible moment to find it. A light refurbishment is sometimes handled differently: the works sum is retained and released in one payment on completion, or advanced against a simple pre-agreed figure, because the scope is small and quick. The heavier the works, the more the funding looks like a miniature version of development finance, with stage certificates and a surveyor, which is why our <a href="/blog/property-finance/bridging-loans-guide">bridging pillar</a> treats heavy refurbishment as the bridge that behaves most like a build facility.</p>

<h2>What refurbishment bridging costs, and how much you can borrow</h2>
<p>Bridging is quoted <strong>monthly</strong>, not annually, and the monthly rate is only part of the true cost. As a guide, and as at July 2026, first-charge investment refurbishment bridges sit broadly in the region of 0.75% to 1.1% per month, with lighter, lower-risk cosmetic work at the keener end and heavier structural schemes higher, plus an arrangement fee often around 1.5% to 2%, a valuation, legal costs and frequently an exit fee. Rates and appetite move constantly with the Bank of England base rate, the lender and the specifics of the deal, so treat every figure here as an indicative range and verify current pricing with a lender or broker before you rely on it. This page does not publish a live rate table, and no single number here is a fixed quote.</p>
<p>On leverage, day-one advances commonly reach around 70% to 75% of the purchase price or value, with the works funded separately, sometimes up to 100% of build costs but released in stages as above. What moves your figures is the property, the scope of works, your track record, and above all the strength of the exit. For how the total cost of a bridge is actually built up (gross versus net loan, rolled versus retained versus serviced interest, the full fee stack), see our <a href="/blog/property-finance/bridging-loan-rates">bridging loan rates and costs</a> guide, and to sketch your own numbers use the <a href="/calculators/bridging-loan-calculator">bridging loan calculator</a>.</p>

<h2>The BRRR exit: buying back your own deposit</h2>
<p>BRRR (buy, refurbish, rent, refinance) is the strategy that makes a refurbishment bridge worth the cost. You buy tired, improve so the property revalues higher, let it, then refinance onto a term mortgage at a percentage of the new value. The refinance redeems the bridge, and if the uplift is large enough it releases some of the cash you put in, which you recycle into the next deal. That is the appeal: the same pot of money worked around several projects rather than parked in one.</p>
<p>Stay honest about how much comes back. Take the light path above: purchase £200,000, works £25,000, plus purchase costs, bridge fees and interest of, say, £12,000, so roughly £237,000 in. Refinance at 75% of the £250,000 valuation is £187,500. That repays the bridge and returns a meaningful slice of your capital, but it leaves around £49,500 in the deal, not zero. Two things temper the "no money left in" pitch. First, many term lenders apply a six-month rule and lend against the lower of price and value until you have owned the property for six months, so a day-one refinance may be capped at the £200,000 purchase price, not the higher valuation. Second, costs on both the bridge and the refinance eat into the release. Model BRRR on the lower of price and value, then treat any early-value uplift as upside. If instead you sell rather than let, the exit is a sale and the tax picture shifts from investment to trading.</p>
<p>Because the exit is a term mortgage, the refinance is where refurbishment bridging meets ordinary buy-to-let lending. For how a bridge repays from a buy-to-let mortgage, and the day-one-value versus six-month-rule point in more depth, see our <a href="/blog/property-finance/bridging-finance-for-buy-to-let">bridge-to-let</a> guide, and for whether to pull equity out at all, our note on <a href="/blog/section-24-and-tax-relief/buy-to-let-refinancing-when-does-it-make-sense">when buy-to-let refinancing makes sense</a>.</p>

<h2>Risks, and the loans this guide does not cover</h2>
<p>Refurbishment carries the standard bridging risks sharpened by build risk. Interest that is rolled up compounds while the works run, so an overrun is expensive twice over, in extra interest and in lost time on the exit. Works can cost more and take longer than the schedule (a contingency of at least 10% to 15% is prudent). And the exit can move against you: a valuation down-valuing the finished property, or a tightening in buy-to-let lending, can leave a BRRR refinance short of what you need to redeem the bridge. Personal guarantees are common, so the risk is rarely ring-fenced in the company alone.</p>
<p>One category sits outside this guide entirely. If the loan is secured on the home you live in, it is a <strong>regulated mortgage contract</strong>, not the unregulated business-purpose product described here, and the same is true of a consumer buy-to-let that is a regulated contract. The distinction turns on the regulated-mortgage boundary in the FCA's <a href="https://www.handbook.fca.org.uk/handbook/PERG/4/" rel="nofollow noopener" target="_blank">PERG 4</a> and the financial-promotion restriction in <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2000/8/section/21" rel="nofollow noopener" target="_blank">section 21 of the Financial Services and Markets Act 2000</a>. If you are refurbishing your own residence, this is a regulated product outside our scope: speak to an FCA-authorised mortgage adviser, who can assess suitability and the consumer protections that apply. This page covers investment and development property held for business purposes only, and it is information, not an invitation to borrow. For market data on rates, LTVs and completion times, the quarterly Bridging Trends report and the <a href="https://bdla.co.uk/" rel="nofollow noopener" target="_blank">Bridging and Development Lenders Association</a> are the industry references.</p>

<h2>The tax angle: flip, hold and interest relief</h2>
<p>How a refurbishment is taxed depends first on your intention, and property tax owns that decision. If you buy, refurbish and sell for profit, HMRC generally treats it as a <strong>trade</strong>, so the profit is income (income tax and Class 4 National Insurance, or corporation tax in a company), and the bridging interest is a trading finance cost, relieved against the trading profit and often capitalised into work in progress until the sale under the principles in HMRC's <a href="https://www.gov.uk/hmrc-internal-manuals/business-income-manual/bim51105" rel="nofollow noopener" target="_blank">Business Income Manual (BIM51105 onwards)</a>. If instead you refurbish and hold to let, you hold a capital investment, and the finance-cost rules apply. The test is decided on the facts, and it is easy to assume wrongly: our guides on <a href="/blog/property-types-and-specialist-tax/are-you-a-property-investor-or-developer">investor versus developer status</a> and <a href="/blog/property-types-and-specialist-tax/property-development-tax-trading-vs-investment-income">trading versus investment income</a> set out how HMRC draws the line.</p>
<p>On the interest itself, the treatment splits by structure. An individual holding a finished <em>residential</em> let is caught by the Section 24 restriction, which relieves finance costs only as a basic-rate 20% tax reducer rather than a full deduction (see HMRC's <a href="https://www.gov.uk/hmrc-internal-manuals/property-income-manual/pim2054" rel="nofollow noopener" target="_blank">Property Income Manual, PIM2054</a>). A company deducts finance costs in full under the loan-relationship rules, with no Section 24. And a trader (a flip) relieves the interest against trading profit. The arrangement fee and incidental costs of the finance are generally deductible too, subject to the same purpose test. For the full breakdown by situation, our guide on <a href="/blog/property-finance/is-bridging-loan-interest-tax-deductible">whether bridging loan interest is tax deductible</a> works through the individual, company, commercial and developer cases side by side.</p>
