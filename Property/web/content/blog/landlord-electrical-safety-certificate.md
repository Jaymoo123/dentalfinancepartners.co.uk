<p>If you let residential property in England, the law requires you to have the fixed electrical installation inspected and tested by a qualified person at least every 5 years, to hold the written report that inspection produces, to give that report to your tenants and the council within fixed deadlines, and to complete any remedial work the report requires within 28 days. Those duties come from the <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2020/312/regulation/3">Electrical Safety Standards in the Private Rented Sector (England) Regulations 2020</a>, and since 1 November 2025 the penalty for breaching any one of them is up to £40,000 per breach, raised from the original £30,000 cap.</p>

<p>This is England-only law. Scotland has required electrical inspections in private rented homes since 2015 under its own rules, and Wales and Northern Ireland run separate regimes again. The regime itself has also just moved: the 2025 amendment regulations extended it to the social rented sector and lifted the penalty cap, and government guidance was reissued on 1 November 2025, so plenty of older guides are now quoting superseded figures. Everything below is the obligation side: the duty cycle, the deadlines, what an unsatisfactory report triggers, how council enforcement escalates, and the tax treatment of the fees, the remedial work and the penalties. What the inspection costs and what drives the quote up or down is a separate question, covered in our <a href="/blog/landlord-tax-essentials/eicr-certificate-cost-landlords">EICR cost guide</a>.</p>

<h2>The certificate the law actually requires</h2>

<p>Start with the terminology, because it trips people up. There is no document called an electrical safety certificate anywhere in the 2020 Regulations. What the law requires is that a qualified person inspects and tests the fixed electrical installation and produces a written report of the results, including the date by which the next inspection is due. That report is what the trade calls an Electrical Installation Condition Report, or EICR, and the report itself is the legal deliverable. When a letting agent chases you for your electrical safety certificate, an EICR is what they mean.</p>

<p>Two neighbouring documents cause confusion. An Electrical Installation Certificate (EIC) is issued when new electrical work is carried out, for example after a rewire or a new consumer unit; it certifies the new work, and for a brand-new installation it can stand in place of a first EICR until the next inspection falls due. Portable appliance testing (PAT) covers plug-in appliances you supply, like a fridge or washing machine; it is good practice and often an HMO licence condition, but it is not what reg 3 requires. The reg 3 duty bites on the fixed installation: the wiring, sockets, switches, light fittings and consumer unit.</p>

<p>The inspection must be done by a qualified person, defined as someone competent to undertake the inspection, testing and any further work required in accordance with the electrical safety standards, which the Regulations peg to the 18th edition of the Wiring Regulations, BS 7671. In practice that means a suitably qualified and insured electrician with periodic inspection and testing experience; <a href="https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/electrical-safety-standards-in-the-private-rented-sector-guidance-for-landlords-tenants-and-local-authorities">government guidance</a> points landlords to competent-person schemes as the sensible way to find one. The Regulations do not let you self-certify unless you genuinely hold that competence.</p>

<p>One more naming quirk: the Regulations began life as the Private Rented Sector Regulations, but the Extension to the Social Rented Sector Regulations 2025 (SI 2025/1043) brought social landlords inside the same regime from 1 November 2025 and renamed the SI to the Electrical Safety Standards in the Private Rented Sector and Social Rented Sector (England) Regulations 2020, so older citations of the original title point at the same law.</p>

<h2>Which tenancies are covered, and the exclusions</h2>

<p>The Regulations apply to tenancies of residential premises in England that grant a right to occupy the premises as an only or main residence for rent. That captures standard assured shorthold tenancies, periodic tenancies, HMOs let room-by-room, and most company lets where an individual occupies. It is the tenancy that matters, not the landlord's size: a single accidental landlord with one flat is as fully in scope as a 40-property portfolio.</p>

<p><a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2020/312/schedule/1">Schedule 1</a> lists the excluded tenancies, and as the law now stands there are eight classes:</p>

<ul>
<li>Accommodation shared with the landlord or the landlord's family, where the occupier shares a toilet, washing facilities, a kitchen or a living room with them (the classic lodger arrangement).</li>
<li>Long leases, and any tenancy granting a right of occupation for a term of 7 years or more.</li>
<li>Student halls of residence.</li>
<li>Hostels and refuges.</li>
<li>Care homes.</li>
<li>Hospitals and hospices.</li>
<li>Other accommodation provided in connection with NHS healthcare.</li>
<li>Tenancies of moveable structures, vehicles or vessels, added in November 2025.</li>
</ul>

<p>The list used to have a ninth entry: tenancies where the landlord is a private registered provider of social housing. SI 2025/1043 removed that exclusion with effect from 1 November 2025, so housing associations and other registered providers now owe the same inspection, supply and remedial duties, with transitional phasing running to 1 May 2026 for some pre-existing social tenancies. The same amendment inserted new duties (regs 3B to 3D) requiring social landlords to check the safety of electrical equipment they supply, a duty private landlords do not have in the SI, though supplying safe appliances remains basic prudence and a standard HMO licence condition.</p>

<p>If you hold a leasehold flat and let it out, note the asymmetry: your tenancy to your renter is in scope, but your own long lease from the freeholder is excluded. The EICR duty is yours as the immediate landlord, and it covers the installation within the premises you let.</p>

<h2>The duty cycle: the 5-year clock and what shortens it</h2>

<p>Regulation 3 sets the interval: inspection and testing at intervals of no more than 5 years, or at shorter intervals where the most recent report specifies them. Both halves of that sentence matter. Five years is the ceiling, but the operative date is the one the inspector writes on the report as the date by which the next inspection and test is due. On a modern installation in good condition that will usually be the full 5 years. On an older installation, or one with a history of problems, the inspector can and does set 3 years or less, and that shorter date is then your legal deadline, not the 5-year maximum.</p>

<p>Three events in practice restart or interrupt the clock:</p>

<ol>
<li><strong>A new report.</strong> Each satisfactory report sets the next due date, so the cycle rolls forward from inspection to inspection, not from tenancy to tenancy. A new tenancy does not require a fresh EICR if a valid one exists; it requires you to supply the existing one before occupation.</li>
<li><strong>An unsatisfactory report.</strong> A C1, C2 or FI observation starts the 28-day remedial clock covered below, and the installation is only compliant again once a qualified person has confirmed the work in writing.</li>
<li><strong>Buying a tenanted property.</strong> The duty sits on the current landlord. If you complete on a purchase with tenants in situ and no valid EICR comes across with the property, you are the one in breach from day one, so an inspection belongs on the completion checklist alongside the deposit and the gas record.</li>
</ol>

<p>It is worth contrasting the cycle with its sibling duty. The <a href="/blog/landlord-tax-essentials/gas-safety-certificates">gas safety check</a> is annual, GB-wide, and must be done by a Gas Safe registered engineer; the electrical check is 5-yearly at most, England-only, and done by a qualified electrician. Landlords who run both off one renewal calendar rarely miss either; landlords who treat the EICR as a one-off document rather than a cycle are the ones the council letters find.</p>

<p>You must also retain each report until the next inspection is carried out, and supply a copy to the person who performs that next inspection, so the inspector can see the installation's history. Treat the report as a live business record, not a filed-and-forgotten PDF.</p>

<h2>The four supply deadlines</h2>

<p>Holding a valid report is only half the reg 3 duty. The report must move, on four separate deadlines, and each missed deadline is its own breach:</p>

<table>
<thead>
<tr><th>Who must receive the report</th><th>Deadline</th></tr>
</thead>
<tbody>
<tr><td>Each existing tenant</td><td>Within 28 days of the inspection and test</td></tr>
<tr><td>A new tenant</td><td>Before the tenant occupies the premises</td></tr>
<tr><td>A prospective tenant who requests it in writing</td><td>Within 28 days of the request</td></tr>
<tr><td>The local housing authority, on written request</td><td>Within 7 days of the request</td></tr>
</tbody>
</table>

<p>The 7-day council deadline is the one that catches landlords who technically have a report but cannot lay hands on it. A written request from the local housing authority usually means the council is already looking at the property, often after a tenant complaint, and failing to produce the report inside 7 days hands the authority a clean, easily evidenced breach before it has even considered the installation itself.</p>

<p>The before-occupation deadline for new tenants deserves equal respect. It is not within 28 days of moving in; it is before the tenant occupies at all, which means the report belongs in the pre-tenancy document pack alongside the EPC, the gas record and the How to Rent guide. Agents typically build this into referencing workflows, but self-managing landlords should serve it with the tenancy agreement and keep proof of service, since the burden of showing compliance sits with the landlord.</p>

<p>Where the report required remedial work, a second round of supply duties applies once the work is done: written confirmation of completion from a qualified person must go to each tenant and to the local housing authority within 28 days of the work finishing, with a copy of the report that required it. That closing of the loop is part of the duty, not an optional courtesy, and it is the piece most often missed by landlords who did actually fix the problem.</p>

<h2>Unsatisfactory reports: C1, C2, C3, FI and the 28-day remedial clock</h2>

<p>An EICR classifies what it finds using the BS 7671 model codes:</p>

<table>
<thead>
<tr><th>Code</th><th>Meaning</th><th>Effect on the report</th></tr>
</thead>
<tbody>
<tr><td><strong>C1</strong></td><td>Danger present, risk of injury; the inspector will normally make it safe or isolate it immediately</td><td>Unsatisfactory</td></tr>
<tr><td><strong>C2</strong></td><td>Potentially dangerous; remedial action required</td><td>Unsatisfactory</td></tr>
<tr><td><strong>FI</strong></td><td>Further investigation required without delay</td><td>Unsatisfactory</td></tr>
<tr><td><strong>C3</strong></td><td>Improvement recommended; not dangerous as it stands</td><td>Satisfactory</td></tr>
</tbody>
</table>

<p>A report carrying any C1, C2 or FI observation is unsatisfactory, and reg 3 requires you to ensure the further investigative or remedial work it calls for is completed within 28 days, or within any shorter period the report itself specifies. A C1 will often carry a much shorter period precisely because danger is present. C3 observations, by contrast, are recommendations: a report with only C3 items is satisfactory, and while acting on them is sensible, no legal clock starts.</p>

<p>Once the work is done, you must obtain written confirmation from a qualified person that the work has been carried out and that the standards are met (or that further work is required, which restarts the process for that item), and supply that confirmation with the report to every tenant and to the local housing authority within 28 days of completing the work.</p>

<p>Walk the timeline with a concrete case. Marcus owns a 1970s terrace let to a young family. His inspection on Tuesday 3 March produces a C2 for a deteriorated consumer unit and an FI for an inaccessible junction box, so the report is unsatisfactory. His deadlines now run in parallel: the family, as existing tenants, must have their copy of the report by Tuesday 31 March (28 days from inspection), and the remedial and investigative work must also be complete by 31 March unless the report set a shorter period. His electrician replaces the consumer unit and resolves the FI on 20 March and issues written confirmation on 24 March. Marcus then has until 21 April (28 days from 24 March, when the work including its confirmation was completed) to give the confirmation and report to the family and to the council. He diarises the next inspection for the date on the new certificate of confirmation, not five years from whenever he happens to remember.</p>

<h2>Enforcement: remedial notices, urgent action and the £40,000-per-breach penalty</h2>

<p>Enforcement belongs to the local housing authority, and the Regulations give it an escalating toolkit. Where the authority has reasonable grounds to believe a landlord is in breach, reg 4 requires it (not merely permits it) to serve a remedial notice specifying the work needed. The landlord must comply within 28 days under reg 5. If the landlord does not, reg 6 lets the authority arrange the remedial work itself, with the tenant's consent, and reg 8 lets it recover its costs from the landlord. Where a report identifies a danger needing urgent attention, reg 10 allows the authority to take urgent remedial action directly. Appeal routes exist against remedial action and cost recovery (regs 7 and 9).</p>

<p>Sitting alongside all of that is the financial penalty under <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2020/312/regulation/11">reg 11</a>. Where the authority is satisfied beyond reasonable doubt that a landlord has breached a duty, it may impose a penalty of up to £40,000 in respect of the breach, and it may impose more than one penalty for a continuing failure. Three features deserve emphasis:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>The cap moved recently.</strong> From 2020 to 31 October 2025 the maximum was £30,000. The <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2025/1043/made">2025 amendment regulations</a> substituted £40,000 with effect from 1 November 2025. Any guide still quoting £30,000 is describing the old law.</li>
<li><strong>It is per breach.</strong> Failing to have an inspection, failing to supply the report to the tenant, failing to produce it to the council and failing to complete remedial work are separate duties, and each breach can attract its own penalty. A landlord with none of the paperwork is not looking at one £40,000 ceiling; the exposure stacks.</li>
<li><strong>It is civil, not criminal.</strong> The authority imposes it directly under the reg 12 procedure, and the landlord's protection is the beyond-reasonable-doubt threshold plus a right of appeal to the First-tier Tribunal.</li>
</ul>

<p>Now the point that most coverage of this topic gets wrong. There are two separate £40,000 penalty regimes that can hit the same property, and they do not merge. The EICR penalty is up to £40,000 per breach under SI 2020/312 reg 11. Separately, operating a licensable property without a licence attracts a civil penalty of up to £40,000 per offence under Housing Act 2004 s.249A, a cap that rose from £30,000 on 1 May 2026. Both caps happening to sit at £40,000 makes it tempting to treat them as one figure; they are different statutes, enforced through different procedures, and a landlord can face both at once.</p>

<p>Take Priya, who bought a five-bedroom student HMO through her company and let it to five sharers without checking the local licensing position or commissioning an EICR. The property needs a mandatory HMO licence, so she is exposed to a s.249A civil penalty of up to £40,000 for the unlicensed operation. Independently, she has no EICR, has supplied nothing to the tenants, and cannot answer the council's 7-day request, giving the authority multiple reg 11 breaches at up to £40,000 each. Her theoretical ceiling is well into six figures. That is maximum exposure, not a typical award, since authorities set penalties on published matrices reflecting culpability and harm, but the stacking is real and councils use it to make examples of the worst files. Licence conditions themselves also fold electrical safety in, which is covered with the rest of the licensing mechanics in our <a href="/blog/landlord-tax-essentials/landlord-licensing-explained">landlord licensing guide</a>.</p>

<h2>Tenancy events: new lets, renewals and HMO licence conditions</h2>

<p>The duty cycle intersects with tenancy events in ways worth pinning down, because the trigger is usually a person moving, not a date passing.</p>

<p><strong>A new let.</strong> Before a new tenant occupies, they must have the current report. If the existing report is still within its validity date, supplying it is enough; nothing about a new tenancy forces a fresh inspection. If the report will expire mid-tenancy, the 5-year clock simply runs on and you inspect when it falls due.</p>

<p><strong>Renewals and the switch to periodic.</strong> A renewal with the same tenant, or a tenancy rolling onto a periodic footing, does not restart the electrical clock either, though the safest practice on any renewal is to re-serve the current report and record that you did. The move to periodic tenancies under the Renters' Rights regime changes how tenancies continue rather than what safety documents they need; our guide to the <a href="/blog/landlord-tax-essentials/renters-rights-act-periodic-tenancy-switch-landlord-obligations">periodic tenancy switch and landlord obligations</a> covers which duties re-trigger and when.</p>

<p><strong>HMOs and licensing.</strong> Licensed HMOs carry the EICR duty twice over: once under SI 2020/312 like any other tenancy, and again as a licence condition, since mandatory licence conditions require declarations about the safety of electrical installations and appliances and councils routinely demand the current EICR at application and renewal. A lapsed EICR on a licensed HMO is therefore simultaneously a reg 11 matter and a licence-condition breach, which is exactly the two-regime stack described above.</p>

<p><strong>Sale and purchase.</strong> The report belongs to the property file. Sellers of tenanted property should hand the current EICR over on completion; buyers should treat its absence as a day-one compliance gap and price the inspection, and any likely remedial work on an older installation, into the deal.</p>

<h2>The tax side and the compliance calendar</h2>

<p>Everything above is what the housing lawyer would tell you. Here is the layer your accountant adds, because every step of the duty cycle has a tax consequence.</p>

<p><strong>The inspection fee is deductible.</strong> The EICR fee is a compulsory regulatory cost of running the rental business, incurred wholly and exclusively for it, so it is a revenue expense deductible against rental income under the trading-income rules applied to property businesses by <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2005/5/section/272">ITTOIA 2005 s.272</a>. That holds for a company landlord against corporation tax in the same way. A pre-letting EICR commissioned before the first tenant ever moves in is normally allowable too, under the pre-trading expenditure rules, treated as incurred on the day the letting business starts. As a rough marker, the report itself is a low-hundreds-of-pounds purchase for most single lets; what moves the quote (circuits, property age, access, region, remedial follow-on) is worked through in our <a href="/blog/landlord-tax-essentials/eicr-certificate-cost-landlords">EICR cost guide</a>, and the annual <a href="/blog/landlord-tax-essentials/gas-safety-certificate-cost">gas safety check</a> sits in the same deductible bucket.</p>

<p><strong>Remedial work forks.</strong> Like-for-like work commanded by the report, replacing damaged sockets, repairing faulty circuits, swapping a deteriorated consumer unit for its modern equivalent, is a repair: revenue, deductible in the year, per the principles in HMRC's Property Income Manual (<a href="https://www.gov.uk/hmrc-internal-manuals/property-income-manual/pim2030">PIM2030</a>). A first-time full rewire that materially upgrades the installation, taking a property from dated part-functional wiring to a wholly renewed system, can instead be capital: no deduction against this year's rent, but an addition to the property's CGT base cost under TCGA 1992 s.38(1)(b) that reduces the gain on eventual sale. The label the electrician puts on the invoice does not decide this; the nature of the work does, and a mixed invoice is worth splitting between the repair and improvement elements before it goes in the accounts. Our <a href="/calculators/section-24-calculator">Section 24 calculator</a> shows what a deductible expense is actually worth to you after the finance-cost restriction, which is the fair comparison when you are deciding whether to phase remedial work.</p>

<p><strong>Penalties are dead money.</strong> A reg 11 penalty is not deductible in any amount, following the long-standing rule that penalties for breaching the law are not allowable (HMRC's Business Income Manual at <a href="https://www.gov.uk/hmrc-internal-manuals/business-income-manual/bim38515">BIM38515</a>). The same goes for a s.249A licensing penalty. A £5,000 penalty therefore costs a higher-rate taxpayer more than £8,000 of pre-tax rental profit to absorb, which is the sharpest financial argument for treating the deadlines seriously: the fee is deductible, the fix is usually deductible, the fine never is.</p>

<p><strong>Align the records.</strong> HMRC expects landlords to keep the records behind a self-assessment return for at least 5 years after the 31 January filing deadline, and the EICR cycle runs at up to 5 years, so one well-kept property file serves both masters: the report, the invoices for inspection and remedial work, proof of service on tenants, and the written confirmation of completed works. When the next inspector asks for the previous report (which reg 3 requires you to give them), and when HMRC asks what that consumer-unit invoice was for, the same folder answers both.</p>

<p>If your EICR date, gas record, licensing renewal and tax records currently live in four different places, it is worth an hour with a property tax specialist or your accountant to build the compliance dates into your record-keeping properly, so each certificate lands in the file already tagged as the deductible expense it is. The duty cycle is not going away, the penalty for missing it has just gone up, and the paperwork that keeps the council satisfied is the same paperwork that protects your deductions.</p>
