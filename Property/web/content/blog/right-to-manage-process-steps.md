<p>Once leaseholders decide to take over management of their building, the Right to Manage stops being a decision and becomes a procedure, and the procedure runs on statutory clocks. The Commonhold and Leasehold Reform Act 2002 fixes a minimum timetable that no amount of goodwill can compress: at least 14 days between the participation notices and the claim notice, at least one month for the landlord's counter-notice, and at least three months from the counter-notice date to the day management actually transfers. Add those floors together and the fastest lawful claim takes roughly four and a half months from first notice to takeover, and that is with nobody objecting and nothing served late.</p>

<p>This page is the timetable. It walks the claim in statutory order, attaches every deadline to the section that creates it, corrects a persistent myth about the counter-notice window, explains who pays what under the costs rules in force since 3 March 2025, and finishes with the handover step, where the service charge money changes hands and where claims most often go financially wrong. Whether RTM is worth pursuing for your building, and the qualifying tests it must pass, is covered in our <a href="/blog/property-types-and-specialist-tax/right-to-manage-explained">guide to whether the Right to Manage is right for you</a>; forming and running the company itself is covered in our <a href="/blog/property-types-and-specialist-tax/right-to-manage-company-setup">RTM company setup guide</a>. Here we assume the decision is made and the company exists, and deal purely with getting the claim from first notice to acquisition date.</p>

<h2>The claim at a glance</h2>

<p>Every RTM claim follows the same statutory sequence under Part 2 Chapter 1 of the 2002 Act:</p>

<ol>
<li><strong>Notices inviting participation</strong> go to every qualifying tenant who has not already joined the RTM company (s.78).</li>
<li><strong>A minimum 14-day wait</strong> follows before the claim can be made (s.79(2)).</li>
<li><strong>The claim notice</strong> is served on the landlord and other required recipients, with the company's membership covering at least half the flats on the day of service (s.79).</li>
<li><strong>The landlord responds</strong>, if at all, by the counter-notice date in the claim notice, either admitting the claim or disputing it (s.84).</li>
<li><strong>A disputed claim goes to the First-tier Tribunal</strong>, on an application the company must make within two months of the counter-notice.</li>
<li><strong>Management transfers on the acquisition date</strong> fixed by s.90, and the handover of contracts, insurance and service charge funds happens around it.</li>
</ol>

<p>Two things are worth fixing in mind before the detail. First, the deadlines run in one direction only: the landlord's clock is a minimum you must give, while your clocks are hard limits you must meet, and missing one generally kills the claim rather than delaying it. Second, this is a no-fault procedure. You do not plead mismanagement anywhere in these notices, and the landlord cannot defend the claim by pointing to good management; the dispute grounds are confined to qualification and procedure.</p>

<p>Stack the floors and the arithmetic is simple: 14 days, plus one month, plus three months is about four and a half months from participation notice to acquisition, before any preparation, service logistics or disputes are added. Nothing in the Act lets you go faster; everything in practice makes you slower.</p>

<h2>Steps one and two: the participation notices and the claim notice</h2>

<p>The claim opens with the <strong>notice inviting participation</strong> under s.78. It goes to every qualifying tenant in the building who is not already a member of the RTM company, and its job is to give each of them a genuine chance to join before the claim is made. The section polices that genuineness with an unusually specific rule: the notice must either include the company's articles of association or state where and when they can be inspected, and an inspection statement must offer periods of at least two hours on each of at least three days, at least one of them a Saturday or Sunday, all falling within the seven days beginning the day after the notice is given, with copies available on request. Under s.78(6), a notice that does not deliver on that statement is treated as never having been given, which in turn poisons the claim notice that follows, so this apparently minor logistical rule is a genuine claim-killer.</p>

<p>Then comes the wait. Section 79(2) forbids serving the claim notice until every person entitled to a participation notice has had one for <strong>at least 14 days</strong>. The clock runs from the last notice given, so a straggler flat noticed late restarts the count for the whole claim.</p>

<p>The <strong>claim notice</strong> under s.79 is the formal exercise of the right, served on the landlord and every other party the section requires (including any management company that is party to the leases). Three things decide whether it survives scrutiny:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>The prescribed form.</strong> The notice must follow the form set by the Right to Manage (Prescribed Particulars and Forms) (England) Regulations 2010, SI 2010/825. Those regulations were last amended in 2013 for tribunal renaming and were not changed by the 2024 reforms, so the 2010 forms remain the current prescribed forms in England. Using a home-made notice, or an out-of-date template with the wrong particulars, is among the commonest defects landlords hunt for.</li>
<li><strong>The membership threshold.</strong> On the day the notice is served, the company's members must include qualifying tenants of not less than half the flats in the building, or both tenants where there are only two (s.79(5)). The test bites at service, so members who join the day after do not count.</li>
<li><strong>The two forward dates.</strong> The notice must state a counter-notice date not earlier than one month ahead (s.80(6)) and an intended acquisition date at least three months after that (s.80(7)). Get either date wrong and the notice is defective.</li>
</ul>

<h2>The landlord's response: counter-notice, admit or dispute</h2>

<p>The landlord's only formal move is the <strong>counter-notice</strong> under s.84, and it must arrive no later than the date the claim notice specified. Here the record needs correcting, because a large share of the guidance you will read gets the window wrong: the often-quoted <strong>35-day counter-notice window is not statutory</strong>. Section 80(6) requires the claim notice to specify a date "not earlier than one month after" the notice is given. The legal minimum is one month; the operative deadline is whatever date the notice states. Claim notices commonly allow a few days beyond the month as a service margin, which is presumably where the 35-day convention originated, but a landlord who treats 35 days as an entitlement when the notice gave 31 will be out of time.</p>

<p>A counter-notice can only do one of two things: <strong>admit</strong> that the RTM company was entitled to acquire the right to manage on the date the claim notice was given, or <strong>dispute</strong> entitlement by alleging a specific reason by reference to a provision of the Chapter. Because the right is no-fault, the dispute grounds are structural: the building does not qualify, the company or its membership does not satisfy the Act, or the notices are defective. If no counter-notice is served at all, entitlement is established by default and the claim rolls forward to the acquisition date in the notice.</p>

<p>A disputing counter-notice starts the most dangerous clock in the whole procedure. The RTM company must apply to the tribunal for a determination of its entitlement <strong>not later than the end of the two months beginning with the day the counter-notice was given</strong> (s.84(3)). Since 3 March 2025 the forum in England is the First-tier Tribunal (Property Chamber) at first instance: LFRA 2024 s.52, brought into force by <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2025/131/made">SI 2025/131</a>, abolished first-instance applications to the High Court in these matters, closing off a route freeholders had occasionally used to escalate cost pressure. Which of the 2024 Act's leasehold reforms are actually in force is a moving picture; our <a href="/blog/property-types-and-specialist-tax/leasehold-reform-act-2024-what-is-in-force">LFRA 2024 in-force ledger</a> tracks it, and the RTM provisions are among the few fully commenced.</p>

<h2>Deemed withdrawal and the classic claim-killers</h2>

<p>Section 87 is where claims die quietly. A claim notice is <strong>deemed withdrawn</strong>, automatically and without anyone serving anything, in three situations:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>The tribunal application is never made.</strong> A counter-notice disputes entitlement and the two-month application window closes without an application. This is the classic failure: the committee assumes the solicitor is handling it, the solicitor assumes instructions are coming, and the claim lapses on a date nobody diarised.</li>
<li><strong>The application is withdrawn.</strong> Pulling the tribunal application, even as part of a negotiation, deems the claim notice withdrawn with it.</li>
<li><strong>The company fails.</strong> Winding up, administration, the appointment of a receiver or manager, a voluntary arrangement, or the company being struck off the register all deem the claim withdrawn. Strike-off is the insidious one, because it usually happens for missed confirmation statements rather than insolvency; keeping the company's filings current during the claim is part of running the claim, and our RTM company guide covers that discipline.</li>
</ul>

<p>The single escape hatch: no deemed withdrawal occurs if the person who gave the counter-notice agrees in writing that the company was entitled on the relevant date. A written concession from the freeholder therefore rescues a claim that would otherwise lapse, and is always worth pressing for when a dispute collapses in correspondence rather than at hearing.</p>

<p>Alongside s.87 sit the notice defects: a participation notice whose inspection arrangements fell short of s.78(5), a claim notice off the prescribed form, a membership count below half the flats at service, a counter-notice date less than a month out, an acquisition date less than three months beyond it. A deemed withdrawal or a fatally defective notice does not end the project, but it does mean starting the sequence again from the participation notices, with the weeks and legal fees that implies.</p>

<h2>Who pays what since March 2025</h2>

<p>Until March 2025, costs were the freeholder's best weapon. The original ss.88-89 of the 2002 Act made the RTM company liable for the landlord's reasonable professional costs of dealing with the claim, win or lose, which let a well-advised freeholder run up recoverable fees as a deterrent. That regime is dead law. LFRA 2024 s.50, in force from 3 March 2025 via SI 2025/131, repealed ss.88-89 and replaced them with two new sections:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong><a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2002/15/section/87A">Section 87A</a>, the general rule:</strong> an RTM company and its members are not liable for costs any other person incurs in consequence of the claim notice. Each side bears its own costs of the process, and the section also blocks the landlord from recycling claim costs into the service charge.</li>
<li><strong>Section 87B, the exception:</strong> the tribunal may order the company to pay the landlord's reasonably incurred <em>non-litigation</em> costs, but only where the claim notice has been withdrawn or has ceased to have effect (a s.87 deemed withdrawal included) <em>and</em> the company acted unreasonably in giving or not withdrawing the notice.</li>
</ul>

<p>For a claim run competently, the budget is therefore your own side only: company formation, notice preparation and service, and tribunal fees if disputed. The costs exposure that remains is concentrated exactly where the procedural traps are, because it is withdrawal plus unreasonable conduct that opens the s.87B door. A claim that lapses through a missed two-month deadline after the company pressed on against clear defects is the fact pattern that risks both losing the claim and paying for it. Any guide warning that leaseholders pay the freeholder's costs of the RTM process win or lose predates March 2025 and describes the repealed regime.</p>

<h2>The acquisition date and the handover</h2>

<p>Section 90 fixes the day management actually transfers, and the route the claim took determines which subsection applies:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Uncontested</strong> (no counter-notice, or counter-notices admitting the claim): the acquisition date is the date specified in the claim notice (s.90(2)).</li>
<li><strong>Tribunal-determined:</strong> three months after the determination becomes final, that is, after appeal rights are exhausted (s.90(4)).</li>
<li><strong>Late written agreement:</strong> where the landlord disputed but then conceded in writing, three months after the agreement (s.90(5)).</li>
<li><strong>Court-order cases under s.85:</strong> the date the order specifies (s.90(6)).</li>
</ul>

<p>From that date the management functions under the leases, repairs, maintenance, insurance, services, and the collection of service charges, are the RTM company's. The Act handles part of the transition mechanically, through contract and contractor notices that deal with existing management contracts, but the financial handover is where preparation pays and where the process guides are thinnest. Treat the acquisition date as an accounting cut-off, not an admin task, and demand before the date:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Final service charge accounts</strong> made up to the acquisition date, showing what was demanded, collected and spent under the old management.</li>
<li><strong>The uncommitted funds and reserves.</strong> Service charge money leaseholders have paid is held on trust under s.42 of the Landlord and Tenant Act 1987; it is not the landlord's money, and uncommitted balances and reserve funds should transfer to the RTM company's own trust account. Get the account details, the balances and the transfer date in writing.</li>
<li><strong>Accrued liabilities.</strong> Work done or ordered before the acquisition date but not yet invoiced will surface after you take over. A schedule of commitments and work in progress stops the first year's budget being eaten by the old management's tail.</li>
<li><strong>The contracts.</strong> Copies of every management, maintenance, lift, cleaning and utility contract, with notice periods, so you can decide what to keep, renegotiate or terminate through the statutory contract-notice machinery.</li>
<li><strong>Insurance from day one.</strong> Responsibility for insuring the building passes with the management functions, so the RTM company's buildings policy must be on risk on the acquisition date itself, not when the broker gets round to it.</li>
<li><strong>The arrears ledger and compliance records:</strong> who owes what, and the fire, asbestos, electrical and other statutory records the company now has to maintain.</li>
</ul>

<p>Reconciling the closing accounts against the funds actually transferred is the accountant's step in the claim, and the one most committees skip. Unexplained gaps between the trust balances shown in the final accounts and the cash received are exactly the disputes that fester for years afterwards; our guide to <a href="/blog/property-types-and-specialist-tax/service-charge-disputes-leaseholders">service charge disputes</a> covers the leaseholder-side machinery that applies when the numbers do not add up. If the amounts are material, having an accountant review the final accounts and the accruals schedule before you sign off the handover is cheap insurance.</p>

<h2>How long it really takes</h2>

<p>The statutory floor is about four and a half months; real claims take longer, because the floors only count the waiting the Act imposes, not the work around it. As sourced estimates rather than statutory figures: a well-run <strong>uncontested claim typically completes in around four to six months</strong> from first participation notice to acquisition, once you allow for preparing and serving notices across many flats, and organising the handover. What stretches uncontested claims is rarely the landlord: it is chasing participation to reach half the flats, tracing the correct recipients for the claim notice, and re-serving after a defect is spotted.</p>

<p>A contested claim runs on a different scale. Here is one walked against the calendar, with illustrative dates and each step cited:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>1 September:</strong> claim notice served, specifying <strong>2 October</strong> as the counter-notice date (one month, s.80(6)) and 5 January as the intended acquisition date (three months later, s.80(7)).</li>
<li><strong>2 October:</strong> the freeholder serves a counter-notice disputing entitlement, alleging the building fails the qualification tests.</li>
<li><strong>By 1 December:</strong> the RTM company must have applied to the First-tier Tribunal, two months beginning with the day the counter-notice was given (s.84(3)). The intended acquisition date in the notice is now academic.</li>
<li><strong>Spring:</strong> the tribunal hears the case and determines that the company was entitled. The determination becomes final once the appeal window passes.</li>
<li><strong>Three months after finality:</strong> the acquisition date (s.90(4)), landing roughly a year after the participation notices went out.</li>
</ul>

<p>That is a contested claim going well. Add tribunal listing pressure, an appeal, or a re-served notice, and contested claims comfortably exceed a year. The practical moral of the timetable is that speed is won early, not late: a claim notice on the prescribed form, served with the membership threshold met and both forward dates correctly calculated, gives a careful freeholder nothing to dispute, and the one-month and three-month clocks then run to a takeover nobody can stop.</p>

<p>The procedure rewards diary discipline more than legal firepower: three of the deadlines above are fatal if missed, and none is extendable by goodwill. If your building is at the start of this process, a solicitor who runs RTM claims will keep the notices on form and the clocks diarised, and an accountant is worth involving before the acquisition date rather than after, so the service charge handover is reconciled while the leverage still exists. For whether to make the claim at all, start with our Right to Manage overview; for what the company must do once it holds the management functions, the company setup guide picks up where this page ends.</p>
