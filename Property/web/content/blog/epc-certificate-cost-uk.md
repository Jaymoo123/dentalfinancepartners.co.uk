<p>A domestic Energy Performance Certificate costs between £35 and £120+ in 2026. Most landlords and sellers who book directly with a local accredited assessor pay between £45 and £90; online booking platforms advertise from about £35; estate agents arranging the certificate as part of a sale or letting package commonly charge £75 to £150. There is no fixed statutory fee, which is why two quotes for the same three-bedroom semi can differ by £50 and both be normal.</p>

<p>These figures, and the regulations behind them, cover England and Wales, where EPCs are governed by the Energy Performance of Buildings (England and Wales) Regulations 2012 (<a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2012/3118/contents">SI 2012/3118</a>). Scotland and Northern Ireland run separate EPC regimes with their own rules and registers, so the duties and penalty figures below do not carry across.</p>

<p>This page is about the money: what you will pay, why quotes vary, when you do not need to pay at all, and how the fee and any follow-on spend are treated for tax. For the wider picture, what an EPC actually is, how the SAP and RdSAP methodology produces the A to G band, when the law requires one and what the exemptions are, see our complete guide to <a href="/blog/landlord-tax-essentials/energy-performance-certificates-epc">Energy Performance Certificates</a>. That page owns the what and the why; this one owns the how much.</p>

Rural properties can carry a travel supplement where the assessor covers a wide patch. <h2>Check the register before you pay anything</h2>

<p>The cheapest EPC is the one you already have. Under <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2012/3118/regulation/9">regulation 9(2) of SI 2012/3118</a>, an EPC is valid if it was entered on the register no more than 10 years before the date it is made available, and no newer certificate for the building has since been lodged. Two practical consequences follow, and both save money.</p>

<p>First, a re-let does not require a new EPC. If a valid in-date certificate exists, you can market and let on it for as many successive tenancies as fall within its 10-year life. The idea that each new tenancy needs a fresh certificate is simply wrong, and it is a belief that quietly sells a lot of unnecessary £60 assessments. The certificate also travels with the building, not the owner: an EPC commissioned by the previous owner or a former letting agent counts, provided it is still the most recent one on the register.</p>

<p>Second, checking takes under a minute and costs nothing. Search the address at <a href="https://find-energy-certificate.service.gov.uk">find-energy-certificate.service.gov.uk</a>, the official register for England and Wales. You will see the current rating, the expiry date, and the recommendation list, and you can download the certificate itself. Do this before booking anything, whether you are letting, selling or remortgaging.</p>

<p>There is a third consequence, less well known, hiding in regulation 9(2)(b): a newer certificate supersedes the old one the moment it is lodged, even if the old one had years left to run. Used deliberately, this is a tool. After insulation, glazing or heating works, a voluntary re-assessment lets you replace an old D with a new C on the register, which matters for mortgage products, marketing and compliance headroom. Used carelessly, it is a trap: commission a casual re-assessment and there is no keeping the old certificate if the new result comes back worse. Re-assess after works, when you expect the number to move in your favour, not on a whim.</p>

<h2>Why quotes range from £35 to £120+</h2>

<p>Every domestic EPC is the same legal document produced by the same RdSAP methodology, so the price spread is entirely about the delivery chain. Five drivers account for nearly all of it.</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Floor area and layout.</strong> The assessor measures every room and records the construction, glazing, insulation and heating evidence for each. A one-bedroom flat is a 20 to 30 minute visit; a large extended detached house with a conversion and a conservatory can take well over an hour, and the fee follows the time.</li>
<li><strong>Location.</strong> Assessor day rates track the local cost of doing business. The same survey prices differently in central London than in a small northern town, and rural rounds carry travel time that someone has to pay for.</li>
<li><strong>Access and property quirks.</strong> Difficult loft access, multiple heating systems, non-standard construction and outbuildings all add survey time, and some assessors quote them explicitly.</li>
<li><strong>Who takes the margin.</strong> This is the biggest structural driver. Book direct and the whole fee goes to the assessor. Book through an online platform and the platform takes a slice, with the headline price kept low by driving volume through each assessor's diary. Book through an estate or letting agent and the assessor's fee has an agent referral margin added on top, which is why agent-arranged EPCs sit £30 to £80 above the direct price for identical work. Nothing stops you declining the agent's package and booking your own.</li>
<li><strong>Speed.</strong> Next-day, evening and weekend slots price above the standard rate, as with any trade. If your marketing timetable allows a few days' notice, you avoid the premium entirely.</li>
</ul>

<p>One small print point when comparing quotes: VAT. Many sole-trader assessors sit below the VAT registration threshold and their quote is the final price, while larger firms, platforms and agent packages usually add 20% at the invoice. A £55 quote plus VAT costs more than a £62 quote with none, so confirm whether the figure you are comparing is VAT-inclusive before deciding the cheaper option. For a landlord the VAT is simply part of the deductible cost, but it still moves the ranking between two quotes.</p>

<h2>The cheap-EPC trade-off, and why it bites landlords hardest</h2>

<p>A £35 online booking and a £90 local booking produce the same certificate on the same register, and the cheap one is usually produced by a properly accredited assessor. The legitimacy question is the wrong question. The right question is what the price does to the survey.</p>

<p>A heavily discounted fee only works commercially at volume, which means more surveys per day, which means less time in each property. Where the assessor cannot see or evidence something, the RdSAP conventions require conservative default assumptions: unverified wall insulation is treated as absent, an inaccessible loft is scored on age-band defaults, an undocumented boiler is rated on its assumed original efficiency. Defaults drag the score down. A careful assessor who photographs the loft insulation depth, records the boiler model number and asks you for installation paperwork will frequently land the same property several SAP points higher than a rushed visit records.</p>

<p>For a seller, a few points rarely change anything. For a landlord, the band boundary is a regulatory line. A property scored into band E on defaults may in truth be a solid D, and a property scored a marginal E is one conservative assumption away from an F, at which point the Minimum Energy Efficiency Standard prohibits the letting without a registered exemption. The economics are lopsided: saving £40 on the assessment risks a result that pushes you into remediation spend or an exemption process costing hundreds or thousands. Our <a href="/blog/landlord-tax-essentials/mees-regulations-landlords">MEES guide for landlords</a> covers that regime in full.</p>

<p>The practical rule: if your property is modern or recently upgraded and comfortably mid-band, the cheapest accredited booking is fine. If it is older stock, near a band boundary, or you have done improvement works that only paperwork can prove, pay for a local assessor with time to gather evidence, and have your installation certificates, building regulations completion certificates and warranty documents ready for the visit.</p>

<h2>Who can produce an EPC and how to check them</h2>

<p>Only an accredited energy assessor can produce and lodge an EPC. <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2012/3118/regulation/22">Regulation 22(1) of SI 2012/3118</a> requires every energy assessor to be a member of an accreditation scheme approved by the Secretary of State, and regulation 22(3) sets the conditions a scheme must meet: carrying out assessments with independence, ensuring members are qualified and fit-and-proper, standard forms, a published code of conduct, indemnity arrangements, a complaints process, and lodgement of every certificate on the central register.</p>

<p>For existing homes the qualification is Domestic Energy Assessor (DEA) status, gained through an accredited training route and maintained through the scheme's quality monitoring. Whoever sells you the booking, broker, agent or the assessor directly, the individual who walks through your property must hold this accreditation, and their name and accreditation number appear on the certificate. If you want to verify before the visit, ask for the assessor's accreditation number and scheme; after lodgement, the register entry itself is the confirmation that an accredited assessor produced it.</p>

<h2>What happens at the assessment</h2>

<p>The visit is non-invasive and takes roughly 30 to 60 minutes for most homes. The assessor measures each room, inspects the heating system and controls, checks glazing, records loft and (where evidenced) wall insulation, and photographs the evidence supporting each data point. Nothing is drilled or lifted; the survey works from what can be seen and documented, which is exactly why the paperwork you hand over matters to the result. The data goes into the RdSAP model, and the certificate is usually lodged on the register within a few days. Our step-by-step guide to <a href="/blog/landlord-tax-essentials/how-to-book-an-epc">booking an EPC</a> covers the process end to end, including how to prepare the property and paperwork so the assessment captures every point you are entitled to.</p>

<h2>Penalties for marketing or letting without an EPC</h2>

<p>Enforcement sits with local trading standards, who can issue a penalty charge notice under regulation 36 of SI 2012/3118. For a dwelling, the penalty is £200 per breach, set by <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2012/3118/regulation/38">regulation 38 of SI 2012/3118</a>. The £200 figure attaches to the core duties: making a valid EPC available free of charge to any prospective buyer or tenant at the earliest opportunity and ensuring the eventual buyer or tenant receives one (regulation 6), and having commissioned an EPC before marketing (regulation 7). Failing to provide copies to an enforcement officer on request carries a further £200 under the same regulation 38 of SI 2012/3118.</p>

<p>Keep the two penalty regimes apart, because the internet routinely blends them into a single "EPC fine" figure. SI 2012/3118 answers one question: does a valid EPC exist for this transaction? Its dwelling penalty is the £200 above. The Minimum Energy Efficiency Standard under SI 2015/962 answers a different question: is the band good enough to let? That regime carries its own, much larger penalty structure for letting a sub-standard property, with its own regulation numbering. A landlord can hold a perfectly valid EPC and still breach MEES because the band is F; equally, a band-C property marketed with no certificate at all breaches SI 2012/3118 and not MEES. The <a href="/blog/landlord-tax-essentials/mees-regulations-landlords">MEES page</a> covers the second regime; on the cost question, the exposure for simply not having the certificate is £200 per breach, plus the practical reality that solicitors and letting agents will not progress a transaction without one.</p>

<p>One deductibility point belongs here rather than below: penalties are not tax-deductible. The £200 goes on the wrong side of the ledger and stays there, while the £60 certificate that avoids it is fully deductible. Few compliance costs have a cleaner cost-benefit line.</p>

<h2>The tax treatment: landlord, seller, improver</h2>

<p>None of the price-comparison content on this subject touches tax, and yet for a landlord the after-tax cost of an EPC is meaningfully below the invoice price, and for a seller the fee has a specific home in the capital gains computation.</p>

<h3>The landlord: a deductible revenue expense</h3>

<p>For a landlord, the EPC fee is a revenue expense incurred wholly and exclusively for the property business, deductible against rental income. The trading-income deduction rules apply to property businesses through <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2005/5/section/272">ITTOIA 2005 s.272</a>, and HMRC's guidance on the wholly-and-exclusively test for landlords is at <a href="https://www.gov.uk/hmrc-internal-manuals/property-income-manual/pim2010">PIM2010</a>. A compulsory regulatory cost of letting, alongside gas safety and electrical certificates, is as clean a deduction as the property pages get. A basic-rate landlord's £80 EPC costs £64 after relief; a higher-rate landlord's costs £48.</p>

<p>Timing does not defeat the deduction either. An EPC commissioned before the first tenant, for a property being prepared for its first letting, is a pre-commencement expense: incurred up to seven years before the business starts, it is treated as incurred on the day letting begins and relieved in the first year, per HMRC guidance at <a href="https://www.gov.uk/hmrc-internal-manuals/property-income-manual/pim2505">PIM2505</a>.</p>

<h3>The seller: an incidental cost of disposal</h3>

<p>A seller's EPC is bought because the law requires one to market the property, and where the disposal is chargeable to capital gains tax (a let property, a second home, a property with lettings-period gains) the fee belongs in the CGT computation. <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1992/12/section/38">TCGA 1992 s.38(1)(c)</a> allows the incidental costs of making the disposal, and s.38(2) defines those costs to include fees for the professional services of any surveyor or valuer and the costs of advertising to find a buyer. A statutorily required certificate produced by an accredited assessor as a precondition of marketing sits within that frame, and HMRC's guidance on allowable incidental costs is at <a href="https://www.gov.uk/hmrc-internal-manuals/capital-gains-manual/cg15250">CG15250</a>. It is a small line next to agent and legal fees, but the computation is built from small lines; our <a href="/calculators/capital-gains-tax-calculator">capital gains tax calculator</a> lets you see what the full set of disposal costs does to the bill.</p>

<h3>The improver: capital versus revenue on the follow-on spend</h3>

<p>The certificate often triggers spending far larger than its own fee, and that spend splits two ways. Works that improve the property beyond its previous specification, insulation where there was none, double glazing replacing single, a new heating system of a higher standard, are capital: no deduction against rent, but they add to the CGT base cost for the eventual sale. Like-for-like replacement, a modern condensing boiler replacing a failed one on a straight swap, is a repair and deductible against rental income. Where you receive grant funding towards the works, the grant reduces the base-cost addition rather than disappearing; our guide to <a href="/blog/landlord-tax-essentials/epc-improvement-grant-schemes-landlords-eco4-bus-gbis">EPC improvement grant schemes</a> covers what is available and how receipts interact with the tax position. And before committing to a large improvement programme aimed at a future band C requirement, read our page on the <a href="/blog/landlord-tax-essentials/epc-c-2030-minimum-energy-efficiency-landlord-spending-cap">EPC C 2030 trajectory</a>: the current legal floor and the widely reported future one are not the same thing.</p>

<h3>A worked example</h3>

<p>Gwen lets a three-bedroom terrace and her tenants have given notice. Before instructing the agent, she searches the register: the property has a band D certificate lodged seven years ago, valid for three more years. She re-lets on it and spends nothing. The following spring she has loft insulation topped up to current standards at a cost of £600, and then books a direct assessment with a local assessor at £65, handing over the installer's paperwork at the visit. The new certificate lands at band C and supersedes the old D on the register, improving her remortgage options and giving her compliance headroom for years. Tax treatment: the £65 assessment fee is a revenue deduction against her rental income under the s.272 rules; the £600 insulation is an improvement, so it is added to her CGT base cost rather than deducted from rent. Contrast her brother Emyr, selling a former buy-to-let: his £70 EPC, required before the agent could list the property, enters his capital gains computation as an incidental cost of disposal under s.38(1)(c), alongside the agent's commission and his solicitor's fee.</p>

<h2>Spend where it matters</h2>

<p>The EPC is one of the cheapest documents in property, and the money decisions around it are mostly about not spending: check the register before booking, book direct rather than through an agent's package, decline the rush premium if your timetable allows, and claim the deduction the fee has earned. The one place to spend up rather than down is assessment quality when your property sits near a band boundary, because the £40 saved on a rushed survey is the most expensive saving in this market. If the certificate has triggered a bigger question, how to fund improvement works, how the capital and revenue split falls across a refurbishment, or how disposal costs shape the gain on a sale, that is the point to put the numbers in front of a property tax specialist before the spend is committed rather than after.</p>
