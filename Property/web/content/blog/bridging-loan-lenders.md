<h2>Why the lender you pick matters as much as the rate</h2>
<p>Most first-time bridging borrowers focus on the monthly rate. Experienced investors focus on the lender. The reason is simple: bridging is a speed-and-certainty product, and the biggest cost is not a slightly higher rate, it is a deal that collapses because the lender could not, or would not, complete on time. A bridge exists to hit a deadline (an auction completion, a chain, a below-market purchase, a refurbishment window). A lender that reprices, adds conditions or runs out of funds a week before completion can cost you the deal, the deposit and the opportunity.</p>
<p>The UK bridging market is not one homogeneous group. It is several distinct types of lender that price differently, underwrite differently and move at very different speeds. This guide maps those types for a property investor, developer or commercial owner, then sets out how to tell a well-run lender from a poorly-run one and how to verify authorisation before you commit a penny. It is education, not a recommendation. We name no individual lenders, endorse none and arrange no finance. For how a bridge itself works and what it costs, start with our <a href="/blog/property-finance/bridging-loans-guide">bridging loans guide</a> and our <a href="/blog/property-finance/bridging-loan-rates">bridging loan rates</a> breakdown.</p>

<h2>The UK bridging lender market, by type</h2>
<p>Broadly, six kinds of lender operate in the UK bridging space. Each occupies a different position on speed, cost, flexibility and appetite for risk.</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Specialist non-bank bridging lenders.</strong> The largest group by deal count. They exist purely to do short-term secured lending, tend to be the fastest, and have the most appetite for unusual security, a weaker exit or a first-time borrower. They usually price above the banks to reflect that flexibility and their cost of capital.</li>
<li><strong>Challenger and specialist banks.</strong> Banks with a dedicated bridging or short-term lending arm. Often cheaper because they fund from deposits, but they underwrite more strictly, move more slowly and have less appetite for the awkward cases. As deposit-takers they are regulated for prudential purposes by the Prudential Regulation Authority.</li>
<li><strong>Private lenders and family offices.</strong> High-net-worth individuals or family capital lending directly, often on larger or more bespoke deals. Highly flexible and fast on the right case, but pricing and terms are negotiated rather than off a rate card, and certainty of funds varies with the individual funder.</li>
<li><strong>Institutional and peer-to-peer platforms.</strong> Lenders funded by institutional capital or a pool of retail and professional investors through a platform. Can be competitive on cost, but the funding model means appetite and speed can shift with investor demand.</li>
<li><strong>High-street banks.</strong> Rarely offer true bridging. Where they do, it tends to be for existing customers on straightforward cases, slowly, and often only as a regulated product. Not a realistic route for most time-critical investment bridging.</li>
<li><strong>Brokers running their own funding lines.</strong> Some intermediaries also lend from a committed funding line, sitting between broker and lender. Useful for access, but ask who ultimately funds the loan and whether the line is committed.</li>
</ul>

<p>The pattern is consistent: speed and flexibility cost more, and cheaper lenders ask more of the deal. The mistake is to assume the categories are interchangeable. A low rate from a lender with no appetite for your security or your exit is not available to you, and chasing it wastes time you may not have.</p>

<h2>Regulated versus unregulated bridging lenders (the own-home line)</h2>
<p>Before comparing lenders at all, establish which side of the regulatory line your loan sits on, because it dictates who can lawfully lend to you.</p>
<p>A bridge secured on a property that you, or an immediate family member, occupy or intend to occupy is a <strong>regulated mortgage contract</strong>. It must be arranged and provided by an FCA-authorised firm operating under the FCA's mortgage conduct rules (MCOB), and it carries consumer protections. The boundary is set by the Regulated Activities Order and explained in the FCA's Perimeter Guidance at <a href="https://www.handbook.fca.org.uk/handbook/PERG/4/" rel="nofollow">PERG 4</a>. A loan secured on land is also caught where at least 40% is used as, or with, a dwelling.</p>
<p>A bridge for a genuine business or investment purpose, secured on an investment property you do not live in, is normally <strong>unregulated</strong>. Most specialist bridging is of this kind. <strong>If your bridge is secured on your own home, it is a regulated product, it is outside the scope of this guide, and you should speak to an FCA-authorised mortgage adviser.</strong> This page addresses only unregulated, business-purpose bridging for property investors, developers and commercial owners. The same distinction runs through the whole of our finance content, and it changes which lenders you can even approach.</p>

<h2>Direct lender or broker: how the market actually works</h2>
<p>There are two ways to reach a lender. You can approach a <strong>direct lender</strong>, which uses its own or its funders' capital and makes the credit decision itself, or you can use a <strong>broker</strong>, an intermediary that packages your case and places it with a lender from its panel.</p>
<p>Most investment bridging goes through a broker, for three practical reasons. First, pricing and appetite move constantly, so a single direct lender only ever shows you its own current position, while a whole-of-market broker sees across many. Second, a good broker knows which lenders will decline your security or your exit before you spend money on a valuation. Third, brokers can often access terms and lenders not marketed directly. The trade-off is a broker fee, which should always be disclosed up front and set against the value of better placement.</p>
<p>Going direct can make sense for a straightforward deal with a lender you already know and trust. For unusual security, a tight completion deadline or a first-time deal, a broker usually earns the fee. The National Association of Commercial Finance Brokers (<a href="https://www.nacfb.org/" rel="nofollow">NACFB</a>) is the trade body for the commercial-finance broker sector and a useful reference point for how the intermediary market is structured. We are not a broker and do not place finance; this is market information.</p>

<h2>How to assess a bridging lender: a due-diligence checklist</h2>
<p>Once you know the type of lender and the route, judge the individual lender on substance, not the headline rate. The questions that separate a well-run lender from a risky one:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Is it authorised, and is the entity the right one?</strong> Check the FCA register (below). For unregulated lending the firm may not need authorisation for the activity, but the identity, track record and corporate standing of the lender still matter. Check its filing history at Companies House.</li>
<li><strong>Is it a BDLA member?</strong> Membership of the Bridging and Development Lenders Association signals adherence to sector conduct standards. It is a positive signal, not a guarantee.</li>
<li><strong>How is the loan funded?</strong> A committed, well-capitalised funding line is far less likely to be pulled or repriced near completion than discretionary or retail capital. Certainty of funds is worth more than a marginal rate saving.</li>
<li><strong>What is the default interest rate, and where is it stated?</strong> A transparent lender states the default rate plainly. If it is vague, buried or punitive, treat that as a serious warning.</li>
<li><strong>What are the exit and redemption terms?</strong> Check for minimum-term interest, early-redemption charges and exit fees. A bridge you repay early should not cost you as if you ran the full term unless that is clearly agreed.</li>
<li><strong>What is the valuation approach?</strong> Ask which basis the lender uses (market value, vacant possession, 180-day) and whether it instructs its own RICS valuer. The valuation basis drives how much you can actually borrow.</li>
<li><strong>How fast, realistically?</strong> Ask for a realistic timeline to completion for your case, not a marketing headline, and what could delay it.</li>
</ul>

<h2>Red flags: what a well-run lender never does</h2>
<p>Some behaviours are consistent warning signs across the market. Any one of these justifies pausing and checking further:</p>
<ul>
<li>A large <strong>non-refundable commitment or arrangement fee demanded before a valuation is even instructed</strong>. Legitimate lenders take fair costs, but front-loaded, non-refundable fees on an unassessed deal are a classic advance-fee red flag.</li>
<li>A <strong>default interest rate that is vague, hidden or disproportionate</strong> to the headline rate.</li>
<li><strong>Unclear or punitive early-redemption terms</strong>, or minimum-term interest not made obvious.</li>
<li>No named, independent solicitor or RICS valuer, or pressure to use only the lender's own connected parties without transparency.</li>
<li><strong>Pressure to complete before you have read and understood the facility letter.</strong> Speed is the point of bridging, but never at the cost of reading the terms.</li>
<li>Any request to pay fees to a personal account, or communications that do not match the registered firm.</li>
</ul>
<p>Opacity about cost is the single most reliable red flag. A good bridging lender is upfront about the default rate, the exit fee and exactly what happens if your exit slips. If you cannot get clear answers on those three points, you have your answer about the lender.</p>

<h2>Verifying a lender: the FCA register, the trade body and the promotion rules</h2>
<p>Two quick checks protect you from most problems. First, search the lender (and any broker) on the <a href="https://register.fca.org.uk/" rel="nofollow">FCA Financial Services Register</a> and confirm the firm reference number matches the exact legal entity in your paperwork. Firms cloning a genuine authorised firm's details are a known scam, so match the name, number and contact details precisely. Second, check BDLA membership through the <a href="https://bdla.org.uk/" rel="nofollow">Bridging and Development Lenders Association</a>, which sets conduct standards for the sector, and look at market data such as the quarterly <a href="https://bridgingtrends.com/" rel="nofollow">Bridging Trends</a> report for a sense of typical rates, loan-to-value and completion times across the market.</p>
<p>One point on how we present all of this. UK financial promotion is restricted: under <a href="https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2000/8/section/21" rel="nofollow">section 21 of the Financial Services and Markets Act 2000</a>, only an FCA-authorised person (or someone whose communication they have approved) may invite or induce you to enter into a credit agreement, a point developed in the FCA's <a href="https://www.handbook.fca.org.uk/handbook/PERG/8/" rel="nofollow">PERG 8</a>. That is why this page describes lender types and how to assess them but names no lender to approach, offers no comparison-to-apply and captures no finance enquiry. It is information to help you choose well, not an invitation to borrow. To find and apply to a specific lender, use a whole-of-market FCA-authorised broker.</p>

<h2>Where the tax sits, and where we can help</h2>
<p>The lender you choose sets the cost of the bridge. How that cost is treated for tax is a separate question, and it can materially change the true cost of borrowing. In outline: a company deducts bridging interest on an investment or trading property as a loan-relationship debit; an individual landlord letting residential property has finance costs restricted to a 20% basic-rate tax credit under Section 24, rather than a full deduction; and interest on commercial property or a genuine development trade is generally deductible in full or capitalised into work in progress. Arrangement and valuation fees may also be relievable as incidental costs of finance.</p>
<p>Because the treatment turns on who borrows (individual, partnership, SPV or trading company) and why, it is worth settling before you borrow, not after. <h2>The bottom line on choosing a bridging lender</h2>
<p>There is no single best bridging lender, only the right lender for your asset, your exit and your timescale. Specialist non-bank lenders buy you speed and flexibility at a higher rate; banks buy you a lower rate at the cost of speed and appetite; private and institutional funders sit in between and vary case by case. Judge any individual lender on certainty of funding, transparency about the default rate and exit terms, and its valuation approach, not on the monthly headline. Verify authorisation on the FCA register, check for BDLA membership, and read the facility letter before you pay a fee. Then make sure the tax treatment of the interest is settled, so the number you have compared is the true cost of the money.</p>
